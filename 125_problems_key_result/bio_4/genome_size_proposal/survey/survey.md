# Survey Agent Report: Why Do Genome Sizes Diverge So Widely?

## Scientific reframing

The broad question is reframed as: **How do lineage-specific rates of repeat-DNA gain, whole-genome duplication, and DNA loss jointly generate extreme nuclear genome-size divergence, and when do genome-size-associated phenotypes impose evolutionary constraints?** Genome size is treated as the 1C nuclear DNA amount, not as gene number, organismal complexity, or a direct measure of adaptation.

The motivating examples need a current boundary. *Encephalitozoon intestinalis* is among the smallest sequenced eukaryotic nuclear genomes, whereas the formerly cited flowering-plant record of *Paris japonica* has been superseded: Fernández *et al.* report a 160.45 Gbp/1C genome in the fork fern *Tmesipteris oblanceolata* [S2]. Such extremes establish the scale of the problem; they do not show that a single mechanism explains all eukaryotes.

## Evidence map

### S1. Genome size is dominated by noncoding and repetitive DNA in many plant comparisons

Pellicer *et al.* synthesize more than 64,000-fold eukaryotic and approximately 2,400-fold land-plant genome-size diversity, while stressing remaining taxonomic and methodological gaps [S1]. In Fabeae, comparative repeat profiling found that differential repeat accumulation explained most measured genome-size differences, with one Ty3/gypsy lineage contributing a large fraction [S5]. These findings support a mechanism-level claim: repeat turnover is often important. They do **not** justify a universal repeat fraction, a universal evolutionary rate, or an effect size for every lineage.

### S2. Transposable elements create lineage-specific gain processes

Transposable elements are endogenous mutators whose host-level effects span parasitism, neutral accumulation, and co-option [S3]. In *Gossypium*, differential amplification of TE lineages, especially gypsy-like elements, tracked major genome-size differences [S4]. This demonstrates that even within related lineages, the relevant repeat family can differ. A model must therefore separate TE amount, family composition, and recent activity rather than using a single “repeat content” predictor.

### S3. Genome contraction is real and cannot be represented as the absence of expansion

The 82 Mb *Utricularia gibba* genome remains gene-complete despite repeated whole-genome duplications and unusually little intergenic DNA [S6]. In *Genlisea*, related lineages show both expansion and contraction; the small genome is consistent with retroelement silencing and deletion-biased double-strand-break repair, while a larger lineage combined whole-genome duplication and retrotransposition [S7]. The evidence supports bidirectional genome evolution. It does not establish that one repair pathway alone causes shrinkage across plants.

### S4. Whole-genome duplication changes the initial condition but not necessarily the long-run size

Polyploidy instantaneously increases DNA content, but post-polyploid reorganizations, gene loss, repeat turnover, and rediploidization decouple an ancient duplication from present-day 1C value [S8]. The very small *Utricularia* genome after multiple duplications is a useful counterexample to treating WGD count as a final-size predictor [S6]. WGD must be modeled as a dated event and an interaction with subsequent gain/loss processes.

### S5. Genome size can matter biologically, but causal direction remains unsettled

Genome size correlates with cell and nuclear properties in multiple settings, yet correlation is not evidence that bulk DNA was selected for a phenotype. Gregory reviews distinct mutation-pressure and nucleotypic explanations [S9]. Knight reports associations between larger plant genomes and evolutionary, ecological, and phenotypic constraints [S10]; within a natural rotifer population, larger genomes correlate with body size, egg size, and embryonic development time [S11]. These observations justify testing trait-linked constraints, with phylogenetic and confounding controls, rather than asserting adaptive optimality.

## Accepted research gaps

| Gap ID | Accepted gap | Why the available evidence is insufficient |
|---|---|---|
| G1 | **Rate-balance gap**: present genome size is often inferred from static repeat fractions without jointly estimating gain, loss, and dated WGD history. | Similar 1C values can arise from different histories; related lineages can expand and contract. |
| G2 | **Repeat-resolution gap**: total repetitive DNA obscures family-specific amplification, silencing, and removal. | Fabeae and *Gossypium* show different dominant repeat trajectories. |
| G3 | **Post-WGD confounding gap**: WGD is frequently interpreted as persistent genomic bulk without a rediploidization/removal model. | *Utricularia* shows a small present genome despite multiple WGD events. |
| G4 | **Constraint-causality gap**: cell, developmental, ecological, and diversification associations are rarely separated from ancestry and correlated life history. | Existing associations do not establish direction or universality. |
| G5 | **Comparability gap**: sparse taxonomic sampling, heterogeneous C-value measurements, assembly quality, and repeat annotation can manufacture apparent lineage differences. | Comparative inference requires explicit measurement and transferability limits. |

## Survey decision

The literature supports a multi-process account: genome size is an evolving balance among DNA-gain processes, DNA-removal processes, demographic/selection context, and lineage history. The evidence does not support a “one master cause” answer. Downstream work must preserve this distinction and must not claim observed genome-size changes from a proposal-only analysis.

## Evidence boundary

This is a targeted, source-bounded survey, not a systematic review. Evidence cards permit general mechanism statements and comparative design choices. They do not permit local effect predictions, trait causation claims, or assertions that a future analysis has measured evolution in real time.
