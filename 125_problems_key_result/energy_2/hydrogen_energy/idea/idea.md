# Idea Agent: Hydrogen-Service Portfolio

## Selected direction

**HYDROGEN-SERVICE-PORTFOLIO**: determine where hydrogen creates additional system value after production, conversion, storage, transport, lifecycle, safety, water, and demand constraints are included. The study compares hydrogen with direct electrification, batteries, sustainable biofuels, ammonia, and incumbent fuels at matched delivered services.

Hydrogen should not be treated as a universal fuel. Its strongest candidate roles are existing chemical feedstocks, selected high-temperature and reduction processes, some shipping and synthetic-fuel chains, remote or resilient power, and long-duration or seasonal storage. Each role has a different comparator, time scale, infrastructure requirement, and acceptable loss or risk.

## Central contribution

The proposed contribution is a **service-normalized, boundary-explicit, and shock-aware hydrogen portfolio framework**. It joins:

1. pathway-level lifecycle emissions and upstream methane or carbon accounting;
2. hourly electricity matching and additionality for electrolysis;
3. production, offtake, transport, storage, and conversion infrastructure;
4. matched end-use service comparisons against direct electricity and competing fuels;
5. leakage, embrittlement, safety, water, land, and material constraints; and
6. staged adoption and demand uncertainty.

The framework avoids the common error of calling a large production volume a success without showing who uses the hydrogen, how it arrives, what service it delivers, and whether the total chain improves climate or reliability outcomes.

## Candidate routes

### 1. Industrial feedstock replacement

Replace incumbent fossil-derived hydrogen in ammonia, refining, and chemical production with lower-emissions pathways. This route has an existing service and demand anchor, but must test whether new hydrogen displaces incumbent production and whether certification and delivered cost are acceptable.

### 2. Hydrogen direct reduction and high-temperature industry

Use hydrogen in iron reduction and selected high-temperature processes where direct electricity is technically limited or costly. Compare hydrogen with electric furnaces, biomass, synthetic fuels, and process redesign at matched output, including ore quality, purity, furnace efficiency, and storage.

### 3. Hydrogen-derived transport fuels

Use hydrogen, ammonia, methanol, or synthetic hydrocarbons in shipping, aviation-derived fuels, and heavy transport where batteries may be constrained by mass, range, charging time, or route structure. Include the full conversion chain and distinguish hydrogen from its derivative at the point of use.

### 4. Long-duration and seasonal storage

Convert surplus or dedicated low-carbon electricity into hydrogen, store it, and recover electricity or heat during prolonged scarcity. Compare round-trip efficiency against batteries, transmission, demand response, firm generation, and other seasonal carriers under the same reliability target.

### 5. Remote resilience and backup

Use hydrogen as a transportable reserve for isolated grids, critical facilities, or sites with infrequent but severe outages. Test fuel inventory, leakage, safety, refueling, and lifecycle cost rather than assuming that low annual utilization is a problem or a benefit.

### 6. Coordinated hydrogen hubs

Co-locate production, storage, pipelines, ports, industry, and flexible power demand to share infrastructure and reduce stranded capacity. The selected system route combines this hub logic with service-specific deployment rather than assuming a single global hydrogen network.

## Falsifiable claims

1. Hydrogen creates greater climate or reliability value than direct electricity only in end uses where its molecular, high-temperature, transportable, or seasonal-storage function compensates for conversion losses.
2. Annual average grid emissions can overstate electrolytic hydrogen benefits when electrolysers operate during fossil-marginal or scarce electricity hours.
3. Production capacity without contracted offtake, transport, storage, and conversion capacity does not establish a viable hydrogen system.
4. Lifecycle emissions, methane leakage, water, safety, material integrity, and replacement constraints can change the ranking of hydrogen pathways.
5. Hydrogen hubs with multiple services and flexible operation are more robust to demand uncertainty than single-user projects, subject to added coordination and safety costs.
6. The value of hydrogen storage is concentrated in rare multi-day or seasonal events and is missed by average utilization metrics.
7. No single hydrogen pathway or color label dominates across regions, sectors, and electricity conditions.

## Required comparisons

The Author Agent should compare at least:

- direct electrification with no hydrogen;
- hydrogen from electrolysis with temporally matched electricity;
- hydrogen from fossil feedstocks with and without carbon capture as separate lifecycle cases;
- hydrogen derivatives such as ammonia, methanol, and synthetic hydrocarbons;
- sustainable biofuels and other non-fossil carriers where the same service is possible;
- hydrogen storage against batteries, transmission, demand response, and firm supply.

Every comparison should report delivered service, lifecycle greenhouse gases, electricity and water use, material and infrastructure needs, safety and leakage risk, cost, reliability, and adoption or offtake uncertainty. A pathway can be useful for one service and inferior for another.

## Expected scientific value

The framework can identify the binding condition for a hydrogen project: electricity carbon intensity, electrolyser utilization, water, storage duration, pipeline or port capacity, end-use conversion, demand certainty, or safety. This supports targeted research and policy. It also allows hydrogen to be valuable without making the stronger and unsupported claim that hydrogen should replace direct electricity everywhere.
