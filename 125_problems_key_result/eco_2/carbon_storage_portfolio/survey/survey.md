# Evidence-Constrained Survey

## Scientific reframing

The broad question “Where do we put all the excess carbon dioxide?” is converted into a testable systems question:

> Which portfolio of carbon dioxide removal and storage pathways can deliver additional, measurable, durable, and equitable net atmospheric CO2 removal at meaningful scale while minimizing lifecycle energy, land, water, material, ecological, leakage, and reversal risks?

This wording separates three actions that are often conflated: avoiding new emissions, capturing concentrated CO2 before it reaches the atmosphere, and removing CO2 that is already in the atmosphere. The study concerns the latter two, but it treats rapid emissions reduction as a prerequisite because storage cannot compensate indefinitely for ongoing emissions.

## Evidence map

### 1. Storage is heterogeneous

Geological storage places compressed CO2 in deep saline formations, depleted reservoirs, or mineralizing formations. Its principal scientific questions are injectivity, trapping, plume migration, well integrity, pressure management, induced seismicity, monitoring, and long-term stewardship. Mineralization can increase permanence but may require reactive rock, grinding, transport, and energy.

Biological storage includes forests, soils, wood products, wetlands, mangroves, seagrasses, and related ecosystems. It can provide co-benefits for biodiversity, water regulation, and livelihoods, but stored carbon can be reversed by fire, drought, harvest, pests, land-use change, or warming. Biological pools are therefore not interchangeable with a permanent geological reservoir.

Engineered removal includes direct air capture with geological storage, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage, enhanced weathering, and selected ocean-based approaches. These methods differ in technology readiness, energy source, cost, land demand, water demand, monitoring burden, ecological side effects, and achievable scale.

### 2. Removal is needed for residual emissions, not a license to delay

The IPCC Working Group III cross-sectoral assessment states that carbon dioxide removal is a necessary element in pathways reaching net-zero CO2 and greenhouse-gas emissions because it counterbalances residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors. It also emphasizes that methods differ in storage timescale, maturity, potential, cost, co-benefits, adverse effects, and governance requirements. This supports portfolio design, not a single-technology answer.

### 3. Sustainable scale is an open research problem

The National Academies research agenda identifies unanswered questions about benefits, risks, sustainable scale potential, commercial viability, and the research and development needed for carbon dioxide removal and reliable sequestration. It explicitly spans land and coastal ecosystem management, accelerated weathering, bioenergy with capture, direct air capture, geological sequestration, and related approaches.

The relevant scale constraint is not only physical pore volume or biomass area. A pathway must also have low-carbon energy, transport and injection infrastructure, monitoring and verification, replacement or stewardship obligations, social permission, and safeguards against shifting burdens to food, water, ecosystems, or vulnerable communities.

### 4. Measurement determines whether storage is real

The study must distinguish gross capture, gross removal, lifecycle emissions, transport emissions, leakage, reversal, and verified retained carbon. A credit based only on captured mass can overstate atmospheric benefit. Monitoring must be designed around the failure modes of each pathway: atmospheric and ecosystem observations for biological pools; pressure, seismic, geochemical, and plume observations for geological storage; and lifecycle energy and material accounting for engineered methods.

## Candidate pathway classes

1. **Geological storage:** saline aquifers, depleted reservoirs, and mineralization; high durability potential, with site-specific integrity and monitoring requirements.
2. **Biological terrestrial storage:** afforestation, reforestation, improved soil carbon, and durable wood products; useful co-benefits but significant reversal and additionality risks.
3. **Coastal and marine biological storage:** wetlands, mangroves, and seagrasses; geographically bounded and vulnerable to disturbance, but potentially valuable for ecosystem services.
4. **Engineered atmospheric removal:** DACCS, BECCS, and enhanced weathering; potentially durable, but constrained by energy, land, water, materials, cost, and ecological impacts.
5. **Ocean-based removal:** alkalinity enhancement and related methods; potentially large but uncertain in ecological effects, verification, and governance.

## Gap ledger

1. Pathways are often compared by nominal removal cost without a common durability-adjusted lifecycle accounting boundary.
2. Geological, biological, and ocean pathways have different reversal timescales, but portfolio models rarely represent those timescales explicitly.
3. Monitoring, reporting, and verification are treated as a reporting layer rather than as a capacity and cost constraint.
4. Energy, land, water, mineral supply, transport, and ecological constraints can interact nonlinearly at scale.
5. A global removal target can hide local land tenure, food security, water, biodiversity, and environmental-justice burdens.
6. Governance after injection or restoration can last longer than project finance, yet stewardship and liability triggers are under-specified.

## Boundary conditions

The survey supports a permanence-aware, portfolio-level experiment. It does not support the claim that all captured CO2 is permanently stored, that forests are equivalent to geological reservoirs, or that future removal can justify continued fossil emissions. It also does not support exact global capacity claims without site-level characterization and lifecycle data.

## Candidate direction

The evidence-compatible direction is **PERSIST-CO2 (Permanence, Energy, Reversal, Storage Integrity, and Trade-offs for CO2)**: a robust portfolio model that chooses pathway shares, infrastructure, and monitoring intensity while accounting for retention, leakage, reversal, lifecycle emissions, resource competition, equity, and long-term stewardship.
