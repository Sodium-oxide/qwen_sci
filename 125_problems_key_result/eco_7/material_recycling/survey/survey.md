# Survey Agent: Toward Near-Universal Material Recovery

## 1. Scientific reframing

The literal question, “Can essentially every material be recycled and reused?”, mixes three different claims:

1. whether a material can be technically transformed into another useful product;
2. whether that transformation preserves enough quality to displace virgin material; and
3. whether the complete collection, sorting, processing, market, and governance system is affordable and environmentally beneficial.

The research question is therefore reformulated as:

> For a defined material-product stream and required service, under what combinations of design, collection, sorting, processing, market demand, and policy can a high fraction of material be repeatedly recovered and reused without increasing life-cycle environmental burdens, toxicity, or total social cost beyond acceptable bounds?

The unit of analysis is a material-product-route-region tuple, not an abstract material label. “Recycled” means that a post-use material is collected, processed, and incorporated into a new product; “reused” means that the product or component delivers another service with limited reprocessing. A material that is technically processable but cannot reach a buyer, fails the next product’s specification, or produces more burden than virgin production is not counted as a successful circular route.

## 2. What the evidence supports

### 2.1 Recycling is a system, not a machine capability

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency describes recycling as collection and processing into new products, while placing source reduction and reuse above recycling in its waste-management hierarchy. Its recycling-system overview separates collection and processing, manufacturing, and purchasing/market closure. It also identifies confusion, infrastructure gaps, weak domestic markets, product-design incompatibility, and inconsistent measurement as barriers. This is directly relevant to the proposed study: a laboratory demonstration is only one link in a multi-stage chain.

Historical material-flow work shows why the problem is urgent. Geyer, Jambeck, and Law estimated the scale of global plastic production, use, and fate and showed that production has outpaced recycling. That result is not a universal estimate for every material, but it establishes the need to measure actual post-use flows rather than infer circularity from a recycling symbol or a theoretical process.

### 2.2 Reuse, mechanical recycling, chemical recycling, and biological processing are different

Reuse retains more of the product’s embodied structure but requires durability, return logistics, cleaning, repair, and sufficiently many successful cycles. Mechanical recycling can retain high value when a stream is clean, sorted, and compositionally narrow; repeated heating, contamination, and additives can reduce quality. Chemical recycling can recover monomers or feedstocks from selected polymers, but energy, solvents, catalysts, purification, yield, and product displacement determine whether it is beneficial. Biological treatment can transform selected organic or polymeric fractions, but treatment conditions, residence time, residual fragments, methane control, and actual facility access must be verified.

These routes cannot be substituted by a single “recyclability” score. A route is successful only if it meets the receiving product specification and displaces an identified virgin or primary resource. Downcycling can be valuable, but it should be reported as a change in service and future recovery potential rather than as closed-loop recycling.

### 2.3 Material complexity is a dominant obstacle

Modern products combine polymers, metals, glass, fibers, coatings, pigments, adhesives, flame retardants, plasticizers, electronic components, food residues, and biological contamination. Multi-material assemblies can outperform a single material during use while becoming difficult to separate at end of life. Small concentrations of additives can alter density, melt behavior, toxicity, odor, color, or regulatory acceptability. A high nominal recovery rate can therefore conceal quality loss, hazardous concentration, or transfer to rejects and residues.

The survey treats contamination as a measurable state variable. It includes moisture, food and biological residues, incompatible polymers, metals, labels, pigments, flame retardants, and prior processing history. Sorting accuracy and contamination are jointly modeled because a technically recyclable object can still enter the wrong stream.

### 2.4 Economic viability is not the same as private profitability

Recycled feedstocks compete with virgin materials whose prices may not include litter, cleanup, health, climate, biodiversity, or future waste-management costs. A recycling route can be privately unprofitable while producing positive social value, or privately profitable while externalizing toxic residues and emissions. The study therefore reports at least three ledgers:

- private operating cost and revenue;
- environmental life-cycle impacts; and
- externalized or socially distributed burdens, including worker exposure, community exposure, transport, and disposal of rejects.

Policy instruments such as recycled-content standards, extended producer responsibility, deposit-return systems, landfill taxes, disposal fees, procurement rules, and producer-funded collection are scenario variables. They are not treated as proof that a route is intrinsically efficient.

## 3. Definitions and decision boundaries

The following terms are kept separate:

- **Technically recoverable:** a specified process can transform the material under stated conditions.
- **Product-grade recyclable:** the recovered output meets the next product’s specification and safety requirements.
- **Actually recyclable:** the product is collected, sorted, processed, and sold in the modeled region.
- **Reusable:** the same product or component delivers additional service after inspection, cleaning, repair, or refurbishment.
- **Circular:** material value is retained through repeated service cycles within a stated system boundary.
- **Economically viable:** the route covers defined private or social costs under a declared market and policy scenario.

The primary functional unit is one unit of delivered service, such as one beverage-container service, one square meter of protective packaging for a specified lifetime, one kilogram of recovered construction aggregate meeting a specified grade, or one electronic component surviving a defined refurbishment cycle. Per-tonne waste throughput is a secondary functional unit because it can hide differences in product lifetime and quality.

The boundary begins at product design and includes feedstock extraction, manufacturing, additives, distribution, use, collection, transport, sorting, preprocessing, conversion, purification, residue management, recovered-material manufacturing, and the avoided production of virgin material. Reuse adds washing, repair, return transport, inspection, loss, damage, and cycle count. Leakage and uncontrolled disposal are explicit routes rather than zero terms.

## 4. Evidence map

| Evidence block | Established insight | Remaining research need |
|---|---|---|
| Waste hierarchy | Reduction and reuse can precede recycling in environmental preference | Quantify when reuse loses to recycling because of washing, transport, or low cycle count |
| Material flows | Production and disposal can greatly exceed recovery | Harmonized material-product-route flow data across regions |
| Mechanical recycling | Clean and narrow streams preserve value best | Predict quality retention under realistic contamination and repeated cycles |
| Chemical recycling | Selected chemistries can yield feedstocks | Measure full energy, solvent, catalyst, purification, and displacement burdens |
| Product design | Complexity and additive packages affect sorting and processing | Co-design rules that are compatible with use performance and end of life |
| Markets | Recovered materials need stable demand and specification | Joint techno-economic and life-cycle models including external costs |
| Governance | Collection and market rules can change system outcomes | Compare policy portfolios under distributional and leakage constraints |

## 5. Research gaps and hypotheses

The central gap is the absence of a common test that links material processability to repeated service, actual routing, economic viability, and environmental benefit. The proposed research tests five hypotheses:

**H1 — Capability gap.** A larger set of objects is technically processable than is product-grade recyclable under realistic contamination and quality requirements.

**H2 — Design gap.** Design-for-disassembly, mono-material architectures, reversible joining, additive disclosure, and standardized identification increase recovery yield and recovered-product quality without necessarily reducing use performance.

**H3 — Route gap.** Facility access, sorting accuracy, contamination, market demand, and transport distance change the preferred route more than nominal material category alone.

**H4 — Value-retention gap.** Reuse or refurbishment dominates recycling for products that survive enough cycles, while recycling dominates when cleaning, return, repair, or inspection burdens and losses are high.

**H5 — External-cost gap.** Incorporating environmental and health externalities changes the economically preferred route for at least some material streams.

The strongest claim the evidence can currently support is conditional: near-universal recovery is plausible only for a deliberately designed portfolio of products, materials, infrastructure, markets, and policies. Literal recovery of every object is not a meaningful or likely target because some mixtures, hazardous residues, contaminated items, and dissipative uses cannot be recovered at acceptable risk or cost.

## 6. Proposed evidence acquisition

The study should combine material-flow analysis, controlled contamination and sorting experiments, repeated mechanical and chemical processing, reuse-cycle testing, techno-economic analysis, life-cycle assessment, and stakeholder-confirmed route data. Each claim receives a source, measurement condition, geography, time horizon, uncertainty, and route status. Technical demonstrations and scenario assumptions are never merged into observed results.

## 7. Survey handoff

The Idea Agent should search over a coupled design space:

product architecture -> material formulation -> identification and sorting -> route assignment -> recovered product -> market/policy scenario

The primary direction is **UNIVERSAL-CIRCULAR-RECOVERY**: maximize repeated value retention subject to service, safety, environmental, social, and economic constraints, while explicitly allowing reduction, reuse, repair, refurbishment, recycling, and controlled disposal to compete. The handoff must not promise that every object will be recyclable or that every technically possible route is environmentally desirable.
