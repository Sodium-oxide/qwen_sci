# Survey: Why Do Humans Stop Growing Taller?

## Scientific reframing

The phrase "stop growing" is ambiguous. This project concerns the transition from measurable **longitudinal long-bone growth** to a sustained low-growth state in humans. It does not treat body weight, adult remodeling, cartilage turnover, or organ-size change as the endpoint. The operational problem is to predict residual linear growth and structural growth-plate closure from developmental, endocrine, imaging, and inherited regulatory variables.

## Evidence synthesis

Longitudinal bone growth occurs at epiphyseal growth plates, where resting-zone progenitors and proliferative chondrocytes supply cells that subsequently hypertrophy and are replaced by bone. The growth plate is therefore a target organ for systemic signals as well as local regulators. The reviewed endocrine synthesis identifies IGF-I, Indian hedgehog (IHH), parathyroid-hormone-related protein (PTHrP), fibroblast growth factors, and other pathways as coordinated regulators of chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation [S4, S7]. These mechanisms explain how a plate can continue or slow growth, but they do not by themselves establish the time at which it will close in an individual.

Pubertal estrogen signaling is central to skeletal maturation in people of every sex. A human estrogen-receptor loss-of-function case showed incomplete epiphyseal closure and continued adult linear growth despite otherwise normal masculinization, demonstrating that estrogen signaling is important for bone maturation rather than being a female-only mechanism [S1]. In an experimental study, estrogen accelerated multiple markers of growth-plate senescence and was associated with abrupt epiphyseal fusion after remaining chondrocytes were replaced by bone [S2]. Follow-up animal work tied estrogen acceleration to earlier fusion and to depletion of resting-zone progenitor cells [S3]. These animal data support a mechanistic hypothesis, but they do not identify a numeric human dose-response or justify clinical intervention.

The term *growth-plate senescence* refers to the developmental decline in growth-plate proliferative capacity. It is not equivalent to chronological aging. The reviewed literature describes a finite proliferative capacity of chondrocytes and local maturation within the plate, helping explain why a child can display catch-up growth after transient illness without resetting the entire developmental program [S5]. In contrast, fixed calendar age is an imperfect proxy because puberty timing, health, and inherited variation differ markedly across individuals.

Genetic architecture matters but does not support the statement that genes alone determine when every person stops growing. Large-scale association evidence shows that adult height is highly polygenic and maps common variation to many biological pathways and tissues, including growth-related mechanisms [S6]. Genetic effects can influence growth-plate signaling and pubertal timing, while endocrine milieu, disease, nutrition, and measurement context modify observed trajectories. The project therefore treats inherited features as one predictive input family rather than a deterministic clock.

## Evidence-bound claims

- **CLM-GP-01:** Longitudinal bone growth is generated by proliferation and differentiation within epiphyseal growth plates; local and systemic regulators jointly shape the process [S4, S7].
- **CLM-GP-02:** Functional estrogen signaling is important for human skeletal maturation and epiphyseal closure in more than one sex [S1].
- **CLM-GP-03:** Experimental estrogen exposure can accelerate growth-plate senescence and epiphyseal fusion; proposed cellular depletion mechanisms remain species- and context-limited [S2, S3].
- **CLM-GP-04:** Growth-plate senescence is a local developmental process and cannot be reduced to a single chronological-age threshold [S5].
- **CLM-GP-05:** Adult height has polygenic architecture, so inherited variation is relevant but not individually deterministic [S6].

## Accepted research gaps

Existing evidence is strong on mechanisms and exceptional clinical cases, yet routine human prediction is weak. First, there is no adequately validated longitudinal human dataset that aligns repeated height velocity, radiation-free structural growth-plate measurements, pubertal endocrine state, and inherited variation before closure. Second, a model that estimates the probability of *sustained* growth cessation must be evaluated for calibration across sex, ancestry, pubertal timing, and health context; a cross-sectional bone-age association is not sufficient. Third, molecular mechanisms from animal plates cannot be silently equated with human causal effects.

## Survey conclusion

The defensible answer is conditional: most people stop getting taller because their growth plates progress through development and ultimately fuse, with pubertal estrogen signaling being a major driver of maturation. Genes help shape the system and its timing, but they do not form a complete stand-alone explanation. The selected next step is a prospective, noninterventional, multimodal prediction study that tests whether the relevant mechanisms improve out-of-sample prediction beyond age and bone age alone.
