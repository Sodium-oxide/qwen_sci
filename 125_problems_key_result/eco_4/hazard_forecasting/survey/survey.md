# Survey Agent: Can Catastrophic Weather Events Be Predicted More Accurately?

## Scientific reframe

The phrase “predict a catastrophic event” hides three different tasks:

> How much can an event-aware, physics-informed forecasting and early-warning system improve probabilistic skill, calibration, warning lead time, and false-alarm control for tropical cyclones, tsunamis, and earthquakes, given that the three hazards have different physical predictability limits?

This distinction is essential. A tropical cyclone forecast estimates a future track, intensity, size, rainfall, and hazard footprint over hours to days. A tsunami warning generally begins after a causative earthquake or submarine disturbance has started and uses seismic, deep-ocean, and coastal observations to estimate arrival and impact. An earthquake early-warning system detects a rupture after initiation and can alert locations that have not yet experienced the strongest waves. A long-horizon earthquake probability is a different object again. A system that improves warning decisions without predicting the exact event date is scientifically valuable.

## Evidence map

### 1. Tropical cyclones: forecastable but uncertain

Tropical-cyclone track and intensity forecasts combine dynamical atmospheric models, data assimilation, statistical relationships, ensembles, and expert synthesis. The National Hurricane Center's forecast-verification material distinguishes dynamical, statistical, statistical-dynamical, interpolated, and consensus guidance. It reports that the best-performing component can vary by year and that consensus strategies can perform strongly. This supports a model-comparison design based on rolling hindcasts, calibrated ensembles, and lead-time-specific errors rather than a claim that one AI model universally wins.

Forecastability is not uniform. Track can be more predictable than intensity in some regimes, while rapid intensification, eyewall replacement, land interaction, ocean heat content, and sparse observations create abrupt uncertainty. A useful forecast must report a distribution over track, intensity, rainfall, wind, storm surge, and arrival time. A lower mean error is not enough if extreme tails are underpredicted or if the forecast is unavailable early enough to support evacuation and infrastructure decisions.

### 2. Tsunamis: rapid detection and scenario forecasting

Tsunamis are generated by a set of mechanisms including seafloor displacement from earthquakes, submarine landslides, volcanic processes, and other disturbances. For earthquake-generated tsunamis, the warning problem is sequential: characterize the source quickly, assimilate deep-ocean and coastal water-level observations, propagate waves, and update the alert as evidence arrives. The U.S. Tsunami Warning System currently distinguishes warning, advisory, watch, and threat products, with updates, geographic adjustment, upgrading, or cancellation as new information and analysis arrive. Its public system exposes DART systems, water-level stations, observations, and forecasts as distinct information layers.

The relevant improvement is therefore not a promise to forecast every tsunami before its source exists. It is faster and better-calibrated source characterization, more accurate wave and inundation estimates, and lower false-alarm cost while preserving life-saving sensitivity. Near-field events leave little time for remote confirmation; far-field events allow more sequential updating. Bathymetry, coastal topography, tide, friction, source uncertainty, and sensor latency must remain explicit.

### 3. Earthquakes: probabilities and early warning, not exact prediction

The USGS states that neither it nor other scientists have successfully predicted a major earthquake in the strict sense. A prediction must specify date and time, location, and magnitude. The same source distinguishes probabilistic forecasts from early warning: early warning is issued after an earthquake starts, while probabilities and forecasts describe future likelihood over a stated region and time. This is a hard scientific boundary, not a lack of ambition.

Machine learning can improve earthquake detection, phase picking, event association, ground-motion estimation, and early-warning latency. It does not follow that a detector has discovered a reliable precursor. Reported electromagnetic, animal, radon, or small-earthquake signals must be evaluated against false alarms, base rates, and prospective tests. A system that improves post-initiation warning or regional hazard probabilities can be successful without claiming deterministic prediction.

### 4. Observations, models, and AI

The three hazards share a need for dense, reliable, low-latency observations but differ in their state variables and observation operators. Satellites and aircraft observe atmospheric structure and ocean conditions for cyclones. Seismometers, GNSS, tide gauges, DART buoys, bathymetry, and coastal topography support tsunami and earthquake systems. Data assimilation converts imperfect observations into an evolving state; numerical models propagate that state; machine learning can learn corrections, emulate expensive components, detect patterns, or calibrate uncertainty.

GraphCast and Pangu-Weather demonstrate the rapid development of data-driven global weather forecasting, but their success on medium-range weather fields does not automatically transfer to rare, small-scale, impact-critical extremes or to seismic rupture initiation. Earthquake Transformer demonstrates the usefulness of deep learning for phase detection and picking, but detection is not deterministic earthquake prediction. The evidence supports hybrid, hazard-specific models with prospective evaluation, not a universal black-box claim.

## Cross-hazard gap ledger

1. **Task conflation:** Prediction, probabilistic forecast, nowcast, early warning, and impact forecast are often reported under one word.
2. **Rare-event scoring:** Average RMSE can reward smooth forecasts while hiding missed extremes, false alarms, and poor tail calibration.
3. **Source-state uncertainty:** Initial conditions, rupture geometry, bathymetry, atmospheric-ocean coupling, and sensor errors are not always propagated into the warning.
4. **Latency accounting:** A more accurate model is not useful if data ingestion, compute, communication, and decision latency consume its available lead time.
5. **Distribution shift:** Climate trends, changing observing networks, unusual storm structure, and new sensor types can move the deployment distribution away from training data.
6. **Cross-hazard transfer:** Shared AI infrastructure may help representation learning, but a common model can impose false similarities between atmospheric convection, seismic rupture, and shallow-water propagation.
7. **Decision calibration:** Warnings are actions under asymmetric loss. Thresholds must be selected with evacuation cost, missed-event cost, false-alarm fatigue, and vulnerable-population exposure in mind.
8. **Prospective validation:** Retrospective fitting and post-event reanalysis can make an impossible forecast appear successful unless time locks and information availability are audited.

## Survey conclusion

Catastrophic-event prediction will improve most credibly by separating the physical task for each hazard, upgrading observations and data assimilation, using ensembles and uncertainty calibration, and optimizing the complete warning chain. The highest-value research target is not a universal exact-prediction machine. It is an event-aware system that learns hazard-specific corrections while preserving physical propagation, publishes calibrated probabilities and tail risk, and measures the time from observation to action.

## Handoff to Idea Agent

The recommended direction is **MULTIHAZARD-X**, a hazard-specific, shared-infrastructure forecasting and warning framework. It has separate cyclone, tsunami, and earthquake state-transition modules; a common uncertainty and decision-calibration layer; and an explicit latency budget. Its synthetic tests know the hidden truth, while prospective hindcasts enforce information cutoffs. It should be evaluated against physics-only, AI-only, and uncalibrated baselines.

The handoff must preserve four boundaries: exact earthquake prediction is not assumed; tsunami warning is sequential after source initiation; forecast accuracy is distinct from warning usefulness; and expected results are not observed results. A strong finding would be a calibrated reduction in impact-weighted error and warning regret at fixed latency, not a claim that catastrophes have become deterministic.
