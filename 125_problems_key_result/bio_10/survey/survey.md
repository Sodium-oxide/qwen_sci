# Survey: How Organisms Evolve

## Scientific reframing

``How do organisms evolve?'' is not answered by natural selection alone, even though natural selection is a central mechanism of adaptive evolution. A testable formulation is:

> In a declared lineage, environment, and time window, what heritable variants changed in frequency; what contributions of selection, drift, mutation, recombination, and gene flow are supported; and through which developmental or physiological pathways did these changes affect phenotype and reproductive success?

Evolution is therefore population-level change across generations. Individual organisms develop and reproduce; populations change when inherited variants are transmitted at different rates. Complex structures such as eyes and placentas should be analyzed as historical, developmental, and ecological systems rather than as endpoints that appeared fully formed.

## Survey method

The survey combined publisher-page inspection with dual-index metadata discovery. Sources were selected to cover: population-genetic mechanisms, experimental evolution, historical contingency, development and gene regulatory networks (GRNs), evolutionary novelty, and study-design standards. The synthesis is evidence-based and design-only. It does not generate new genomes, fitness measurements, ancestral reconstructions, or causal experimental results.

## Mechanism map

| Mechanism | Immediate population effect | Necessary measurements | Frequent interpretive error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mutation and recombination | Create or rearrange heritable variants | pedigree, mutation-accumulation, haplotype, and time-series genomic data | Calling every observed variant adaptive |
| Natural selection | Changes transmission through differential survival or reproduction | trait, survival/fecundity, environment, and genotype data | Treating correlation with environment as selection |
| Genetic drift | Random frequency fluctuation, strongest in small effective populations | effective population size, replicate trajectories, neutral controls | Mistaking a frequency change for selection without a null model |
| Gene flow | Imports variants and can homogenize or introduce adaptive alleles | ancestry, migration, geography, and admixture data | Inferring local origin from present-day presence |
| Developmental regulation | Maps genetic and environmental inputs to phenotypes; shapes accessible variation | developmental time course, cell states, perturbation, and comparative anatomy | Treating a regulatory association as a causal origin story |
| Ecological feedback | Alters the selective environment and trait performance | matched environmental exposure and fitness design | Equating performance in a laboratory assay with lifetime fitness |

## Evidence boundaries

Wiser, Ribeck, and Lenski followed 12 *Escherichia coli* populations for 50,000 generations and reported continued mean-fitness gains consistent with a power-law model involving diminishing-returns epistasis and clonal interference. This demonstrates how controlled time-series experiments can measure adaptation; it does not imply that all natural populations evolve monotonically or in a constant environment.

Blount and colleagues showed that a key innovation in one experimental *E. coli* lineage depended on historical contingency. The result separates the occurrence of a beneficial phenotype from the prior mutations that potentiated it. It is therefore a warning against inferring an inevitable trajectory solely from the endpoint.

Shubin, Tabin, and Carroll reviewed evidence that eyes, limbs, and other anatomical novelties often modify pre-existing regulatory circuits. Their ``deep homology'' argument supports testing co-option and regulatory reuse. It does not state that all complex organs share one molecular origin or that homologous genes generate homologous structures without context.

Erwin and Davidson describe the hierarchical organization of developmental GRNs and why changes at different network levels can have different phenotypic consequences. This motivates collecting regulatory and developmental measurements alongside sequence data. A variant with a selection signature still needs a genotype-to-phenotype evidence path before being assigned a mechanistic role in organ evolution.

## Literature gaps motivating the design

1. Population-genomic studies often identify frequency shifts without directly observing developmental mechanism or fitness.
2. Evo-devo studies can reveal regulatory differences without quantifying population-level transmission and selection.
3. Experimental evolution provides causal leverage but may have limited ecological generality.
4. Comparative data can reveal repeated patterns, but convergence can arise through different genomic routes.
5. Historical reconstructions become overconfident when ancestral states, sampling frame, or alternative mechanisms are not declared.

## Survey conclusion

A credible account of evolution must link five evidence layers: inherited variation, population transmission, ecological performance, organismal/developmental mechanism, and reproducible causal evaluation. The downstream proposal, EVOTRACE, treats these layers as gates. An observation may support a descriptive evolutionary claim while still being insufficient for a mechanistic claim about a complex trait.
