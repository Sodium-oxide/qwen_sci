# Survey: Can Cold Fusion Become a Reproducible Energy Technology?

## 1. Scientific reframing

The phrase "cold fusion" mixes at least three different questions: whether low-energy nuclei can undergo a reaction in a condensed-matter environment, whether the 1989 electrochemical palladium-deuterium claims are real, and whether a repeatable device could deliver useful net energy. These questions must be separated. The scientific question adopted here is:

> Under what precisely specified materials, loading, electrical, thermal, and environmental conditions could a low-temperature nuclear process produce reproducible net excess heat together with nuclear products and an energy balance consistent with known reaction channels, and could that process outperform established fusion or non-nuclear energy options?

This reframing makes the claim falsifiable. A calorimetric anomaly alone is not sufficient. A credible result must survive independent, blinded, preregistered replication; calibrated heat and power accounting; isotope and radiation controls; and a quantitative comparison between released energy and the measured products.

## 2. What the original claim was

Fleischmann and Pons reported in 1989 that electrolysis of heavy water with a palladium cathode could produce anomalous heat that they interpreted as deuterium-deuterium fusion. The claim was attractive because the apparatus operated near ordinary laboratory temperatures and used electrochemistry rather than a hot plasma. The associated proposal was not simply that deuterium entered a metal lattice; it was that the lattice somehow enabled a nuclear process at an enormous rate without the expected high-energy radiation.

The contemporaneous record already contained competing outcomes. Jones and colleagues reported low-level neutron observations in condensed matter, while Miskelly and colleagues analyzed published calorimetric evidence and Williams and colleagues reported upper bounds on cold-fusion signals in electrolytic cells. The immediate controversy was therefore not resolved by one positive or one negative experiment. It concerned calibration, background subtraction, palladium loading, cell recombination, material history, detector sensitivity, and the relationship between heat and nuclear products.

The U.S. Department of Energy's 1989 Cold Fusion Panel reviewed calorimetry, fusion products, materials characterization, and related evidence. Its report is a primary historical source for how the evidence was assessed. The report did not establish cold fusion as an accepted energy source. Later reviews and historical analyses describe the field as failing to achieve the reproducible, independently verifiable evidence required for mainstream acceptance.

## 3. Physical baseline

The standard low-energy D-D reactions provide a stringent baseline. The two dominant branches are

\[
D+D\rightarrow T+p+4.03\;\mathrm{MeV},
\]

and

\[
D+D\rightarrow {}^3\mathrm{He}+n+3.27\;\mathrm{MeV}.
\]

The radiative branch

\[
D+D\rightarrow {}^4\mathrm{He}+\gamma+23.85\;\mathrm{MeV}
\]

is strongly suppressed relative to the charged-particle branches at ordinary fusion energies. A useful hypothesis cannot select the heat-producing branch while suppressing all expected neutrons, tritium, charged products, gamma rays, and material activation without a physical mechanism and an independently measured signature.

For two positive nuclei, the reaction probability at low relative energy is governed by the Coulomb barrier and the nuclear cross-section. A simplified expression uses the Gamow factor

\[
P(E)\propto \exp[-2\pi\eta],\qquad
\eta=\frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar v}.
\]

Electron screening, lattice polarization, defects, local fields, or collective excitations can modify the effective interaction. However, screening is not a license to assume arbitrarily large enhancement. The enhancement must be measured or derived, inserted into a reaction-rate model, and checked against products, heat, and material state. The 2008 analysis by Huke and colleagues is an example of work that connects possible metal-related enhancement to experimental implications rather than treating enhancement as proof of a power source.

The central energy-accounting identity is

\[
Q_{\mathrm{nuc}}N_{\mathrm{rxn}}
 = E_{\mathrm{heat}}+E_{\mathrm{radiation}}+E_{\mathrm{stored}}+E_{\mathrm{chemical}}+E_{\mathrm{escape}},
\]

where \(Q_{\mathrm{nuc}}\) is the energy per reaction and the terms on the right must be measured or bounded. If a proposed reaction rate predicts a large heat output but the corresponding neutron, tritium, helium, gamma, or activation signal is absent beyond detector limits, the discrepancy becomes a core physical result to explain, not a detail to omit.

## 4. Evidence map

### 4.1 Calorimetry

The relevant measurement is not cell temperature but excess power relative to a matched control under the same electrochemical input. Open-cell and closed-cell calorimetry face different errors. Recombination of electrolytically produced hydrogen and oxygen can return chemical energy as heat. Thermal gradients, evaporation, gas pressure, electrical lead heating, calibration drift, and changes in emissivity can mimic small anomalies. A valid protocol therefore needs electrical input metrology, blank and dummy-cell controls, calibrated heat-loss models, and an energy audit that includes gas flows and chemical inventory.

### 4.2 Nuclear products

Products should be measured in independent channels rather than inferred from heat. Neutron and gamma detectors need efficiency calibration and shielding characterization. Tritium and helium measurements require contamination controls, isotope standards, blank samples, and chain-of-custody procedures. Surface and bulk composition should be analyzed before and after a run. A reported heat signal without a corresponding product balance is physically incomplete, while a product signal without a calibrated heat balance may be a contamination or detector problem.

### 4.3 Materials state

Palladium-deuterium behavior depends on loading ratio, temperature, stress, grain structure, impurities, cracks, defects, electrochemical history, and isotope purity. The phrase "same cell" is therefore not a sufficient replication specification. A materials protocol must document lattice composition and loading in time, current density, electrolyte, electrode geometry, surface preparation, pressure, thermal history, and post-run microscopy and spectroscopy.

### 4.4 Replication and publication history

The most important negative evidence is repeated failure to obtain the claimed effect under controlled conditions. The 2019 Nature perspective by Berlinguette and colleagues describes a multi-institution re-evaluation designed with high scientific rigor and states that it had not yielded evidence of the effect. That result does not prove that every conceivable low-energy nuclear process is impossible. It does show that the canonical 1989 claim did not meet the evidentiary standard required for a reproducible technology by that study.

## 5. What "possible" should mean

The title question has several thresholds:

1. **Nuclear possibility:** a low-energy nuclear event can be detected with an independently validated signature.
2. **Reproducibility:** the event occurs above preregistered detection thresholds in repeated runs and at multiple laboratories.
3. **Energy feasibility:** the integrated device has positive net energy after electrochemical input, heat extraction, gas handling, shielding, control, and maintenance.
4. **Engineering viability:** output is stable, controllable, safe, manufacturable, and scalable.
5. **Technology competitiveness:** the service is better than established fission, hot-fusion, renewable, storage, or other energy technologies for a stated use case.

The existing evidence does not establish thresholds 1-5 for canonical cold fusion. A future result could change that conclusion, but it would need to identify a specific reaction mechanism and pass the full chain rather than rely on an unexplained excess-heat observation.

## 6. Research gaps

The survey identifies six gaps that motivate the proposed study:

- **Measurement gap:** laboratories use different calorimetry conventions and do not always publish raw time series, calibration residuals, or complete energy inventories.
- **Materials gap:** loading and defect states are treated as hidden variables even though they may dominate run-to-run variation.
- **Product gap:** heat, helium, tritium, neutron, and gamma observations are rarely acquired in a single synchronized experiment with common blanks.
- **Statistics gap:** small positive claims are vulnerable to optional stopping, multiple comparisons, detector backgrounds, and selective reporting.
- **Replication gap:** independent protocols are difficult to compare because cell construction and material histories are underspecified.
- **Decision gap:** even a scientifically real effect has not been translated into a transparent threshold for net power, safety, lifecycle impact, and comparison with conventional fusion.

## 7. Survey conclusion and handoff

The strongest current conclusion is conditional: the canonical cold-fusion energy claim has not been established as a reproducible nuclear phenomenon, and the 2019 multi-institution reassessment reported no evidence of the effect. A scientifically useful future study should not ask whether a positive temperature excursion looks interesting. It should ask whether a preregistered, product-linked, blinded energy balance survives independent replication and whether the inferred reaction rate is compatible with nuclear physics.

The handoff is a **blind product-linked replication and mechanism test**. It will compare palladium-deuterium electrochemical cells with matched protium and open-circuit controls, measure heat and nuclear products simultaneously, randomize run identities, model detector backgrounds before unblinding, and use a Bayesian evidence threshold plus predefined null criteria. The experiment remains design-only until actual laboratory data are obtained.
