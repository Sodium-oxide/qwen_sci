# Survey: Where Do Human Emotions Originate?

## Scope and scientific reformulation

This survey treats the question "Where do human emotions originate?" as a causal systems-neuroscience problem. It does not ask for a single anatomical location, nor does it attempt a philosophical account of subjective experience. The operational question is: **which interacting neural, bodily, computational, developmental, and evolutionary processes generate measurable affective states, action tendencies, and emotion reports under defined conditions?**

The answer supported by current evidence is distributed and conditional. Emotion-relevant states arise when neural systems integrate exteroceptive cues, interoceptive signals, homeostatic and allostatic needs, memory, learned predictions, and action policies. Subcortical and brainstem circuits contribute to rapid defense, approach, arousal, nociception, autonomic control, and endocrine regulation. Cortical and hippocampal systems integrate sensory context, memory, value, bodily state, and behavioral goals. Language and category learning can alter the label, report, and fine-grained discrimination of a state without implying that language alone creates the underlying regulatory process.

This formulation separates four targets that are too often conflated:

1. **Affective state:** a dynamically regulated organism-level state involving valence, arousal, bodily control, and action readiness.
2. **Emotion episode:** a context-bound configuration of affect, perception, memory, action, physiology, and often conscious report.
3. **Emotion concept or label:** the learned categorical description used in communication and self-report.
4. **Measurement channel:** neural activity, autonomic/endocrine physiology, behavior, and report. None is a complete ground truth on its own.

## Evidence map

### Conserved regulatory and action systems

Animal lesion, stimulation, pharmacology, and ethology provide the strongest causal evidence for specialized components of defense, reward seeking, nociception, caregiving, arousal, and action selection. The amygdala, hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray, ventral striatum, midbrain dopamine systems, brainstem autonomic nuclei, and related networks are repeatedly implicated. The appropriate inference is not that any one structure is a location for a human emotion. Rather, these circuits implement biologically meaningful computations and coordinated actions that constrain later affective experience. LeDoux emphasizes the distinction between defensive survival circuits and the conscious experience named fear; this prevents reverse inference from a circuit response to a subjective category. Adolphs similarly distinguishes emotion states, concepts, expressions, and experiences.

The amygdala is an important hub for salience, associative learning, autonomic regulation, and threat-related behavior, but its activity is neither necessary nor sufficient as a universal marker of fear. Reward research also separates wanting, liking, prediction, and reinforcement, with partially dissociable dopaminergic, striatal, pallidal, cortical, and brainstem mechanisms. These examples motivate a component model instead of a one-region/one-emotion map.

### Distributed human representations

Human functional neuroimaging and multivariate decoding show that emotion episodes recruit widespread cortical and subcortical patterns. A meta-analysis by Lindquist et al. found little evidence that discrete categories consistently map one-to-one onto distinct brain regions. Conversely, distributed-pattern studies can classify multiple induced emotion categories above chance. Both observations are compatible: a category may have a reproducible multiregion configuration without possessing a dedicated, invariant anatomical center.

Important cortical regions include anterior and midcingulate cortex, anterior insula, orbitofrontal and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal systems, temporal association cortex, and hippocampal formations. Their contributions should be described in computational terms such as sensory integration, value representation, contextual memory, precision/attention, action selection, interoception, and regulation. Anatomical labels alone are inadequate causal explanations.

### Brain-body coupling, interoception, and allostasis

The brain receives continuous visceral input from cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, immune, and endocrine systems. Interoception is the processing of these signals. It is central to affect because emotion episodes involve changes in and predictions about the body's internal condition. The insula, cingulate regions, somatosensory systems, brainstem nuclei, hypothalamus, and autonomic pathways participate in this bidirectional brain-body loop.

Allostasis provides a biological control problem: organisms predict and prepare for energetic and regulatory needs before a deviation becomes dangerous. Construction-oriented and predictive-processing accounts propose that affective experience depends partly on predictions about interoceptive and exteroceptive causes. These proposals yield empirical predictions about context dependence, cross-modal integration, uncertainty, learning, and individual differences, but they must be tested against alternatives rather than treated as established fact.

Autonomic measures such as electrodermal activity, heart rate, heart-rate variability, respiration, pupil size, and cortisol are valuable because they measure bodies participating in an episode. They are not emotion detectors. The same physiological pattern can occur during exertion, orienting, cognitive effort, temperature regulation, or distinct affective contexts. Analyses therefore require concurrent task, behavioral, neural, and report data.

### Learning, memory, development, and social cues

Experience changes affective processing through associative learning, extinction, habit formation, memory reconsolidation, neurodevelopment, and neuromodulation. The hippocampus supports context sensitivity, while prefrontal and striatal systems contribute to value-based learning, control, and policy selection. Developmental exposure and social signals can tune how bodily changes and environmental cues are interpreted; these influences are biological learning variables, not an appeal to non-mechanistic cultural explanation.

Human social emotions such as shame, guilt, pride, and attachment depend on complex models of self, other agents, social prediction, and learned categories. A natural-science account should therefore expect larger and more context-sensitive networks, not merely an enlarged version of a basic facial-expression circuit.

## Competing model families

| Model family | Central claim | Strong prediction | Principal limitation if used alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discrete functional-circuit model | Recurrent ecological challenges recruit partially specialized evolved circuits. | Threat, reward, pain/avoidance, and caregiving tasks show partly separable causal and physiological signatures. | Can overgeneralize animal action circuits into claims about human conscious categories. |
| Distributed representational model | Emotion categories are distinguishable by multivariate patterns across interacting systems. | Held-out participants and contexts permit above-chance decoding from distributed neural and bodily features. | Decoding alone does not identify mechanism or causal necessity. |
| Predictive-interoceptive/allostatic model | Episodes depend on predictions that regulate and interpret bodily state under context and uncertainty. | Manipulating bodily prediction and contextual priors changes reports and multichannel state estimates beyond stimulus intensity. | Formal predictions require explicit models and careful comparison against arousal and demand alternatives. |
| General arousal/task-demand null | Common activity reflects salience, attention, response preparation, or difficulty rather than emotion-specific organization. | Matched non-affective effort tasks explain apparent affective signatures. | Cannot explain reliable condition-specific action, learning, report, and physiological configurations if they remain after controls. |

## Measurement and inference rules

1. Do not use a self-report category as the sole definition of emotion.
2. Do not infer a specific subjective emotion directly from activation in one region.
3. Report convergent and divergent validity across neural, autonomic, behavioral, and report channels.
4. Treat fMRI, EEG, endocrine, and autonomic association studies as correlational unless an intervention and identification assumptions justify a causal claim.
5. Distinguish the temporal scale of measurements: EEG, pupil, electrodermal, cardiac, fMRI, and cortisol do not index the same biological event at the same latency.
6. Prefer preregistered competing-model comparison and held-out prediction over post hoc narratives.

## Knowledge gaps that motivate the study

* Few studies compare circuit, distributed, and predictive-interoceptive models in one preregistered multimodal protocol.
* The correspondence between within-person bodily dynamics and categorical reports remains weakly quantified.
* Many findings rely on reverse inference or samples too small for reliable individual-level conclusions.
* Context, uncertainty, action availability, and task demand are frequently confounded with valence and arousal.
* Human causal perturbation evidence is constrained by ethics and limited access to clinical stimulation cohorts.

## Survey conclusion

The most defensible scientific answer is that human emotions originate in an integrated brain-body control system, not in a single brain location or a single chemical. Conserved regulatory systems supply biological constraints; distributed neural networks construct context-sensitive state representations; interoceptive and autonomic signals participate in regulation; and learning and memory shape categories and actions. The following idea and design convert this synthesis into falsifiable tests.
