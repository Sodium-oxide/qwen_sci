# Survey - Can Human Aging Be Stopped?

## Scientific restatement

The literal question is not testable as posed. A finite human study cannot establish that aging has stopped forever, that a maximum lifespan no longer exists, or that an intervention prevents every age-associated disease. This project therefore asks a narrower question: **can a specified intervention causally reduce a preregistered rate of change in a multidomain biological-and-functional aging state, while preserving safety and producing a concordant downstream health signal?** The required evidence is stronger than a single biomarker improvement and weaker than a claim of immortality.

Chronological age is elapsed time; biological aging is a set of heterogeneous changes in cells, tissues, physiology, resilience, and disease susceptibility. Mortality risk is related to, but not identical with, either construct. A report that death risk among verified survivors is similar at ages 105 and 106 concerns a conditional demographic hazard. It does not imply that molecular damage ceases, that each individual has stopped changing, or that a medicine has arrested aging. Selection, population quality, exposure history, and uncertainty at exceptional ages must remain visible in the interpretation.

## Evidence map

The hallmarks framework describes interacting processes rather than a single clock: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alteration, loss of proteostasis, altered nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem-cell exhaustion, and altered intercellular communication. The 2023 update additionally foregrounds disabled macroautophagy, chronic inflammation, and dysbiosis. This framework is useful for forming mechanism hypotheses, but it is not a validated diagnostic definition of biological age and does not license intervention efficacy claims by itself.

Biomarkers can include epigenetic, proteomic, metabolomic, immune, imaging, and physiologic measures. They differ in technical repeatability, tissue specificity, responsiveness to short-term exposures, and relation to outcomes that matter to people. A clock that moves in a favorable direction may be a useful target-engagement signal without being a validated surrogate for disability-free survival or mortality. Conversely, a functional outcome can be distorted by learning, expectation, or acute illness. A credible program predefines several orthogonal domains, calibrates their measurement error, and reports disagreement rather than selecting only favorable assays.

Human studies support neither a current ability to stop aging nor the use of any putative geroprotector as general clinical advice. Caloric-restriction research, nutrient-sensing modulation, senescence-targeting approaches, exercise and risk-factor management, and other interventions motivate mechanistic and translational research, but they differ in evidence quality, eligibility, safety, feasibility, and endpoint relevance. An intervention that changes a laboratory signal but worsens frailty, infection risk, cognition, reproductive health, or chronic disease control fails a health-span objective. The proposed work is a research design, not treatment guidance.

## Source-bound conclusions

The evidence supports three direct conclusions. First, human aging should be studied as a coordinated but nonidentical collection of processes and outcomes. Second, late-life mortality plateaus, where present in a carefully defined population, are demographic observations that do not answer whether individuals biologically stop aging. Third, translating mechanism-directed interventions requires randomized or otherwise causally credible human evidence with function, clinical events, and safety tracked alongside biomarkers.

The evidence does not support claims that centenarians are biologically non-aging, that any intervention has permanently halted human aging, that a biological-age clock is sufficient for regulatory efficacy, or that reducing mortality risk at one age proves indefinite survival. These exclusions define the boundary for the Idea and ExperimentDesign stages.
