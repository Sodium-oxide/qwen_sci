# Survey Agent Report: Fundamental Principles of Collective Motion

## Research framing

The question is scientifically sharpened from “What are the fundamental principles of collective motion?” to: **which measurable, conditional mechanisms explain and predict the transition among disordered motion, correlated disordered motion, polar collective motion, and active-turbulent motion across biological and engineered groups?** This wording avoids assuming that birds, fish, bacteria, cells, and robots share one microscopic interaction law merely because their trajectories can look alike.

Collective motion is an out-of-equilibrium phenomenon: agents inject and dissipate energy locally, exchange information or forces with neighbors, and move in an environment that can alter those interactions. The evidence supports recurring macroscopic observables--polarization, spatial velocity correlation, response propagation, density heterogeneity, and defect or vortex statistics--but it does not establish a universal set of numerical coefficients or a single causal kernel valid at every scale. The useful scientific target is therefore a **conditional universality program**: identify the smallest mechanism-aware state description that remains predictive when species, scale, sensing modality, and environmental geometry change.

## Evidence map

### E1. Minimal alignment models expose robust collective states, not a complete biology

The Vicsek-class perspective and its hydrodynamic descendants establish that self-propulsion, alignment, and noise can produce long-range orientational order, density structures, and anomalous fluctuations in dry active matter [S1]. Reviews of active matter emphasize that persistent energy consumption invalidates equilibrium assumptions and makes symmetries, conservation laws, and transport terms central to the large-scale description [S1, S2, S11]. These models are useful null models because they make explicit what follows from a proposed interaction rule; they are not direct measurements of animal cognition, bacterial signaling, or a robot-controller implementation.

### E2. Local interaction laws must be inferred from trajectories rather than assumed

Empirical fish trajectory analysis found that speeding and turning responses are dynamically modulated, that directional changes can be copied from individuals ahead, and that three-body effects can contribute materially even where pairwise structure is informative [S9]. The accompanying methodological review argues that common assumptions in individual-based schooling models require experimental re-examination [S5]. This evidence rules out a simple practice of fitting a visually plausible flocking animation and calling its rule fundamental. Predictive validation must be held out in time, group size, and perturbation context.

### E3. Correlation, order, and responsiveness are distinct observables

Wild midge swarms can exhibit strong correlations without global polar order [S8]. Conversely, bird flock analyses and turning models treat the transmission of directional information as a separate question from steady polarization [S4, S7]. The first principle supported by the survey is therefore operational rather than taxonomic: a system should be described by a vector of collective observables, not a binary label of “flocking” versus “not flocking.”

### E4. Interaction channels vary by scale and medium

At the microswimmer and bacterial scale, low-Reynolds-number hydrodynamics, propulsion geometry, boundaries, oxygen or chemical conditions, and steric interactions can all shape collective behavior [S3, S12]. Dry-aligning models suppress many of those channels by construction [S1]. Animal groups may instead be limited by sensing, decision delay, visibility, behavioral state, and social context [S4, S5, S9]. Active matter provides shared language--activity, coupling, noise, geometry, and order--but not permission to exchange mechanisms without testing.

### E5. Engineered swarms demonstrate that physical and informational constraints matter

Outdoor drone-flocking work required explicit treatment of communication delay, barriers, perturbations, bounded motion, and collision avoidance; those terms are not cosmetic additions to an idealized alignment rule [S10]. This makes collective-robot systems a valuable, controllable comparison domain, provided their validation is not mistaken for evidence about an animal’s sensory or motivational mechanism.

## Accepted gaps

| Gap ID | Evidence-supported gap | Why it matters | Required downstream constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | There is no consistently validated map from inferred local mechanisms to a shared, cross-scale collective-state description. | Similar patterns can be generated by different mechanisms. | Compare mechanism-aware and species-specific models on held-out prediction. |
| G2 | Polar order, correlation length, turning propagation, and local forecastability are often reported separately. | A group can be correlated without globally ordered. | Treat them as a joint measurement vector and pre-register decision rules. |
| G3 | Model selection is frequently based on fit to the same trajectories used for inference. | Visual realism is not a causal test. | Require blocked temporal, group-size, and perturbation validation. |
| G4 | Environmental geometry and delay are poorly integrated into cross-domain comparisons. | Obstacles, confinement, fluid coupling, and sensing can change a regime. | Encode geometry and delay as explicit moderators, not residual noise. |
| G5 | Cross-scale analogies rarely quantify what transfers and what does not. | “Universal” claims become unfalsifiable. | Use a shared-latent versus domain-specific model comparison. |

## Evidence plan and claim boundary

The downstream Idea Agent may claim that activity, neighbor coupling, stochasticity, delay, and geometry are candidate organizing variables. It may not claim that any one interaction topology, hydrodynamic mechanism, or behavioral heuristic is universal. It must preserve the distinction between correlation, order, causal influence, and out-of-sample prediction.
