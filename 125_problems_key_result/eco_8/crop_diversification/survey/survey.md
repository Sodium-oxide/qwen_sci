# Survey Agent: Beyond Monoculture Dependence

## 1. Scientific reframing

The question “Will we soon see the end of monocultures like wheat, maize, rice, and soy?” is too broad to answer with a date. It combines farm-level practice, crop diversity, genetic diversity, food-system concentration, ecological services, climate risk, farmer income, nutrition, and policy. A scientifically testable version is:

> For production systems dominated by wheat, maize, rice, or soy, when do crop rotations, intercropping, varietal diversification, mixed farming, and neglected or underutilized crops improve food-supply stability and ecological services without unacceptable penalties in yield, income, nutrition, land use, water, or labor under climate and pest shocks?

The unit of analysis is a crop-region-management-shock tuple. A monoculture is defined by the spatial, temporal, and genetic scale at which one crop or a narrow genetic base dominates. Diversification can occur across time through rotation, across space through mixtures or mosaics, across genotype through cultivar mixtures, and across the food system through a portfolio of major and neglected crops. These forms are not interchangeable.

## 2. Evidence map

### 2.1 Why dominant crops persist

Wheat, maize, rice, and soy are supported by seed systems, machinery, storage, processing, feed markets, trade infrastructure, agronomic knowledge, and policy. A farmer may choose a monocrop because it lowers per-unit operational cost, simplifies timing, and has a reliable buyer even when it creates longer-term soil, pest, pollination, or climate exposure. Removing monoculture without replacing these services can reduce adoption or shift production elsewhere.

### 2.2 Ecological and epidemiological risks

Repeated cultivation of a narrow crop and genetic base can simplify habitat, increase dependence on pesticides or fertilizers, reduce soil organic matter, and create synchronized exposure to drought, heat, flood, pests, or pathogens. The danger is not that one pest will always eliminate a crop; it is that correlated failures across large areas can produce simultaneous yield loss, price shocks, feed shortages, and nutrition effects. Climate change can alter pest ranges and phenology, making historical performance less reliable.

The risk should be measured as covariance and tail loss, not only as mean yield. A diversified system may have a slightly lower mean yield but lower variance, lower probability of catastrophic failure, or better recovery after a shock. Conversely, diversification can fail if all component crops share the same climate sensitivity, if markets cannot absorb them, or if land conversion increases.

### 2.3 Benefits and trade-offs of diversification

Rotation and intercropping can improve nutrient cycling, soil cover, weed suppression, biological control, and pollinator resources. Genetic diversification can slow pathogen adaptation or reduce synchronized susceptibility. Forgotten or underutilized crops can add nutrition, drought or heat tolerance, local value chains, and cultural fit. However, benefits depend on species, climate, management, scale, and processing.

Diversification can also introduce costs: more seed and machinery types, complex planting and harvest timing, crop competition, lower mechanization efficiency, new pests, more post-harvest handling, uncertain prices, additional labor, and a need for new agronomic knowledge. A crop portfolio is not automatically sustainable. Its land, irrigation, fertilizer, pesticide, transport, and processing burdens must be compared with the food and ecosystem services it delivers.

### 2.4 Biodiversity as an agrifood-system variable

FAO describes biodiversity as the foundation of sustainable agricultural production and food security. Its biodiversity page links agrifood systems to crop, livestock, soil, freshwater, and ecosystem diversity, and reports that many crops depend at least partly on animal pollination. This supports a system-level hypothesis: diversity can be an input to resilience and ecosystem function, not merely a conservation endpoint.

The evidence does not justify saying that all monocultures are harmful or that all diversified farms yield more. The correct question is conditional: which form of diversity, at what scale, in which region, and for which outcome?

## 3. Definitions and functional units

The study reports at least four levels:

- **Field diversity:** number and evenness of crops or genotypes in one field and season.
- **Rotation diversity:** sequence of crops across seasons and years.
- **Landscape diversity:** spatial mosaic, non-crop habitat, and connectivity at farm and watershed scales.
- **Food-system diversity:** concentration of production, calories, protein, micronutrients, seed sources, and trade routes across crops and regions.

Primary functional units are one hectare-year of production and one unit of delivered nutrition. Nutrition units can be one megajoule of dietary energy, one kilogram of protein, or a micronutrient-defined basket; food loss and processing are included. A yield comparison in tonnes alone is secondary because crops differ in moisture, calories, protein, micronutrients, storage, and feed or industrial use.

The system boundary includes seed and breeding, land preparation, fertilizer, irrigation, pesticides, machinery, labor, field emissions, soil carbon, harvest, storage, processing, transport, food waste, prices, and dietary substitution. For risk, it includes shocks, correlated failure, recovery time, imports, reserves, and farmer income. Pollination, natural pest control, erosion, and soil functions are recorded as ecosystem-service outcomes.

| Soil and nutrients | Rotations and cover can alter soil structure and nutrient cycling | Long-term, region-specific measurements under commercial equipment |

## 5. Gaps and hypotheses

The central research gap is that agronomic diversification, ecological function, and food-system resilience are often evaluated in separate studies. The proposed study tests:

**H1 — Stability.** A crop or varietal portfolio has lower production tail risk than a monoculture portfolio under correlated climate and pest shocks, even when its mean yield is not higher.

**H2 — Mechanism.** Rotation, intercropping, and genetic diversification improve distinct mechanisms; combining them can reduce pesticide dependence or soil loss, but may create competition and management costs.

**H3 — Scale.** Diversity at field, landscape, and food-system scales produces different outcomes; field diversity alone cannot guarantee national food security if processing and trade remain concentrated.

**H4 — Forgotten-crop transition.** Adding locally adapted neglected or underutilized crops improves nutrition or climate resilience only when seed, agronomy, processing, storage, demand, and price risks are jointly supported.

**H5 — Adoption.** A diversification package is more likely to persist when its total expected value includes reduced tail loss, ecosystem services, and transition support rather than only first-season yield.

**H6 — No universal prescription.** No one diversification pattern dominates wheat, maize, rice, and soy systems across all climates, soils, farm sizes, and market conditions.

## 6. What would count as “the end”

The end of monoculture should not be defined as zero single-crop fields. Some crops and regions may remain specialized because of climate, mechanization, storage, safety, or market requirements. A meaningful transition has three measurable components:

1. lower concentration of production, genetic risk, or synchronized failure where concentration is hazardous;
2. sufficient alternative crops, cultivars, rotations, or habitats to reduce ecological and food-system risk; and
3. an economically viable transition that does not export land conversion, labor burden, or food-price harm to another region.

This definition allows a targeted monoculture to coexist with diversified rotations, landscape habitat, cultivar mixtures, regional crop portfolios, and strategic reserves. It is more useful than a binary monoculture versus diversity label.

## 7. Proposed evidence acquisition

The study should combine long-term field trials, commercial-farm observations, remote sensing, crop and pest models, seed and market data, nutrition analysis, and farmer-led transition experiments. Weather and pest covariates should be linked to yields at the same spatial and temporal scale. Out-of-sample years and regions are required because a diversification effect learned in one climate may not transfer.

A causal design should compare matched farms or randomized management blocks, pre-register functional units and thresholds, and report both mean and tail outcomes. The study should measure crop yield, nutritional output, soil organic carbon, erosion, water, fertilizer, pesticide, pollinator and natural-enemy indicators, labor, machinery, income, price volatility, adoption, and food loss. The life-cycle model must include land-use change and displaced production.

## 8. Survey handoff

The Idea Agent should search over:

crop portfolio -> field and landscape design -> genetic diversity -> agronomic inputs -> pest and climate shock -> nutrition and market -> adoption and policy

The selected direction is **DIVERSIFIED-RESILIENT-AGRIFOOD**. It should optimize food and ecosystem services under shocks, not maximize the number of crops. The handoff must keep mean yield, tail-risk reduction, nutrition, income, land, water, labor, and adoption as separate objectives.
