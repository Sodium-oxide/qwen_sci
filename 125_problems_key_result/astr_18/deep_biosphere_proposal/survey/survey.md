# Survey Agent Report: Volume, Composition, and Significance of the Deep Biosphere

## Scientific reframing

This project defines the marine deep biosphere as a connected set of dark, high-pressure habitats: bathypelagic and abyssal waters, subseafloor sediments, and permeable oceanic crustal fluids. The threshold of 1,000 m is useful for the water column, but it is not a biological boundary. Volume, cell abundance, taxonomic composition, metabolic activity, and ecosystem significance must be estimated separately for each compartment before aggregation.

The evidence-supported conclusion is that the deep biosphere is globally extensive and biogeochemically consequential, but its integrated size and activity remain uncertain because sampling geometry, cell-count conversion, molecular biases, and decompression artifacts are not uniform across compartments. It should not be described as a single homogeneous bacterial or archaeal reservoir.

## Frozen evidence

S1 Colwell and D'Hondt review deep-biosphere extent and metabolism. S2 Kallmeyer et al. provide a global subseafloor abundance and biomass synthesis. S3 Hoshino et al. report globally distributed marine-sediment community diversity. S4 Orcutt et al. review dark-ocean and subseafloor microbial ecology. S5 Biddle et al. show archaeal importance in deep marine sediment. S6 Amano et al. demonstrate that atmospheric-pressure handling can overestimate deep-sea heterotrophic activity. S7 Nagata et al. synthesize bathypelagic ecology and biogeochemistry. S8 Wasmund et al. examine sulfur cycling in marine sediments. S9 Lever et al. connect energy limitation to subsurface microbial life. S10 Starnawski et al. describe long-term persistence in deep sediment.

## Evidence map

| Question | Established evidence | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat volume | Bathymetry, sediment coverage, and crustal circulation constrain physical volume | Habitable pore-water volume and accessibility vary spatially |
| Cell inventory | Sediment abundance compilations and water-column counts support a large reservoir | Cell-to-carbon conversion and sample recovery generate uncertainty |
| Composition | 16S and metagenomic studies show bacteria and archaea structured by redox and carbon gradients | DNA does not establish viability or flux |
| Activity | Sulfur, methane, hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, and iron transformations support energy-limited life | Rates are low, patchy, and pressure sensitive |
| Significance | Transformations alter carbon burial, remineralization, methane, and oxidant budgets | Scaling site rates globally risks false precision |

## Accepted gaps

G1: No pressure-corrected cross-compartment framework jointly estimates habitat volume, cell inventory, and metabolic flux.

G2: Community composition is often measured after decompression or with method-specific molecular bias, weakening the link between observed taxa and in situ function.

G3: Global significance calculations often combine cell abundance with activity rate estimates that have incompatible depth, pressure, and geochemical coverage.

G4: The distinction among standing biomass, viable cells, active cells, and carbon flux is frequently blurred.

## Survey handoff

Downstream work must preserve compartment identity, quantify uncertainty rather than publish a single point estimate, include pressure-retention comparisons, and treat molecular potential separately from measured activity. The highest-value direction is a pressure-resolved atlas that joins volumetric mapping, absolute abundance, multi-omics, and in situ geochemical rates.
