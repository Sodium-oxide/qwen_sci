# Idea Agent Output: Stage-Adaptive Proteostasis–Immune–Repair Platform

## Primary direction

The **Stage-Adaptive Proteostasis–Immune–Repair Platform (SPIRP)** is a biomarker-guided therapeutic research program for Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease. It does not assume that “neurodegenerative disease” is one disorder. Instead, it combines a shared systems backbone with disease-specific pathology modules and tests whether intervention order determines durable outcome.

The backbone has three layers:

1. **Proteostasis restoration:** reduce toxic aggregate burden and restore autophagy-lysosomal or related clearance capacity.
2. **Immune-state normalization:** move microglia and astrocytes from a maladaptive inflammatory state toward a clearance- and repair-compatible state without eliminating protective innate functions.
3. **Neural repair:** protect surviving neurons and, only after the niche is characterized as permissive, test stem-cell-derived neuronal or glial replacement.

The Alzheimer module measures amyloid-beta and tau-related burden; the Parkinson module measures alpha-synuclein burden and nigrostriatal dopaminergic vulnerability. The design asks whether a shared order-of-operations rule exists even when the molecular target differs.

## Candidate routes and selection

**Route A — Single universal anti-aggregation drug.** Rejected. It ignores disease-specific proteins, glial state, stage, and neuronal loss.

**Route B — Broad anti-inflammatory treatment.** Rejected as a primary direction. It risks suppressing protective clearance and cannot define a responder state.

**Route C — Drug repurposing screen only.** Retained as a discovery engine, but insufficient without brain exposure, target engagement, and durability testing.

**Route D — Stem-cell replacement first.** Rejected as a first-line sequence because unresolved pathology may damage transplanted cells and circuit integration cannot substitute for disease control.

**Route E — SPIRP.** Selected because it covers all eight accepted gaps, produces phase-specific predictions, and can be staged from patient-derived cells to animal models and then carefully selected human biomarker studies.

## Mechanistic thesis

The key innovation is to treat cure as a trajectory through a biological state space. A patient or model is not simply “diseased” or “healthy”; it occupies a state defined by aggregate burden, clearance capacity, glial state, neuronal reserve, circuit function, and systemic covariates. A therapy should be selected by the state it is intended to move, not only by the disease label.

## Falsifiable predictions

- A proteostasis intervention will improve aggregate clearance and cellular resilience only in models with measurable target engagement; a generic reduction in inflammatory markers without clearance is insufficient.
- A state-selective immune intervention will improve neuronal survival more than broad immunosuppression when protective clearance and maladaptive inflammatory outputs are measured separately.
- Combination order will matter: clearing or stabilizing pathology before cell replacement will improve graft survival, integration, and circuit rescue compared with replacement in an unresolved pathological niche.
- A composite biomarker state will predict response better than any single blood, CSF, imaging, or transcriptomic marker in held-out patient-derived models.
- Durable remission requires maintained functional benefit and no pathological rebound after a prespecified washout; transient biomarker improvement will not qualify.

## Portfolio decision

SPIRP is ambitious without claiming an immediate clinical cure. It offers a concrete path to discover which disease stages can be durably controlled and which require repair. The strongest result would be a replicated stage-by-module rule with a post-treatment durability window. A failure to obtain this rule would reject the claim that one shared therapeutic sequence applies across Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, while still producing disease-specific response maps.
