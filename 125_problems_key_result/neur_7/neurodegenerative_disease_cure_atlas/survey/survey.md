# Survey Agent Output: Can We Cure Neurodegenerative Diseases?

## Scientific reframing

The broad question is converted into a testable biomedical question:

> Can a biomarker-stratified, stage-adaptive combination that restores proteostasis, normalizes maladaptive neuroinflammation, and protects or replaces vulnerable neurons produce durable disease modification across Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease models?

“Cure” is not used as a rhetorical synonym for symptom relief. In this proposal it has three nested operational levels: **functional control**, sustained slowing or arrest of progression; **biological remission**, durable reduction of disease-linked pathology and inflammatory state after treatment withdrawal; and **repair**, recovery of lost circuit function without renewed progression. The first two levels are realistic near-term experimental endpoints; the third is a demanding endpoint requiring both disease control and neuronal repair. A therapy is not called curative merely because a short-term motor or cognitive score improves.

## Evidence map

### 1. Neurodegeneration is heterogeneous but has convergent mechanisms

Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease are distinct disorders, yet both can involve protein misfolding and aggregation, synaptic and axonal dysfunction, mitochondrial or metabolic stress, glial activation, and progressive neuronal vulnerability [E1], [E2], [E3]. Convergence does not imply that every disease has the same initiating cause or that one drug will work across all stages. It does justify testing a shared therapeutic backbone with disease-specific pathology modules.

### 2. Protein aggregation is a target and a timing problem

The amyloid hypothesis established a mechanistic framework for Alzheimer’s disease in which amyloid-beta accumulation can initiate or amplify downstream tau, synaptic, inflammatory, and neuronal injury processes [E1]. The clinical success of lecanemab in early symptomatic Alzheimer’s disease shows that removing a disease-linked protein species can slow decline, but it does not demonstrate reversal or complete arrest [E6]. The therapeutic implication is direct: pathology clearance must be paired with stage selection and downstream injury measurements. A late intervention may remove a target while leaving established circuit damage.

Parkinson’s disease includes alpha-synuclein misfolding and Lewy pathology alongside dopaminergic neuronal loss, circuit dysfunction, and non-dopaminergic disease processes [E2], [E3]. Therefore, an Alzheimer-specific amyloid intervention cannot be generalized to Parkinson’s disease without an alpha-synuclein and nigrostriatal module. The common design variable is proteostasis burden, not a common protein.

### 3. Neuroinflammation can be harmful, protective, or state-dependent

Microglia and astrocytes participate in debris clearance, synapse remodeling, innate immune signaling, and tissue repair. Chronic or maladaptive activation can amplify synaptic loss and neuronal stress, but indiscriminate suppression may block protective clearance and repair. Heneka et al. positioned neuroinflammation as a central Alzheimer’s disease mechanism while emphasizing a complex, stage-dependent biology [E4]. Single-cell work identified disease-associated microglial states rather than one uniform “activated microglia” population [E5]. The survey therefore favors state-selective immune modulation with pharmacodynamic biomarkers, not blanket immunosuppression.

### 4. Autophagy and proteostasis connect multiple disease pathways

Autophagy regulates damaged organelles and aggregated proteins. Patient-derived human induced-pluripotent-stem-cell models have been used to study autophagy and neurodegenerative phenotypes, offering a bridge between molecular mechanisms and human genetic backgrounds [E7]. The models are not complete human brains and require neuronal maturation, glial co-culture, and external validation. Their value is to test whether a candidate compound restores clearance and cellular resilience in a genotype-aware system before animal or clinical studies.

### 5. Drug repurposing can accelerate mechanism testing

Repurposed drugs have established pharmacokinetic and safety information, which can reduce early development cost and time, but prior approval for one indication does not establish efficacy, brain exposure, target engagement, or long-term safety in neurodegeneration. Pushpakom et al. describe the opportunities and obstacles of drug repurposing, including biological plausibility, trial design, intellectual-property incentives, and evidence quality [E8]. Repurposing is therefore an efficient discovery funnel, not a shortcut around randomized validation.

### 6. Cell replacement is a repair module, not a standalone cure

Stem-cell-derived neurons and glia may eventually replace lost cell types or deliver trophic support. However, cell survival, phenotypic maturity, circuit integration, immune compatibility, tumor risk, and persistence of the underlying disease environment remain decisive. A replacement cell introduced into an unresolved proteinopathy or inflammatory niche may itself be damaged. NPB-CA therefore places cell repair after or alongside disease-state control and requires integration and safety endpoints.

### 7. Current clinical evidence changes the question

Lecanemab provides an important proof of disease modification in an early Alzheimer’s population, but the slowing of decline is not equivalent to cure, and adverse imaging and infusion-related risks require careful selection and monitoring [E6]. The field has consequently moved beyond the binary question “symptomatic versus curative.” The scientifically useful question is whether an intervention can be matched to molecular state, applied before irreversible neuronal loss, and combined with a repair module that produces durable function after treatment ends.

## Deterministic gap ledger

**G1 — Definition gap.** Studies use “neuroprotection,” “disease modification,” “remission,” and “repair” inconsistently; a cure claim needs prespecified pathological, functional, durability, and safety criteria.

**G2 — Stage gap.** The same molecular intervention may work during protein accumulation and fail after neuronal loss. Cross-disease stage-by-treatment maps are incomplete.

**G3 — Combination logic gap.** Proteostasis restoration, immune-state control, mitochondrial support, and neuronal repair are usually tested separately, leaving their ordering and interaction unresolved.

**G4 — Biomarker gap.** Blood, CSF, imaging, and cellular biomarkers are not yet unified into a treatment-selection and target-engagement framework that predicts durable benefit.

**G5 — Translation gap.** iPSC and organoid models capture patient genetics but incompletely represent aging, vascular factors, systemic immunity, and whole-brain circuits.

**G6 — Repurposing gap.** Candidate drugs often have plausible mechanisms without validated brain exposure, target engagement, or disease-stage-specific efficacy.

**G7 — Repair gap.** Cell replacement may restore a damaged circuit only if the pathological environment is controlled and the graft integrates safely.

**G8 — Durability gap.** Few interventions are tested after washout for pathology rebound, progression arrest, or long-term functional preservation.

## Survey conclusion

The evidence does not support a single universal cure for all neurodegenerative diseases. It does support an ambitious, testable route toward durable remission in defined molecular and disease stages: combine pathology reduction with state-selective immune normalization, use patient-derived cellular models for target and responder selection, and add neuronal repair only after the tissue environment becomes permissive. The strongest downstream idea is a **Stage-Adaptive Proteostasis–Immune–Repair Platform (SPIRP)** that treats “cure” as a measurable sequence rather than a one-drug promise.

## Evidence boundary

No new clinical, animal, cellular, or computational experiment was performed for this report. Existing clinical observations are described only as evidence anchors. The downstream design is a proposal, with `observed_results=[]`. It does not recommend off-label drug use or claim that stem-cell therapy is clinically ready.
