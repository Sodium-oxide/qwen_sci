# Survey Agent: What Creates the Earth's Magnetic Field (and Why Does It Move)?

## Scientific reframe

The popular question is scientifically decomposed into two coupled questions:

> How do thermal and compositional convection in Earth's electrically conducting outer core, organized by rotation and constrained by the core--mantle boundary, sustain the main geomagnetic field; and how can internal core dynamics be separated from ionospheric, magnetospheric, lithospheric, and induced signals to explain and predict observed field motion?

The object of study is therefore not a permanent bar magnet inside Earth. It is a time-dependent electromagnetic field generated by fluid motion in a conducting planetary core, observed through a mixture of internal and external sources. The “movement” of the magnetic field includes secular variation of the internal field, apparent motion of the magnetic poles, short-period external disturbances, and field changes induced in the crust and oceans. These processes have different time scales and must not be collapsed into one pole-drift number.

## Evidence map

### 1. Internal field generation

The geodynamo hypothesis identifies the liquid outer core as the main source of the large-scale field. A conducting fluid moving through a magnetic field induces electric current; the current produces magnetic field, and Lorentz forces feed back on the flow. The system is self-excited rather than an externally powered coil. Sustained operation requires a positive energy budget for convection and magnetic induction after viscous, Ohmic, and other losses.

Thermal buoyancy from secular cooling and compositional buoyancy associated with inner-core growth can drive convection. Rotation supplies a strong organizing constraint through the Coriolis force. Rotation does not create magnetic flux by itself: it changes the geometry, coherence, and helicity of the convective flow so that induction can overcome diffusion. The field is generated throughout a turbulent, rotating spherical shell and then filtered by the conducting mantle, crust, ocean, atmosphere, and magnetosphere before it reaches a measurement site.

Numerical dynamo studies demonstrate that rotating spherical-shell models can produce self-sustaining magnetic fields and reversals under appropriate control parameters. Classic simulations and later scaling studies establish a mechanism class, not a unique Earth solution. The unresolved issue is how the accessible parameter regime, heterogeneous boundary heat flux, inner-core morphology, electrical conductivity, and unresolved turbulence combine to produce Earth's amplitude, spectrum, secular variation, and reversal behavior.

### 2. Why the field moves

On decadal to millennial time scales, the main field changes because the flow and magnetic field in the outer core change. The field at the surface is often represented by a spherical-harmonic expansion. Its coefficients evolve, causing intensity changes, directional changes, and apparent pole motion. A pole trajectory is an observation-derived summary; it is not itself a moving solid object.

The core contribution is not the only time-varying contribution. Ionospheric and magnetospheric currents respond to solar-wind forcing, auroral processes, tides, conductivity, and electrodynamic coupling. Their signals can be large on seconds-to-days time scales and can contaminate an estimate of the slowly varying internal field if data selection and source separation are inadequate. Time-varying fields also induce currents in the lithosphere, oceans, and infrastructure. A physically meaningful analysis must model the internal and external sources jointly or use a source-separation procedure whose error is quantified.

### 3. What is observed

Ground observatories provide long time series at fixed locations and are essential for secular variation, magnetic storms, and regional field changes. Satellite missions provide global spatial coverage and simultaneous measurements of the field, position, and space-weather environment. The Swarm mission is particularly relevant for separating spatial scales and characterizing lithospheric, core, ionospheric, and magnetospheric signals. The International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF) provides a community model and a common representation of the main field and its secular variation.

Paleomagnetic records extend the observational window beyond instrumental measurements. They constrain long-term intensity, direction, excursions, and reversals but have irregular spatial and temporal sampling and nontrivial recording biases. Core--mantle boundary flow inversions provide a bridge between observations and dynamics, yet they are non-unique: different flow fields can produce similar surface magnetic changes when only limited harmonic degrees and noisy time derivatives are available.

### 4. Evidence anchors

Roberts and King review the physical origin of Earth's magnetism and the dynamo problem, including the role of core convection and rotation. Buffett frames the core energy and geodynamo problem in terms of the sources and sinks that determine whether induction can be sustained. Glatzmaier and Roberts provide an early three-dimensional self-consistent numerical demonstration of a reversing geodynamo. Christensen and Aubert establish scaling relationships for rotating convection-driven dynamos. Alken et al. document the thirteenth-generation IGRF as a reference-field product and explain the use of global geomagnetic observations for field representation. These sources support the mechanism and measurement architecture; they do not by themselves validate a new forecast or a particular causal decomposition.

## Gap ledger

1. **Energy-to-field closure:** It remains difficult to connect uncertain core heat and composition budgets, boundary heat-flow heterogeneity, unresolved turbulence, and magnetic dissipation to a surface field with the observed amplitude.
2. **Core-flow non-uniqueness:** Surface secular variation underdetermines the three-dimensional flow at the core--mantle boundary. The same field change can be explained by multiple latent flow states.
3. **Source separation:** Internal secular variation and external ionospheric/magnetospheric currents overlap in space and time. An apparent rapid pole or intensity change may reflect source mixing rather than a sudden change in core dynamics.
4. **Multi-scale coupling:** Models often treat core, mantle conductivity, lithosphere, ocean, ionosphere, and magnetosphere in separate stages even though induction and observation errors cross these boundaries.
5. **Time-scale transfer:** Instrumental records are short relative to reversal and secular-variation histories. Paleomagnetic records are long but sparse and filtered by geological recording processes.
6. **Forecast validation:** A model can fit past field coefficients while failing to predict held-out vector changes, regional anomalies, or the uncertainty of pole motion.
7. **Parameter identifiability:** Rotation, buoyancy, conductivity, boundary heat flux, and external-current parameters can compensate for one another in an inverse model. Identifiability must be measured, not assumed.

## Scientific implications

The strongest supported conclusion is that the main field is best treated as a coupled fluid-electromagnetic dynamo whose surface expression moves because its source flow and its external electromagnetic environment evolve. Rotation is a necessary organizing influence in the dynamo picture, but “rotation causes the field” is incomplete: rotation must interact with buoyancy, electrical induction, diffusion, Lorentz feedback, and boundary conditions. Similarly, “the ionosphere moves the field” is incomplete: external currents modify the observed field and can induce currents below, but they are not the primary energy source of the long-lived internal dipole.

## Handoff to Idea Agent

The highest-value research direction is a coupled state-space and data-assimilation experiment. It should infer a latent outer-core flow and magnetic state while explicitly modeling external current components and observation operators for ground and satellite measurements. The test must compare a full coupled model against core-only and static extrapolation baselines on held-out field vectors and secular variation. It must use synthetic twin experiments to identify which parameters are recoverable before interpreting real observations.

The handoff should preserve four boundaries: (i) internal versus external field, (ii) field generation versus field observation, (iii) measured historical variation versus proposed forecast, and (iv) design expectations versus observed results. The next stage should be bold about a testable causal model while refusing to convert an illustrative geodynamo simulation into evidence that Earth's future field has already been predicted.
