# Structural Dynamics of Biomolecular Condensates and Synthetic Compartments: From Native Crowding to Engineered Protocells

The architectural organization of the intracellular milieu has undergone a fundamental paradigm shift, moving beyond classical biophysical models predicated on dilute aqueous solutions to recognize the cytoplasm as a densely packed, structurally heterogeneous environment governed by macromolecular crowding. Within this framework, the boundaries of contemporary research are defined by three interconnected pillars: structural characterization, dynamic properties, and functional implications. As delineated in [1], this survey establishes a rigorous distinction between traditional membrane-bounded organelles and liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS)-driven condensates, highlighting how cells exploit differential partition coefficients to optimize biochemical reaction networks. By integrating native biomolecular condensates (BMCs), synthetic compartmentalization strategies, and advanced imaging modalities, we frame the investigation around the physical principles that govern molecular availability and spatial organization.

This structural reorientation reveals an overarching trend: cellular architecture is increasingly understood as a tunable thermodynamic landscape where physical constraints actively modulate functional coordination. The subsequent analysis navigates the critical challenges inherent in mapping this landscape, particularly the validation of indirect measurement proxies and the integration of multimodal datasets. While cryogenic preservation techniques have revolutionized our capacity to visualize ultrastructure at near-physiological conditions, translating raw imaging data into mechanistic insights remains constrained by calibration gaps and resolution mismatches. Consequently, bridging descriptive correlations with quantitative validation demands standardized evaluation criteria that can anchor proxy measurements to biological reality. By confronting these methodological limitations, the field can transition toward predictive models of multiscale organization, ultimately enabling the rational engineering of synthetic compartments that faithfully replicate the vibrant complexity of native cellular environments.

## 1. Introduction

The intracellular milieu presents a paradigmatic challenge to classical biophysical models predicated on dilute aqueous solutions, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of molecular organization and function. Unlike the idealized test tube, where solutes are assumed to behave as independent particles in a homogeneous solvent, the cellular interior is a densely packed matrix where macromolecules occupy a substantial fraction of the total volume, typically estimated between 20% and 40%. This high degree of macromolecular crowding imposes rigorous physical constraints that govern molecular interactions, transport phenomena, and the thermodynamic stability of biomolecular assemblies. The conventional assumption of random mixing and negligible steric interference breaks down in this environment, necessitating a theoretical framework that explicitly accounts for volume exclusion, entropic forces, and the complex rheological properties of the cytoplasm. From a thermodynamic perspective, the presence of a high concentration of inert macromolecules generates an excluded volume effect that elevates the effective chemical potential of other solutes. This shift in activity coefficients favors states with reduced excluded volume, thereby promoting compaction, oligomerization, and phase separation. Consequently, reactions and equilibria that appear unfavorable in dilute buffers can proceed spontaneously within the cell, driven by the entropic gain released when crowders are displaced. Moreover, the cytoplasm is not a uniform suspension of identical spheres but a heterogeneous mixture of diverse macromolecular species with varying sizes, shapes, and surface chemistries. This heterogeneity introduces additional layers of complexity, including soft interactions beyond hard-core repulsion and the potential for specific crowder-solute interactions that can further modulate phase behavior. The interplay between hard-sphere steric effects and soft electrostatic or hydrophobic interactions creates a nuanced energy landscape where molecular conformations are biased toward compact states, and binding affinities are significantly enhanced compared to dilute conditions. Thus, the crowded landscape acts as a universal modifier of molecular energetics, setting the baseline conditions under which all cellular processes operate and rendering dilute approximations increasingly inaccurate as one approaches physiological densities.

A direct manifestation of this dense packing is the profound alteration of molecular mobility, where the cytoplasm functions as a dynamic, heterogeneous porous medium rather than a simple Newtonian fluid. The fluctuating meshwork of proteins, nucleic acids, ribosomes, and transient complexes creates a tortuous path for diffusing molecules, imposing severe restrictions on transport that depend critically on the size and shape of the tracer relative to the characteristic mesh size of the cytoplasmic network. This structural complexity leads to anomalous diffusion behaviors, where the mean squared displacement of particles scales non-linearly with time, reflecting the intermittent trapping and release events caused by collisions with obstacles. Empirical investigations utilizing inert tracers have provided quantitative evidence of these hindrances, revealing that diffusion coefficients within the bulk cytoplasm are orders of magnitude lower than those predicted by the Stokes-Einstein equation for a water-like viscosity. [2] offers critical insights into this phenomenon by characterizing the diffusion of inert tracers, demonstrating how the presence of macromolecular obstacles systematically reduces mobility across the cell. The study highlights that the porous nature of the cytoplasm imposes a size-selective filter; larger complexes experience significantly greater retardation, while smaller molecules may retain relatively higher mobility over short length scales. This differential mobility has profound implications for cellular physiology, as it can decouple reaction rates from diffusion limits, create concentration gradients even in the absence of active transport, and facilitate the formation of local reaction microenvironments. Furthermore, the viscoelastic properties of the cytoplasm suggest that diffusion is not purely diffusive but may involve cage-like effects and memory terms, complicating the prediction of encounter rates between reactants. The porous medium model thus captures the essence of intracellular transport as a process constrained by both geometric obstruction and the dynamic rearrangement of the crowding scaffold, emphasizing that molecular motion is coupled to the mechanical state of the cytoplasm.

Beyond restricting mobility, macromolecular crowding exerts a powerful thermodynamic drive toward association, promoting the aggregation and condensation of biomolecules that would otherwise remain soluble. The excluded volume effect effectively increases the local concentration of interacting species, lowering the critical saturation concentration required for phase transitions such as liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and aggregation. In dilute solutions, the entropic cost of bringing molecules together may outweigh the enthalpic gains from specific interactions, keeping the system in a dispersed state. However, within the crowded cellular environment, the entropic penalty of maintaining a disordered, expanded conformation becomes prohibitive, as this configuration would exclude a larger volume of crowders. Consequently, crowding biases the equilibrium toward compact, associated states, enhancing the propensity for multivalent interactions to drive demixing. This physical pressure toward organization is vividly illustrated in the formation of biomolecular condensates, which serve as membrane-less compartments for concentrating specific reactants. A striking example is found in bacterial cell division, where the tubulin homolog FtsZ assembles into a ring structure that orchestrates septum formation. Research on Alphaproteobacteria has revealed that FtsZ can undergo condensation to form dense, liquid-like droplets during polar growth, a process that relies on the interplay between protein concentration and the crowded cytoplasmic environment. [3] documents the formation of these FtsZ condensates, providing direct observational evidence that crowding facilitates the assembly of higher-order structures essential for cellular morphology. The ability of FtsZ to form condensates rather than simple filaments underscores the role of crowding in modulating the dimensionality and material properties of cytoskeletal assemblies. Moreover, the promotion of condensation by crowding is not limited to structural proteins but extends to signaling molecules and metabolic enzymes, suggesting that phase separation is a general strategy exploited by cells to organize biochemistry in response to the physical constraints of the crowded interior.

Despite the strong tendency of crowding to drive association, the cellular landscape is characterized by a delicate balance between order and disorder, maintained through precise regulatory mechanisms that prevent pathological aggregation and ensure functional reversibility. If crowding were the sole determinant of assembly, the cell would likely succumb to irreversible precipitation or gelation, compromising viability. Instead, biological systems employ sophisticated networks of regulatory factors that modulate the phase behavior of key components, allowing for dynamic switching between dispersed and condensed states. This regulation involves proteins that can either stabilize condensed phases through cross-linking or destabilize them by disrupting interactions, thereby tuning the system's position relative to its phase boundary. The assembly of FtsZ serves as a paradigm for this regulatory control, where accessory proteins fine-tune the transition between polymeric filaments and condensed droplets. Mechanistic analyses indicate that proteins such as ZapA act as stabilizers that promote cross-linking and enhance condensation, whereas factors like SlmA function as destabilizers that inhibit aggregation or promote disassembly. [4] elucidates this regulatory interplay, detailing how the competition between stabilizing and destabilizing elements enables the cell to dynamically regulate FtsZ organization. This capacity for state switching is crucial for responding to cellular signals and environmental cues, ensuring that condensates form only when necessary and dissolve upon completion of their function. Furthermore, the regulation of phase transitions in crowded environments must account for the risk of maturation, where liquid droplets gradually evolve into rigid solids or amyloid fibrils. Regulatory mechanisms likely include chaperones or post-translational modifications that maintain the condensates in a metastable, functional state, preventing aging and preserving reversibility. Thus, the crowded cellular landscape is not a static trap but a tunable medium where thermodynamic drivers are constantly balanced by kinetic controls and regulatory interventions, allowing cells to harness the power of crowding while avoiding the pitfalls of uncontrolled aggregation.

The integration of these insights reveals why traditional dilute solution models are fundamentally inadequate for describing cellular behavior and why a comprehensive understanding of the crowded landscape is essential. Dilute models neglect the collective effects of volume exclusion, the anomalous diffusion arising from porous media constraints, and the regulatory modulation of phase transitions, leading to significant discrepancies between in vitro predictions and in vivo observations. Specifically, dilute frameworks fail to capture the reduced saturation concentrations for phase separation, the altered reaction kinetics due to restricted mobility, and the emergence of structurally heterogeneous domains that arise from the interplay of crowding and specific interactions. As evidenced by the direct observations of FtsZ condensates and the characterization of diffusion barriers, the cell operates in a regime where crowding is a dominant organizing principle that shapes molecular availability, encounter rates, and structural integrity. The physical constraints imposed by the crowded landscape dictate the thermodynamic stability of complexes, the spatial distribution of reactants, and the material properties of biomolecular assemblies. Therefore, any robust model of cellular organization must incorporate the non-ideal thermodynamics and hydrodynamics of the cytoplasm. This recognition sets the stage for distinguishing between different modes of compartmentalization, such as membrane-bound organelles versus phase-separated condensates, and for developing synthetic strategies that replicate the complex physical environment of the cell. By acknowledging the cell as a crowded, porous, and regulated medium, researchers can better interpret experimental data, design physiologically relevant reconstitutions, and engineer synthetic compartments that function effectively within the constraints of the native intracellular context. Ultimately, the crowded cellular landscape represents a fundamental physical reality that must be central to any discourse on biomolecular organization, structural dynamics, and the engineering of protocells, bridging the gap between abstract biophysical theories and the complex, vibrant reality of life at the microscopic scale.

### 1.1. From Membranes to Phase Separation

The architectural organization of the intracellular milieu has traditionally been conceptualized through the lens of lipid bilayer-defined compartments, wherein membrane-bound organelles establish distinct biochemical domains separated by semi-permeable barriers. This classical framework emphasizes the role of membranes in isolating incompatible reactions, maintaining steep electrochemical gradients, and facilitating selective transport via embedded protein machinery. However, the recognition of liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) as a pervasive mechanism for intracellular organization has fundamentally altered this perspective, revealing a parallel layer of compartmentalization characterized by the absence of lipid boundaries. Biomolecular condensates, often referred to as membrane-less organelles, emerge from the thermodynamic demixing of macromolecules driven by multivalent, weak interactions involving intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), folded domains, and nucleic acids. Unlike the rigid enclosure imposed by membranes, these condensates represent dynamic, liquid-like phases that coexist with the surrounding cytosol or nucleoplasm, exchanging components continuously while enriching specific molecular species. This dichotomy between membrane-enclosed and phase-separated compartments is not merely a matter of physical containment but reflects divergent strategies for regulating molecular localization, condensation, availability, and functional coordination. Membranes achieve isolation through impermeable barriers requiring active transport, whereas LLPS achieves compartmentalization through differential partition coefficients governed by solution thermodynamics, enabling a flexible, tunable mode of organization that responds sensitively to cellular conditions.

A primary functional advantage of LLPS-driven compartmentalization lies in its capacity for rapid response and reversible assembly, capabilities that are often constrained in membrane-bound systems. The formation and dissolution of membrane-bound organelles typically necessitate energetically costly processes such as de novo membrane biogenesis, vesicle trafficking, or protein import machineries, imposing temporal delays that can limit agility in dynamic environments. In contrast, biomolecular condensates can assemble or disassemble on timescales dictated by diffusion and binding kinetics, allowing cells to instantaneously reorganize their internal landscape in response to physiological cues. This immediacy is particularly evident in stress responses, where the rapid condensation of proteins and RNA into stress granules facilitates the swift sequestration of translationally inactive mRNAs, effectively reprogramming gene expression within minutes. The fluid nature of these condensates supports continuous exchange with the dilute phase, ensuring that substrates, cofactors, and regulatory proteins can access the condensed interior while waste products or inhibitory molecules are excluded. Such dynamic equilibrium enables precise control over reaction thresholds; minor perturbations in the concentration of scaffold components, changes in post-translational modifications, or shifts in environmental parameters can trigger abrupt phase transitions. These transitions act as biological switches that amplify signals and coordinate population-level behaviors, leveraging the non-linear response inherent in phase separation to convert graded inputs into all-or-none outputs. Synthetic models have further validated this principle, demonstrating that coacervate-based systems can be engineered to undergo rapid, stimulus-responsive assembly, underscoring the potential of LLPS to serve as a kinetic module for cellular decision-making.

Beyond rapid responsiveness, LLPS offers significant benefits for metabolic channeling and the optimization of biochemical reaction networks. By concentrating enzymes, substrates, and cofactors within a confined volume, condensates can dramatically enhance local reaction rates through proximity effects, effectively bypassing diffusion limitations that constrain dilute solutions. This mechanism parallels the strategy employed by multi-enzyme complexes in metabolic pathways, where intermediates are channeled directly between active sites to minimize leakage and increase flux. In the context of synthetic biology and protocell engineering, this principle has been substantiated through the design of coacervate compartments that exhibit substantial improvements in catalytic activity compared to bulk aqueous environments. Research utilizing enzymatically reconfigurable liquid crystalline coacervates demonstrates that confining reactive species within these artificial compartments can lead to enhanced turnover and stability, highlighting the capacity of LLPS to create optimized microenvironments for specific biochemical tasks. Similarly, studies on binary peptide coacervates reveal that mixing different polypeptide chains can stabilize liquid droplets against the formation of rigid fibrils, thereby preserving the dynamic environment necessary for efficient catalysis. The ability to tune the material properties of condensates—ranging from low-viscosity liquids to more gel-like states—allows cells to modulate the diffusivity of entrapped molecules, balancing the need for high local concentration with the requirement for component mobility. This tunability suggests that evolution has exploited LLPS to create specialized reaction crucibles tailored to distinct metabolic demands, ranging from the rapid processing of transcriptional machinery to the storage and mobilization of metabolites. Comparative analyses indicate that while membranes excel at long-term isolation, condensates provide superior efficiency for transient, high-throughput reactions where rapid access and product release are paramount.

The relationship between membrane-bound organelles and LLPS-driven condensates is increasingly recognized as synergistic, with membranes often serving as scaffolds that template or restrict the formation of condensates, and condensates in turn influencing membrane dynamics and identity. Many membrane-bound structures exhibit internal sub-compartments formed via phase separation, indicating that the two mechanisms can operate in concert to achieve hierarchical organization. For instance, the inner mitochondrial matrix contains ribonucleoprotein condensates that may regulate metabolic flux, while the nuclear envelope is associated with heterochromatin domains that phase-separate to modulate gene silencing. Advanced synthetic models have begun to recapitulate this interplay, highlighting the complexity of multi-layered architectures that integrate phase separation with surface chemistry. Systems combining coacervates with lipid assemblies demonstrate how electrostatic interactions and surface tension can drive the formation of core-shell structures, where a liquid core is stabilized by a lipid monolayer or bilayer. Such designs, informed by observations of pH-dependent reversibility and lipid recruitment, provide insights into how natural organelles might integrate phase separation with membrane curvature sensing and mechanical adaptation. The oleate/PDDA/ATP system, for example, illustrates how electrostatic coacervation can be coupled with lipid assembly to generate complex, responsive interfaces that mimic the adaptive properties of cellular boundaries. These findings emphasize that the functional advantages of LLPS extend beyond simple demixing; they encompass the ability to engage with membrane surfaces, mediate mechanical forces, and participate in feedback loops that regulate organelle identity. Furthermore, the incorporation of ATP-dependent processes into coacervate systems reveals how energy dissipation can sustain non-equilibrium condensates, bridging the gap between passive phase separation and active, membrane-associated organelle maintenance.

Finally, the distinction between discrete membrane-bounded compartments and fluid-like demixing raises critical considerations regarding the stability, maturation, and functional integrity of condensates. While the liquid state is advantageous for dynamics and exchange, there is a persistent risk of transition to solid-like or aggregated states, which can impair function and lead to pathological accumulation. The discrimination between healthy phase separation and aberrant aggregation depends on the balance of interaction valency, sequence features, and environmental conditions. Mechanisms that prevent irreversible aggregation often involve chaperone proteins or regulatory motifs that maintain the metastability of condensates, ensuring that they remain responsive to cellular signals. Synthetic approaches have sought to emulate these safeguards by designing peptide mixtures that resist fibrillization, thereby extending the operational window of liquid droplets and preserving their catalytic competence. Understanding the thermodynamic landscapes that govern these transitions is essential for elucidating how cells maintain functional condensates over long periods despite thermal fluctuations and molecular noise. Moreover, the comparison between membrane-bound and phase-separated systems reveals inherent trade-offs in terms of robustness versus adaptability; membranes offer stable isolation but limited plasticity, whereas condensates provide high adaptability but require sophisticated regulatory networks to prevent pathological solidification. Future investigations must focus on identifying the discriminating observations that distinguish functional LLPS from aggregation, as well as the molecular rules that dictate the choice between membrane encapsulation and phase separation for different cellular functions. Such insights will not only refine our understanding of native cellular organization but also guide the rational design of synthetic compartments with tailored stability and functionality.

### 1.2. Survey Scope and Methodological Challenges

The scientific inquiry into intracellular organization has fundamentally shifted from viewing the cytoplasm as a homogeneous aqueous solution to recognizing it as a densely packed, structurally heterogeneous environment governed by macromolecular crowding. Within this paradigm, the boundaries of contemporary research are defined by three interconnected pillars: structural characterization, dynamic properties, and functional implications. Structural characterization seeks to map the precise spatial distribution of proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and carbohydrates within crowded cellular milieus, moving beyond bulk averages to resolve mesoscopic and nanoscopic architectures. This endeavor is complicated by the fact that the cytoplasm acts as a porous medium, where high concentrations of macromolecules reduce diffusion coefficients and promote aggregation or condensation. Consequently, traditional models based on dilute solutions fail to capture the non-ideal thermodynamic behavior that drives phase separation. Distinguishing between classical membrane-bounded organelles and emerging biomolecular condensates formed via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is therefore critical, as the latter offer distinct functional advantages such as rapid compositional switching and metabolic channeling. Dynamic properties encompass the temporal evolution of these structures, including phase transitions, component exchange rates, and responsiveness to environmental stimuli such as pH, temperature, and ATP concentration. Understanding these dynamics is essential because condensates are not static repositories but active hubs where reaction kinetics are modulated by local viscosity and restricted molecular mobility. Functional implications tie these physical attributes to biological outcomes, examining how compartmentalization enhances reaction efficiencies, facilitates substrate channeling, and enables rapid cellular responses to stress. However, bridging the gap between these domains requires robust methodological frameworks capable of capturing complex biomolecular condensates and synthetic compartments in their near-native states. Traditional fixation and staining protocols often introduce artifacts that distort phase boundaries or obscure transient intermediates, thereby compromising the fidelity of structural and dynamic assessments. Consequently, the development and refinement of advanced imaging modalities have become central to validating hypotheses about condensate formation, stability, and function. As researchers increasingly turn to cryogenic preservation techniques to arrest biological processes in vivo, the reliance on high-resolution volumetric imaging has intensified. Yet, the translation of raw imaging data into meaningful biological insights is constrained by inherent methodological limitations, particularly regarding the validation of indirect measurement proxies and the integration of multimodal datasets. These challenges necessitate a critical re-evaluation of how we interpret spatial organization and dynamic behavior within crowded environments, setting the stage for a comprehensive survey of current capabilities and unresolved questions.

Among the most transformative advancements in this domain is the evolution of cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), which has revolutionized our capacity to visualize cellular ultrastructure at near-physiological conditions. By rapidly vitrifying samples, cryo-ET preserves the native hydration and conformational states of biomolecules, circumventing the dehydration and chemical fixation artifacts associated with conventional electron microscopy. Recent methodological innovations have significantly expanded the applicability of cryo-ET to whole-cell imaging. For instance, the implementation of focused ion beam (FIB) milling has enabled the precise thinning of intact cells into electron-transparent lamellae, facilitating high-resolution tomographic reconstruction of intracellular compartments without the need for extensive mechanical sectioning ([5]). This capability is particularly crucial for studying delicate structures such as bacterial division rings, polar growth assemblies, and eukaryotic organelles, where spatial context and structural integrity are paramount. Furthermore, the integration of correlative workflows has addressed historical limitations in target identification. Techniques such as correlative montage tomography allow researchers to navigate large cellular volumes efficiently, correlating low-magnification overview maps with high-resolution tilt-series data to pinpoint regions of interest ([6]). Such approaches have proven instrumental in documenting the heterogeneity of condensate states, distinguishing between liquid-like droplets, polymeric networks, and solid aggregates. Despite these strides, cryo-ET primarily provides static snapshots of molecular density distributions. While invaluable for establishing baseline structural organization, these images alone cannot fully resolve the kinetic parameters governing condensate assembly, disassembly, or component turnover. Moreover, the sheer complexity of the crowded cytoplasm means that density variations in tomograms often reflect overlapping contributions from multiple macromolecular classes, complicating the attribution of specific structural features to distinct biochemical entities. Thus, while cryo-ET establishes a vital ground truth for spatial architecture, its utility in deciphering dynamic and functional properties remains partially dependent on complementary methodologies that can capture the fluidity and responsiveness of these systems.

To overcome the static limitation of cryo-ET and capture dynamic processes, researchers have increasingly integrated cryo-fluorescence microscopy, creating powerful correlative platforms that combine the specificity of optical labeling with the resolution of electron tomography. However, this integration introduces a fundamental methodological challenge: the validation of cryo-fluorescence and cryo-ET proxies for measuring nanoscale biomolecular localization, condensation, and availability. Fluorescent markers, typically introduced via genetically encoded tags or small-molecule dyes, provide highly sensitive readouts of protein presence and relative abundance. Yet, these signals serve as indirect proxies rather than direct measures of molecular organization. The intensity of a fluorescence signal does not linearly correlate with local concentration due to factors such as fluorophore maturation efficiency, photobleaching, quenching effects in dense phases, and steric hindrance imposed by the crowding environment. Consequently, mapping fluorescence intensities to true nanoscale molecular densities requires rigorous calibration against independent reference measures. Current methodologies frequently lack standardized protocols for quantifying this mapping, leading to potential misinterpretations of condensate composition and phase state. For example, a region exhibiting high fluorescence intensity might be interpreted as a dense protein-rich condensate, whereas it could alternatively represent a highly labeled, sparse network or an artifact of tag clustering. Similarly, cryo-ET density maps, while providing structural context, do not inherently identify the chemical identity of the observed masses. Without explicit correlative alignment, assigning specific fluorescently tagged molecules to distinct density features remains speculative. The absence of robust calibration frameworks means that many studies rely on qualitative co-localization rather than quantitative validation, leaving significant uncertainty regarding the actual molecular availability and partitioning coefficients within condensates. This proxy validation gap underscores the necessity for developing explicit mathematical models and experimental benchmarks that can reliably translate optical and electron signals into unified, physically meaningful representations of cellular organization.

Compounding the proxy validation challenge are inherent failure modes and biases embedded within current computational pipelines designed to integrate cryo-fluorescence and cryo-ET evidence. Modern correlative microscopy systems, such as those utilizing precision instrumented equipment for super-resolution and cryo-imaging, aim to bridge the diffraction limit of light microscopy with the nanometer-scale resolution of electron tomography ([7]). While these multimodal platforms expand the horizons of subcellular investigation, they introduce complex registration uncertainties and resolution mismatches that must be explicitly addressed ([8]). Light microscopy data typically suffers from point spread function broadening and limited axial resolution, whereas cryo-ET data is constrained by missing wedge artifacts and dose limitations that reduce signal-to-noise ratios. When algorithms attempt to align these disparate datasets, subtle misregistrations can lead to false positives or negatives in co-localization analyses. Furthermore, labeling bias presents a persistent threat to data integrity; the addition of fluorescent tags can alter the intrinsic biophysical properties of target proteins, potentially influencing their propensity to phase separate or interact with partner molecules. If the tagged variant exhibits different solubility thresholds or binding affinities compared to the endogenous protein, the resulting correlative maps will reflect engineered artifacts rather than native organizational principles. Current computational strategies often treat these biases as negligible or attempt to correct them through post-hoc statistical adjustments, rather than incorporating uncertainty-aware segmentation and probabilistic modeling into the primary inference pipeline. Without addressing these failure modes at the algorithmic level, the integration of multimodal data risks amplifying systematic errors, leading to overconfident conclusions about molecular localization and condensate architecture. Recognizing these limitations is essential for designing next-generation correlative workflows that prioritize transparency in error propagation and robustness against labeling-induced perturbations.

Addressing these methodological hurdles requires a concerted shift toward standardized calibration protocols and rigorous evaluation criteria that can anchor proxy measurements to biological reality. Future research must prioritize the development of quantitative benchmarks that explicitly test the mapping between fluorescent signals, electron density maps, and known molecular concentrations. This involves constructing controlled synthetic systems with precisely defined compositions and phase behaviors, allowing researchers to systematically validate correlative pipelines under reproducible conditions. Additionally, computational frameworks must evolve to incorporate explicit uncertainty quantification, enabling the generation of probabilistic maps of molecular organization rather than deterministic overlays. Such approaches would acknowledge the inherent limitations of each modality and provide confidence intervals for localization estimates, thereby preventing overinterpretation of noisy or ambiguous data. Furthermore, expanding the scope of correlative studies to include diverse macromolecular classes beyond proteins—such as lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids—will be crucial for capturing the full complexity of crowded cellular environments. Until these methodological standards are established, claims regarding the functional implications of biomolecular condensates must remain qualified, emphasizing the provisional nature of proxy-based observations. By confronting these calibration gaps and computational biases head-on, the field can transition from descriptive correlations to mechanistic validations, ultimately unlocking a deeper understanding of how structural dynamics govern cellular function. This rigorous approach will not only enhance the reliability of existing findings but also pave the way for innovative synthetic compartment engineering, where precise control over molecular organization is paramount for achieving desired biochemical outcomes.

## 2. Physical Principles of Biomolecular Condensates

The fundamental physical principles governing the assembly of biomolecular condensates hinge on a delicate thermodynamic equilibrium between entropic costs and enthalpic gains. In dilute solutions, macromolecules remain fully solvated, but within the densely packed intracellular environment, the exclusionary volume effect—often conceptualized through macromolecular crowding—fundamentally shifts this free energy landscape. The introduction of inert crowding agents, such as high-molecular-weight polyethylene glycol (PEG), generates significant osmotic pressure and depletion forces that effectively lower the energy barrier for demixing. Under these crowded milieus, liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) emerges not merely as a specialized function of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) or low-complexity domains, but as a universal biophysical property inherent to polypeptides regardless of their structural complexity or sequence homogeneity. Comprehensive studies testing diverse protein libraries have demonstrated that even highly charged and folded globular proteins undergo LLPS when subjected to sufficient intermolecular interactions. This perspective reframes LLPS as a null model for in vitro systems, suggesting that the propensity to phase separate is an intrinsic feature of molecular architecture. Consequently, the thermodynamic driving force is less about unique sequence motifs and more about the collective strength of multivalent contacts, which can be quantitatively linked to the saturation concentration ($C_{sat}$) required for condensation. The partition coefficient of the crowding agent itself is also critical; for instance, PEG acts primarily as an excluded volume inducer rather than being sequestered into the condensates, ensuring that the phase transition is driven by the protein-protein interactions rather than artifactually by crowder accumulation [9]. It is crucial to acknowledge that while this establishes LLPS as a generic property of polypeptides under synthetic crowding, the in vitro setup does not fully capture the complex intracellular architecture involving membranes, lipids, or carbohydrates.

The enthalpic contributions to this phase transition are predominantly mediated by three distinct classes of non-covalent interactions: electrostatic, hydrophobic, and hydrogen bonding. The relative weighting of these forces varies significantly across different protein families, dictating both the kinetics of nucleation and the material properties of the resulting condensates. For instance, certain globular and disordered proteins rely heavily on electrostatic attractions, which are highly sensitive to ionic strength and can be readily disrupted by salt addition. Conversely, others are stabilized by hydrophobic patches exposed on their solvent-accessible surfaces, making them susceptible to disruption by chaotropic agents like 1,6-hexanediol. Hydrogen bonding networks, often associated with aromatic residues or polar side chains, further modulate the cohesive energy density. Minimalistic peptide models have proven instrumental in dissecting these drivers, revealing that even simple homo-polypeptides can phase separate via specific interaction types dictated by their sequence. Homopolymeric glycine requires high concentrations driven by hydrogen bonding, whereas valine-rich sequences phase separate at lower concentrations via hydrophobic interactions. Charged peptides, such as those rich in arginine or aspartic acid, generally require high salt concentrations to neutralize repulsive forces before LLPS can occur, or they engage in co-phase separation when mixed to achieve charge neutralization. A logarithmic predictive model incorporating the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) contributions of polar, hydrophobic, charged, and aromatic residues has been developed to accurately forecast $C_{sat}$ values. Validated against external proteins such as FUS, TDP43, p53, and human serum albumin (HSA), this model achieves a high Pearson correlation, demonstrating that static structural features can reliably predict phase behavior. Furthermore, Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) assays have confirmed that the homotypic binding affinity ($K_D$) serves as a primary determinant of phase separation thresholds, establishing a strong linear correlation where stronger binding affinities invariably lead to lower saturation concentrations [9].

While the initial formation of condensates typically yields dynamic, liquid-like droplets characterized by rapid internal rearrangement and fusion, these states are frequently metastable. Freshly formed condensates exhibit hallmark liquid properties, including spherical morphology, rapid droplet fusion, and fast fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), indicating high molecular mobility within the dense phase. However, over time, the dense phase undergoes a thermodynamic relaxation toward more ordered, solid-like states, transitioning into gels, glasses, or amyloid fibrils. This maturation process represents a shift from a kinetically trapped liquid state to a thermodynamically favored crystalline or aggregated assembly. The transition is governed by the gradual reorganization of intermolecular contacts, where transient, multivalent interactions give way to stable, cross-beta sheet structures or crystal-like packing arrangements. Notably, this rigidification does not always correlate with pathological amyloid formation; some proteins mature into amorphous, viscoelastic aggregates without exhibiting classic fibrillar morphologies or thioflavin T positivity. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy reveal that while some aged condensates retain disordered secondary structures, others develop extensive beta-sheet content. Understanding the kinetic barriers that prevent or delay this liquid-to-solid transition is crucial, as cells must actively regulate these pathways to maintain functional liquidity while avoiding aberrant aggregation. The timescale of this transition is heavily influenced by environmental parameters, including temperature, pH, and the presence of specific modulators that can either stabilize the liquid state or catalyze nucleation [9].

To mitigate the inevitable progression toward rigid aggregation, particularly in simplified synthetic systems, compositional heterogeneity offers a powerful thermodynamic strategy. Research utilizing binary peptide coacervates (BPCs) demonstrates that mixing distinct peptide sequences can fundamentally alter the aggregation landscape. When single diphenylalanine-based tripeptides are allowed to phase separate, they rapidly transform from metastable liquid droplets into rigid fiber-like structures driven by extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding and $\pi$-$\pi$ stacking. However, introducing a second, structurally similar but chemically distinct peptide species disrupts this ordering. All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations reveal that mixed peptide systems exhibit a significantly lower clustering degree and a reduced propensity for forming extensive intermolecular hydrogen bond networks compared to their single-component counterparts. By introducing steric and chemical mismatch, the system hinders the precise alignment required for ordered fibril nucleation, thereby trapping the condensate in a stable liquid state over extended periods. Specifically, the study utilizes diphenylalanine-based tripeptides, such as FFM (Phe-Phe-Met) and MFF (Met-Phe-Phe), which individually tend to form rigid fibers upon pH-induced phase separation. However, when mixed at optimal weight ratios, these peptides form highly dynamic coacervates that resist solidification for days. High-resolution $^1$H NMR spectroscopy confirms that the composition of the coacervate phase closely mirrors the input mixture, indicating that both peptide types partition into the dense phase. The MD simulations quantify this stabilization by tracking the aggregation propensity (AP) and clustering degree (CD); mixed systems consistently display lower AP and CD values compared to pure FFM or MFF controls. The reduction in intermolecular hydrogen bonds between heterogeneous peptide pairs prevents the formation of the extended beta-sheet networks necessary for fibrillization. This "kinetic trapping" mechanism ensures that the system remains in a metastable liquid basin, effectively decoupling the thermodynamic drive for phase separation from the kinetic pathway toward aggregation [10]. However, because these observations are restricted to simplified synthetic peptide models, they do not provide direct evidence of spatial organization or molecular availability within native, crowded cellular environments.

The stability of these liquid condensates is inherently coupled to their responsiveness to external stimuli, allowing for dynamic regulation of the entropy-enthalpy balance. Binary peptide systems exhibit remarkable reversibility upon changes in pH, temperature, or oxidative stress. Adjusting the pH alters the ionization state of acidic and basic residues, modulating electrostatic repulsion and attraction to trigger dissolution or reformation. Similarly, thermal fluctuations can overcome the cohesive energy holding the droplets together, while oxidative modifications, such as the conversion of methionine residues to sulfoxides, increase hydrophilicity and drive disassembly. These responsive behaviors underscore that biomolecular condensates operate far from equilibrium, continuously sampling conformational space in response to cellular cues. Beyond structural stability, the liquid state of BPCs provides an ideal microenvironment for biochemical reactions. The high partition coefficients for hydrophobic substrates and transition metal catalysts within these droplets lead to substantial enhancements in reaction rates, such as the accelerated uncaging of fluorophores or copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) click chemistry. The liquid nature of BPCs facilitates the sequestration of various biomolecules, including enzymes like glucose oxidase (GOX) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP), which retain their catalytic activity within the condensate. When integrated into multicompartment artificial cells (MACs) constructed from quaternised amylose (Q-Am) and carboxymethylated amylose (C-Am) stabilized by BSA-modified MnO2 nanoparticles, these droplets serve as modular reaction vessels. The hierarchical organization of MACs allows for the encapsulation of distinct catalytic systems, enabling tandem reactions that would be incompatible in a bulk solution. For instance, the presence of Ru catalysts within the BPC droplets, combined with the specific microenvironment provided by the coacervate, triggers fluorescent product formation only when both inputs are present, functioning as an AND logic gate. Similarly, the disassembly of internal BPCs upon exposure to either protons (acid) or hydrogen peroxide (oxidant) operates as an OR gate. These examples illustrate how thermodynamic stability can be harnessed to create adaptive, life-like compartments capable of information processing [10].

Ultimately, the dichotomy between demixing and aggregation represents a continuous spectrum of material states dictated by the fine-tuning of intermolecular forces. While native cellular environments likely employ a combination of post-translational modifications, chaperone activity, and RNA scaffolding to regulate this balance, synthetic models provide essential insights into the fundamental physics. The observation that LLPS is a generic property of polypeptides necessitates a critical evaluation of physiological relevance, as cells must possess active mechanisms to suppress non-functional phase separation. Simultaneously, the successful stabilization of liquid coacervates through binary mixing illustrates that the transition to solid states is not an insurmountable barrier but a tunable parameter. Future investigations must bridge the gap between these simplified in vitro observations and the complex, multi-component reality of the cytoplasm. Elucidating how nanoclusters evolve into macroscopic condensates and how quality control machinery intercepts maturation pathways will be pivotal for understanding both normal cellular physiology and the pathology of protein aggregation diseases. Moreover, the development of standardized calibration protocols for imaging modalities and the integration of computational models with experimental constraints will further refine our ability to predict and manipulate these phase transitions, paving the way for next-generation biomimetic materials and therapeutic interventions.

### 2.1. Environmental Responsiveness and Regulation

The structural dynamics of biomolecular condensates are intrinsically coupled to their microenvironment, where external stimuli such as pH, temperature, and chemical concentrations serve as precise regulators of phase stability, material properties, and functional output. While native cellular condensates operate within tightly buffered physiological regimes, synthetic models provide a reductionist framework to dissect how specific environmental variables modulate the thermodynamic balance of liquid-liquid phase separation. Recent investigations into programmable coacervate systems have elucidated distinct mechanisms by which compositional heterogeneity and interfacial engineering confer robust responsiveness to external perturbations. For instance, studies utilizing binary peptide coacervates (BPCs) derived from diphenylalanine-based tripeptides have demonstrated that mixing distinct peptide sequences can fundamentally alter the energy landscape of demixing, stabilizing liquid droplets against irreversible solidification while maintaining high sensitivity to environmental cues [10]. These systems reveal that responsiveness is not merely a passive consequence of molecular composition but can be actively engineered through the strategic disruption of intermolecular interaction networks, enabling the design of adaptive compartments that mimic the dynamic regulation of biological organelles.

The stabilization mechanism underlying binary peptide coacervates relies on the introduction of compositional complexity to hinder ordered aggregation pathways. When individual diphenylalanine-based tripeptides such as FFM or MFF are mixed, they form stable liquid droplets that resist the rapid transition to rigid fibrous structures typically observed in short peptide systems [10]. Molecular dynamics simulations provide mechanistic insight into this phenomenon, indicating that mixed peptide compositions lower the clustering degree and reduce the probability of intermolecular hydrogen bond formation between disparate peptide types, thereby disrupting the nucleation of ordered fibrils and preserving a dynamic liquid state for extended periods, up to three days [10]. This compositional tuning renders the condensates highly tunable across a range of physical stimuli. The phase behavior exhibits pronounced pH dependence, with droplets undergoing reversible dissolution and reformation upon cycling between acidic conditions (pH ~6.5) and basic conditions (pH ~7.5), underscoring the critical role of electrostatic repulsion in modulating the effective concentration of peptides at the phase boundary [10]. Thermal stimuli exert a similarly profound influence; heating to 80°C induces complete dissolution followed by rapid reformation upon cooling, demonstrating exceptional thermal reversibility over multiple cycles without significant degradation of the coacervate integrity [10]. Beyond physical parameters, chemical triggers offer orthogonal control over condensate state. The addition of hydrogen peroxide oxidizes methionine residues to sulfoxides or sulfones, increasing local hydrophilicity and triggering disassembly, a feature that can be exploited to induce controlled liquid-to-solid transitions when combined with peptides prone to fibrillization [10]. Furthermore, the system responds to solvent polarity and ionic strength; urea disrupts hydrogen bonding networks to cause disassembly, while elevated salt concentrations screen electrostatic repulsions, thereby enhancing coacervate stability through Debye screening effects [10].

While peptide-based systems emphasize internal compositional regulation, the integration of lipid membranes onto coacervate cores introduces an additional layer of environmental responsiveness governed by interfacial electrostatics and monomer availability. Research on fatty acid membrane assembly around polyelectrolyte/nucleotide coacervates has revealed that the formation of stable hybrid protocell architectures is strictly dependent on pH and the physicochemical state of the lipid components. In systems combining cationic polymers like polyallyldimethylammonium chloride (PDDA) with anionic ribonucleotides such as ATP, the self-assembly of oleate bilayers occurs specifically on the coacervate surface rather than forming separate vesicles, a process identified as monomer-directed assembly [11]. This mechanism requires the oleate concentration to remain below its critical micelle concentration, ensuring that lipid monomers directly interact with the charged coacervate interface to form multilayered structures [11]. Small-angle X-ray scattering analyses confirm the presence of distinct Bragg reflections indicative of contracted interlayer spacing, attributed to strong electrostatic interactions between the anionic oleate headgroups and the polycationic PDDA matrix, which stabilizes the hybrid architecture against thermal and mechanical fluctuations [11]. The necessity of monomer-directed assembly is further evidenced by experiments showing that pre-formed oleate vesicles fail to coat droplets and instead become sequestered internally, emphasizing that the kinetic pathway of membrane formation is dictated by the availability of free lipid monomers relative to the coacervate surface charge density [11]. Reproducibility of this assembly is optimized through a two-step addition protocol, which overcomes kinetic barriers associated with single-step mixing, yielding higher surface coverage and more uniform hybrid structures [11].

The regulatory capacity of these hybrid systems extends significantly to ionic strength tolerance, where the lipid shell confers robust protection against salt-induced disassembly. Uncoated PDDA/ATP droplets exhibit rapid disintegration at sodium chloride concentrations exceeding 25 mM, accompanied by a marked loss of internal viscosity detectable via fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy [11]. In contrast, oleate-coated droplets maintain structural integrity and viscosity profiles up to 150 mM NaCl, demonstrating that the amphiphilic barrier effectively screens electrostatic fluctuations within the core and prevents osmotic collapse [11]. At intermediate salt concentrations, partial fusion of aggregated droplets is observed, characterized by Maltese-cross patterns under cross-polarized light, confirming the preservation of intact lipid bilayers during stress responses [11]. The surface charge modulation induced by membrane assembly is quantifiable through zeta potential measurements, which track a systematic shift from positive to negative values upon oleate incorporation, validating the formation of an anionic outer layer that dictates subsequent molecular partitioning [11]. Dynamic light scattering data further indicate that while most systems show minimal size change upon coating, certain compositions like oligolysine/RNA exhibit partial aggregation, highlighting that stability is composition-dependent and sensitive to the balance between polymer charge density and lipid affinity [11]. Moreover, the permeability of these hybrid compartments remains selectively regulated; cationic dyes such as methylene blue and rhodamine 6G exhibit varied uptake depending on the coating status, while anionic species like fluorescein and calcein face exclusion barriers, illustrating how environmental charge gradients can be leveraged to control molecular partitioning [11]. Retention assays using fluorescent ATP analogs confirm that the internal coacervate matrix remains functionally intact even after rigorous membrane encapsulation, preserving the ability to sequester and concentrate specific substrates within the protected core [11].

The convergence of these findings highlights a broader principle: environmental responsiveness in condensates can be harnessed to construct adaptive, logic-gated microreactors capable of performing computational operations. By integrating the pH and oxidative sensitivity of binary peptide coacervates with the hierarchical organization of multicompartment artificial cells, researchers have engineered systems capable of Boolean logic functions. For example, an OR gate operation is achieved where either proton influx (acidification) or oxidative stress triggers the disassembly of internal BPC droplets, translating distinct chemical inputs into a unified output signal that reports on the metabolic or environmental state of the compartment [10]. Conversely, an AND gate configuration requires the simultaneous presence of a transition metal catalyst and the BPC microenvironment to facilitate bioorthogonal reactions, such as ruthenium-catalyzed deallylation or copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition, resulting in reaction rates enhanced by orders of magnitude compared to bulk solution [10]. This catalytic enhancement arises from the preferential partitioning of hydrophobic substrates into the aromatic-rich coacervate phase, driven by $\pi$-$\pi$ and cation-$\pi$ interactions with phenylalanine residues, which locally concentrates reactants and accelerates kinetics; partition coefficients for aromatic dyes like Nile Red and thioflavin T reach values exceeding 700, reflecting extreme enrichment within the droplet phase [10]. Similarly, the selective uptake capabilities of hybrid protocells allow for the compartmentalization of incompatible enzymatic cascades, where the lipid shell acts as a selective filter while the coacervate core optimizes reaction environments through macromolecular crowding, enabling the sequestration of enzymes like glucose oxidase and horseradish peroxidase without leakage [11]. Together, these strategies demonstrate that by tuning environmental parameters—whether through pH shifts, thermal cycling, oxidative triggers, or ionic strength adjustments—it is possible to program the stability, morphology, and functional output of synthetic condensates, paving the way for sophisticated biomimetic models that bridge the gap between simple phase separation and complex, life-like regulatory networks.

### 2.2. Crowding and Diffusion Constraints

The intracellular environment has historically been conceptualized as a homogeneously viscous continuum, a simplification that often fails to capture the complex biophysics governing molecular mobility within living cells. Traditional models frequently relied on bulk viscosity measurements or phenomenological descriptions of anomalous diffusion, assuming that macromolecular crowding acted as a uniform drag force that suppressed all molecular motion proportionally to particle size. However, contemporary investigations fundamentally challenge this classical view, revealing that the cytoplasm operates as a highly heterogeneous, scale-dependent landscape where crowding imposes distinct physical constraints across different spatiotemporal regimes. This heterogeneity is particularly critical when considering the formation, stability, and dynamic exchange of biomolecular condensates, which rely on precise localization and controlled interaction with the surrounding nucleoplasm or cytoplasm. The prevailing paradigm now recognizes that intracellular diffusion cannot be adequately described by a single macroscopic viscosity parameter; instead, it requires a nuanced framework that accounts for the intricate interplay between freely diffusing solutes and spatially organized, immobile structural elements. By decoupling short-range stochastic motion from long-range obstructed transport, modern research provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how cells regulate reaction kinetics, maintain compartmentalization without relying solely on lipid bilayers, and orchestrate the self-assembly of phase-separated entities. Consequently, the cytoplasm must be re-evaluated not merely as a crowded solution, but as a structured, multi-scale medium that actively modulates the thermodynamic and kinetic pathways of biomolecular condensation.

At the shortest spatiotemporal scales, the dynamics of inert macromolecules defy the traditionally accepted notion of universally suppressed, anomalous diffusion. Advanced imaging methodologies, particularly image mean square displacement (iMSD) analysis derived from raster image correlation spectroscopy, have successfully resolved molecular trajectories well below the diffraction limit, achieving displacements as fine as 20 to 30 nanometers with unprecedented temporal resolution. These high-resolution observations demonstrate that small, inert proteins, such as monomeric green fluorescent protein, undergo unobstructed, normal Brownian motion within the cytoplasm over extremely brief timescales, ranging from $10^{-6}$ to $2 \times 10^{-5}$ seconds. During this initial phase, corresponding to displacements strictly between 25 and 100 nanometers, the effective diffusion coefficient closely matches that observed in dilute aqueous solutions [12] [12]. This empirical evidence resolves longstanding discrepancies between rotational and translational diffusion measurements, suggesting that the immediate solvent shell around proteins experiences negligible steric hindrance and that the local environment is essentially unobstructed. To validate these cellular findings, researchers employed sophisticated model systems that mimicked different types of crowding agents. Solutions containing homogeneously diffusing crowders, such as bovine serum albumin, resulted in uniformly suppressed diffusion regardless of the timescale, failing to replicate the biphasic profile observed in living cells. In contrast, systems populated with spatially organized, immobile obstacles, such as polymeric Sephacryl beads, perfectly reproduced the cellular data, confirming that the initial free diffusion is indeed followed by long-range suppression caused by fixed structural barriers. As the observation window extends beyond the critical 100 nanometer threshold, the molecular trajectory deviates significantly from Brownian predictions, exhibiting a substantial reduction in mobility—often by a factor of three or more relative to the dilute baseline. This crossover point marks a definitive transition from the nano-viscous regime to a 'micro-viscous' state, where motion becomes increasingly constrained by the percolation threshold of the intracellular architecture, highlighting that the cytoplasm's resistance to transport is not intrinsic to the solvent itself but emerges from the spatial arrangement of its constituent components.

The emergence of this long-range diffusion barrier underscores the necessity of modeling the cytoplasm not as a simple fluid, but as a hierarchical porous medium dominated by nanometric obstacles. Comprehensive multiscale analyses indicate that the primary impediments to macromolecular transport are not large, micro-scale organelles, but rather a dense meshwork of smaller structural elements, including ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum networks, and internal membrane systems. The volume fraction of these nanometric obstacles exhibits a strong anti-correlation with local diffusivity, establishing the accessible pore space as the governing parameter for molecular mobility. Within this porous framework, diffusion is hindered by two distinct physical mechanisms: tortuous hindrance, which arises from the geometric complexity of the obstacle network forcing molecules into longer, winding paths, and porous hydrodynamic drag, which reflects the enhanced friction experienced by particles moving through confined spaces near solid boundaries. Numerical simulations solving closure problems on representative elementary volumes have shown that incorporating both mechanisms is essential to accurately predict experimental diffusivity data. Experimental perturbations, such as hyper-osmotic shocks that compress the cell and increase the local concentration of nanometric crowders, further validate this model by inducing a non-linear, size-dependent suppression of diffusivity that aligns precisely with theoretical predictions [2] [2]. Notably, the disruption of major cytoskeletal filaments does not drastically alter bulk diffusion rates, reinforcing the conclusion that the passive, static architecture of the cytoplasm—not active filamentous networks—is the primary determinant of these porous medium effects. Furthermore, the corrected diffusivity data collapse onto a single universal curve when plotted against the nano-obstacle fraction, demonstrating that this structural parameter serves as a robust predictor of transport efficiency across various physiological conditions. At extreme osmotic pressures, however, deviations from the model suggest the onset of complex biophysical changes, such as phase transitions or extreme viscosity shifts, indicating that the porous medium approximation has limits under severe cellular stress.

The bifurcation of intracellular transport into rapid short-range exploration and restricted long-range migration carries profound implications for the biophysics of biomolecular condensates. The preservation of water-like nano-viscosity at sub-100 nanometer scales ensures that the initial encounter rates between interacting molecules are exceptionally high, thereby facilitating the nucleation and growth of liquid-liquid phase-separated compartments. Once formed, the porous medium constraints inherent to the surrounding cytoplasm actively modulate the internal dynamics of these condensates. The localized reduction in diffusion coefficients effectively traps reactants within the condensed phase, enhancing local reaction rates and promoting metabolic channeling through proximity effects. Conversely, the same porous barriers that accelerate local interactions act as significant bottlenecks for long-range transport, limiting the rapid equilibration of components between the condensate and the bulk cytoplasm. This kinetic asymmetry is crucial for maintaining the stability of condensates against Ostwald ripening and for enabling cells to establish distinct biochemical microenvironments with tailored reaction kinetics. Moreover, the size-dependent nature of porous hindrance implies that larger macromolecular complexes or nascent fibrils will experience disproportionately greater restrictions, potentially driving the maturation of liquid condensates into gel-like or solid states over time. Understanding these dual constraints provides a unified physical description of how cellular crowding orchestrates the spatial organization, assembly, and functional regulation of biomolecular condensates. For synthetic biology, replicating this hierarchical porous architecture in engineered protocells could enhance catalytic efficiency and compartment stability. Furthermore, disruptions to the nano-obstacle network, as seen in certain pathological states or under extreme osmotic stress, may lead to aberrant condensate formation or impaired transport, highlighting the physiological importance of maintaining optimal cytoplasmic porosity. Future research must integrate these transport principles with active cellular processes, exploring how ATP-dependent remodeling of the cytosolic meshwork dynamically tunes the porous environment to control condensate liquidity, turnover, and response to environmental stimuli.

## 3. Structural Organization in Native Systems

The bacterial cytoskeleton, particularly the FtsZ-mediated Z-ring, has traditionally been conceptualized through the lens of GTP-dependent polymerization, where FtsZ monomers assemble into protofilaments that scaffold the divisome. However, emerging evidence compels a paradigm shift toward viewing FtsZ organization as a multifaceted process governed by liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and biomolecular condensation. This perspective reveals that the spatial and temporal control of cell division involves a dynamic equilibrium between polymeric scaffolds and liquid-like condensates, offering a robust mechanism for regulating division site selection, assembly kinetics, and stress responses across diverse bacterial lineages. Rather than functioning solely as a static polymer, FtsZ appears to operate within a tunable phase diagram where its organizational state is modulated by intrinsic sequence features, partner protein interactions, and the physicochemical environment of the crowded cytoplasm.

In polar-growing Alphaproteobacteria, FtsZ exhibits a pronounced propensity for self-assembly into dynamic condensates that function as precursors or reservoirs for the division machinery. Investigations into [3] highlight that the FtsZ homolog from Agrobacterium tumefaciens ($FtsZ_{At}$) spontaneously undergoes phase separation under molecular crowding conditions, independent of GTP. This behavior is intrinsically linked to the protein's unusually long C-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR) linker. Truncation experiments demonstrate that reducing the linker length to match that of Escherichia coli FtsZ significantly diminishes condensate formation, establishing a causal relationship between IDR length and phase separation propensity. In vivo, $FtsZ_{At}$ localizes to distinct polar foci that exhibit hallmark liquid properties, including rapid subunit exchange confirmed by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) with half-lives around 5.8 seconds and high mobile fractions. These foci can migrate and coalesce, suggesting they serve as functional storage compartments that sequester FtsZ away from the division site until activation. Notably, the addition of GTP induces the radiating formation of filaments directly from these condensates, linking the condensed state to immediate polymerization capacity. While native observations in A. tumefaciens support this model, some mechanistic validations relied on ectopic expression in E. coli, underscoring the importance of distinguishing lineage-specific adaptations from universal principles. The evolutionary correlation between extended IDRs and polar growth suggests that this phase separation mechanism may be a specialized adaptation for managing asymmetric division and complex cell cycle architectures found in Alphaproteobacteria.

Beyond intrinsic sequence features, the formation and stability of FtsZ condensates are critically modulated by environmental factors and regulatory protein interactions. Research detailed in [13] elucidates how the cellular milieu and partner proteins orchestrate this phase behavior. Using in vitro reconstitution systems that mimic physiological conditions, these studies reveal that potassium glutamate (KGlu), the dominant intracellular anion, significantly promotes FtsZ condensate formation compared to chloride-based buffers, correcting previous underestimations of phase separation propensity based on non-physiological salts. Furthermore, supported lipid bilayers act as potent nucleation sites, accumulating condensates at the membrane interface and extending approximately 10 micrometers above the surface. This accumulation is synergistic, highlighting the role of membrane proximity in organizing the divisome. The regulator SlmA, bound to specific DNA sequences (SBS), plays a dual role: it recruits FtsZ into condensates, effectively sequestering the protein away from the nucleoid, and modulates the material properties of the droplets. The interplay between stabilizing proteins like ZapA and destabilizing regulators like SlmA suggests a tunable regulatory network that switches FtsZ between polymeric and condensed states. Although direct in vivo visualization of these ternary complexes remains challenging, the reconstitution data provides compelling evidence for a mechanism where SlmA·SBS acts as a scaffold to lower the saturation concentration of FtsZ, thereby facilitating condensate formation under physiological crowding.

A central insight from these investigations is the reversible, nucleotide-dependent cycling between condensate and polymer states, which provides a rapid response mechanism for cell division. In the presence of SlmA·SBS complexes, FtsZ partitions primarily into condensates in its GDP-bound state. Upon GTP addition, these condensates disassemble, transitioning into dynamic protofilaments. This GTP-responsive cycle implies that FtsZ condensates function as a metabolic sensor; when GTP levels drop, potentially due to stress or antibiotic exposure, FtsZ can revert to a condensed, inactive state, contributing to the formation of persister cells. Conversely, restored GTP availability triggers the dissolution of condensates and resumption of division. The kinetic analysis of droplet fusion on lipid surfaces further supports the liquid-like nature of these assemblies, with time-lapse imaging showing significant coalescence over hours. However, the viscosity and dynamics can be modulated by divalent cations like Mg$^{2+}$, which may cross-link components or increase internal rigidity, adding another layer of regulation. Sensitivity to 1,6-hexanediol varies between in vitro condensates and in vivo foci, with in vivo structures requiring higher concentrations for disruption, suggesting that cellular factors or indirect physiological effects may stabilize these assemblies beyond simple weak multivalent interactions. This differential sensitivity underscores the complexity of the native environment and the potential involvement of additional stabilizing factors in live cells.

Collectively, these findings construct a cohesive model where FtsZ organization is governed by a phase diagram sensitive to protein sequence, partner interactions, and physicochemical environment. The Alphaproteobacterial reliance on extended IDRs for spontaneous condensation contrasts with the more heavily regulated, SlmA-dependent mechanisms observed in model organisms, suggesting evolutionary divergence in how different bacteria manage the trade-off between assembly speed and spatial precision. ### 3.1. Spatial Heterogeneity and Protein Localization

The intracellular milieu fundamentally deviates from the classical assumption of a homogeneous aqueous solution, presenting instead a landscape of profound spatial heterogeneity where macromolecules are distributed non-uniformly to create distinct functional niches. This organization is driven by macromolecular crowding, which acts not merely as a passive obstacle but as a structured porous medium that modulates molecular mobility and promotes demixing through entropic exclusion effects. Recent analyses highlight that the crowded environment imposes significant diffusion constraints, effectively reducing the mean free path of solutes and enhancing local reaction probabilities through proximity effects. The cytoplasm behaves as a viscoelastic network where the mesh size of the endogenous polymer matrix dictates the accessible volume for different species, leading to a spectrum of microenvironments ranging from fluid-like regions to sterically restricted zones. Evidence from tracer studies indicates that while short-range Brownian motion is preserved over limited distances, long-range transport is severely hindered by the dense mesh of endogenous polymers, resulting in anomalous diffusion dynamics that vary spatially across the cell. [2] demonstrates how inert tracers experience restricted diffusion within the bulk cytoplasm, underscoring the heterogeneous nature of the intracellular landscape where molecular availability and transport kinetics are dictated by local concentration gradients and steric hindrance rather than simple aqueous diffusion coefficients.

Within this heterogeneous matrix, proteins exhibit highly localized distributions that are critical for executing specific cellular functions, often concentrating into discrete domains via phase separation mechanisms. A paradigmatic example is the spatial organization of the bacterial division machinery, where the distribution of FtsZ is strictly regulated to ensure accurate septal ring formation. Contrary to viewing the Z-ring solely as a static polymer, emerging evidence reveals that FtsZ can undergo liquid-liquid phase separation to form condensates, particularly in contexts such as polar growth where traditional mid-cell positioning is adapted. [3] provides direct observational evidence of FtsZ forming condensates in polar regions of Alphaproteobacteria, suggesting that phase separation mechanisms may underpin the rapid assembly and disassembly cycles required for cell division in organisms with asymmetric growth patterns. This protein-centric view emphasizes that localization is not accidental but regulated to concentrate specific machineries at precise subcellular coordinates, facilitating cooperative behavior among FtsZ monomers and enabling the transition from dispersed precursors to functional division sites without the kinetic penalties associated with purely diffusive search processes. However, it is important to contextualize this observation, as the study focuses specifically on bacterial cell division machinery and does not provide broad coverage of nucleic acids, lipids, or carbohydrates, nor does it establish the general principle for all proteins, only those with specific intrinsic disorder characteristics like FtsZ. Furthermore, in vivo evidence relies heavily on ectopic expression in Escherichia coli for some controls, though native Agrobacterium tumefaciens data is present, which necessitates caution when extrapolating these findings to diverse cellular contexts.

The spatial distribution of proteins is further refined by regulatory networks that switch components between different organizational states, thereby tuning the physical properties of condensates in response to cellular cues. The localization of FtsZ is tightly controlled by accessory proteins that modulate its propensity to condense or polymerize, acting as molecular switches that determine the structural integrity of the division site. Interplay between stabilizing factors like ZapA and destabilizing regulators such as SlmA dictates the spatial boundaries of the division zone, ensuring that the Z-ring forms only at the appropriate location and avoiding aberrant placement over the nucleoid. [4] details how these proteins regulate the equilibrium between polymeric filaments and condensed droplets, illustrating a mechanism where competitive binding and allosteric modulation prevent the indiscriminate aggregation of essential components. While this regulatory logic is well-supported by microdroplet models and in vitro reconstitution, it is crucial to acknowledge that direct in vivo visualization of these specific ternary condensates remains technically challenging, and the findings are largely extrapolated from controlled biochemical assays rather than comprehensive native imaging. The paper does not provide structural resolution of the ternary complexes interfaces, and direct in vivo visualization of these specific ternary condensates in living bacteria is noted as challenging and not performed in this study, requiring validation for native cellular contexts. Nevertheless, this mechanism illustrates how spatial heterogeneity is actively maintained through dynamic regulation, allowing cells to rapidly reconfigure their internal architecture in response to developmental or environmental signals.

Beyond static localization, the dynamic properties of proteins within these heterogeneous domains reveal complex transport behaviors that directly impact biochemical reaction rates. Proteins localized within condensates or near membrane surfaces often exhibit altered diffusion coefficients compared to their counterparts in the dilute cytosol, reflecting the varying rheological properties of different subcellular compartments. [12] reports on the dynamic translational motion of molecules in living cells, showing that while short-range Brownian motion persists, the effective diffusion is significantly reduced in crowded regions due to transient trapping and viscous drag. This reduction in mobility enhances the residence time of reactants, thereby boosting local reaction kinetics and promoting metabolic channeling within confined spaces. However, this enhancement comes at the cost of reduced accessibility for large substrates, highlighting a fundamental trade-off between catalytic efficiency and mass transport. The non-uniform distribution thus creates a functional gradient where reaction hotspots are balanced by diffusion-limited corridors, optimizing the allocation of cellular resources. These dynamic constraints underscore the importance of considering both spatial organization and temporal fluctuations when evaluating the functional output of biomolecular condensates, as the turnover rates of components can dictate whether a domain operates as a liquid reservoir or a solid storage depot.

Despite these advances in understanding protein localization, significant gaps remain regarding the spatial organization of other macromolecular classes, revealing a pronounced bias in current observational datasets. Current evidence is heavily skewed toward proteins, with limited direct data available for the arrangement of lipids and carbohydrates within crowded cellular environments, leaving their contributions to spatial heterogeneity poorly characterized. [10] explores organizational principles using synthetic peptide systems, offering insights into how multivalent interactions can drive phase separation and stabilize liquid droplets against rigid fibril formation. However, these models do not provide direct evidence for lipid or carbohydrate organization in native cells, nor do they capture the full complexity of multiscale interactions present in vivo, such as the coupling between protein condensates and membrane curvature or glycocalyx structures. Consequently, while protein-centric observations paint a vivid picture of compartmentalization, the roles of lipid rafts, carbohydrate-mediated cross-linking, and nucleic acid scaffolding in shaping the intracellular landscape remain underexplored and lack rigorous empirical validation. Bridging this gap requires correlative imaging strategies that can resolve nanoscale lipid and sugar distributions alongside protein condensates, moving beyond protein-focused proxies to a holistic view of cellular architecture. Until such multimodal evidence becomes available, claims regarding the universal principles of spatial heterogeneity must be qualified by the recognition that current knowledge is predominantly anchored in protein dynamics, leaving the broader physicochemical integration of all macromolecular classes as a critical frontier for future investigation.

### 3.2. Dynamic Equilibrium and Turnover

Biomolecular condensates formed via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) represent a paradigm shift in our understanding of cellular organization, moving away from the classical view of a homogeneous solution toward a highly structured, phase-demixed environment. A defining characteristic of these membrane-less assemblies is their capacity for continuous, bidirectional molecular exchange with the surrounding cytoplasmic milieu. Unlike traditional membrane-bound organelles that rely on selective transporters and gated channels to regulate internal composition, native condensates function as dynamic steady-state systems governed by the principles of thermodynamics and kinetics. The continuous turnover of proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites across the droplet interface is not merely a passive consequence of the liquid state but an actively regulated process essential for cellular homeostasis. This dynamic equilibrium allows cells to rapidly modulate local concentrations of signaling molecules, sequester aberrant proteins before they aggregate, and buffer against stochastic fluctuations in macromolecular crowding. The thermodynamic balance underlying this exchange relies on a delicate interplay between multivalent weak interactions—such as electrostatic forces, pi-pi stacking, and cation-pi interactions—that drive demixing, and entropic forces that favor solubility in the dilute phase. Consequently, the structural integrity of a condensate is maintained not by a rigid physical barrier but by a persistent dynamic flux, ensuring that the internal microenvironment remains exquisitely responsive to extracellular cues and intracellular metabolic demands. Within this porous medium, molecular mobility is constrained; while short-range Brownian motion persists, long-range diffusion is significantly hindered, creating localized reaction hubs where substrate availability and enzyme concentration are optimized for specific biochemical tasks.

The principles governing native condensate turnover have profoundly influenced the design of bottom-up synthetic biology, particularly in the development of dynamic synthetic cells that emulate these open-reactor architectures. Classical synthetic compartments, such as lipid vesicles or polymer micelles, often suffer from limited permeability and require complex, energy-dependent transport machinery to facilitate material exchange, which complicates their scalability and autonomy. In contrast, LLPS-based synthetic compartments offer inherently permeable boundaries that decouple the timescale of chemical reactions from the timescale of mass transport. As comprehensively reviewed in [14], this open nature allows synthetic protocells to engage in continuous material uptake from supersaturated solutions, effectively mimicking the nutrient absorption processes of primitive cellular life. The field categorizes LLPS into segregative types, such as aqueous two-phase systems (ATPS) driven by polymer incompatibility, and associative types, including complex coacervation mediated by attractive electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged polyelectrolytes. Both strategies facilitate the partitioning of biomolecules based on charge, hydrophobicity, size, and shape relative to the phase-separating components. For example, RNA and DNA preferentially accumulate in dextran-rich phases of ATPS or specific coacervates, while ATP can be concentrated up to twenty-fold in oligolysine/ATP coacervates compared to the dilute phase. This selective enrichment enhances local reaction rates through proximity effects and molecular crowding, accelerating processes such as hammerhead ribozyme cleavage and non-enzymatic oligonucleotide ligation. Furthermore, the ability to reversibly transition between segregated aqueous phases and associative coacervates enables engineers to tune the permeability barrier dynamically. By adjusting environmental parameters such as pH, ionic strength, or temperature, synthetic systems can modulate the partitioning coefficients of encapsulated biocatalysts and substrates, thereby controlling reaction kinetics without the need for artificial pores or gated channels. This approach highlights a critical insight: the most effective synthetic compartments are those that embrace, rather than suppress, the inherent permeability of phase-separated states, leveraging it to create responsive, adaptive microenvironments.

While passive exchange dominates the initial stages of condensate formation, sustaining long-term functionality requires active, energy-dependent turnover mechanisms that prevent system collapse into inert solids or complete dissolution. Native condensates achieve this sophisticated regulation through ATP-dependent remodeling complexes, kinase/phosphatase cycles, and proteolytic degradation pathways that continuously refresh the protein composition within the droplet. Translating this complexity into synthetic systems necessitates the integration of dissipative chemistry, where the synthesis and degradation of phase-separating components compete to maintain a non-equilibrium steady state. [14] emphasizes that stimuli-responsive phase separation, driven by light, enzymatic activity, or redox changes, provides a robust framework for achieving such controlled turnover. Enzyme-responsive mechanisms, for instance, utilize kinase/phosphatase pairs to regulate the charge state of polyelectrolytes, directly modulating coacervation capacity. Similarly, protease-mediated hydrolysis of intrinsically disordered proteins can trigger rapid dissolution, while cell-free transcription systems produce RNA that drives phase separation followed by dissolution via charge inversion. These active processes transform static droplets into autonomous, life-like entities capable of growth, division, and even rudimentary metabolism. Growth mechanisms in synthetic systems include material uptake from supersaturated solutions, coalescence or fusion events often modulated by acoustic forces for uniform sizing, and Ostwald ripening. Division mechanisms are equally diverse, ranging from spontaneous fission driven by physicochemical instabilities, such as polymerized nanofibril contraction, to energy-fueled active division utilizing light-induced isomerization of azobenzene derivatives or chemical reactions that maintain non-equilibrium states. Notably, the reconstitution of cytoskeletal elements, such as FtsZ filament assembly inducing invagination and fission in coacervate droplets, demonstrates how mechanical forces can be coupled to chemical turnover to drive morphological changes, bridging the gap between soft matter physics and biological morphogenesis. The enhancement of biochemical activity within these confined spaces is often attributed to the up-concentration of reactants and the excluded volume effect, which reduces the entropic penalty of molecular association. However, this localization is a double-edged sword; substrate inhibition can occur if enzymes are sequestered within the dense phase while substrates remain excluded in the dilute phase, or vice versa. Additionally, alterations in local viscosity and dielectric constants within the condensate can significantly impact enzymatic turnover rates and binding affinities. Therefore, optimizing the partitioning ratios of all reaction components is paramount to achieving maximal catalytic efficiency.

The concept of dynamic equilibrium extends beyond individual compartments to encompass population-level behaviors, where collective turnover drives emergent properties in synthetic cell communities. In crowded environments, the exchange of materials between neighboring droplets can lead to Ostwald ripening, where larger condensates grow at the expense of smaller ones, ultimately homogenizing the size distribution. However, by introducing competitive or cooperative biochemical networks, researchers can stabilize polydisperse populations and foster symbiotic relationships. Higher-order structures, such as vacuolization and nested architectures observed in RNA/peptide coacervates and elastin-like polypeptide systems, illustrate the potential for multi-phase organization. Artificial organelles have been successfully created by capturing proteinosomes within fatty acid micelle coacervates, forming host-guest protocells that compartmentalize incompatible tandem reactions. Population-level phenomena, including predatory-prey interactions and chemical coupling between synthetic cells, further underscore the necessity of integrating genetic circuits and metabolic networks with LLPS dynamics to achieve true autonomy. Predatory dynamics have been modeled using protease-loaded coacervate droplets that bind to and degrade the membranes of proteinosome prey, illustrating how differential turnover rates can dictate survival and propagation. Parasitic interactions, such as glucose oxidase in proteinosomes producing gluconic acid to lower pH and trigger host coacervate-to-vesicle transitions, demonstrate how metabolic byproducts can be exploited to hijack compartmental resources. These collective behaviors highlight the importance of designing systems where feedback loops regulate component synthesis, degradation, and partitioning, thereby maintaining structural stability over extended periods. The strategic design of reactor boundaries further refines this dynamic exchange. While open reactors permit unrestricted diffusion, many applications demand semi-closed or closed configurations to retain large catalytic complexes or prevent unwanted fusion. [14] outlines several stabilization strategies, including the interfacial assembly of nanoparticles, lipid vesicles, or block copolymers around coacervate droplets. These hybrid interfaces create a tunable permeability barrier that restricts the escape of macromolecules while still allowing the passage of small metabolites and ions. Reversible transitions between membrane-free droplets and membrane-bound vesicles have been achieved through precise manipulation of chemical triggers. For example, pH shifts can induce the protonation or deprotonation of fatty acids, facilitating the transformation of coacervates into stable vesicular structures. Similarly, variations in ionic strength or the introduction of polyoxometalates can trigger the desolvation-driven assembly of elastomeric proteins or the precipitation of specific polymers, effectively locking the compartment into a defined state. These reversible transitions are critical for creating synthetic cells that can cycle between different functional modes, such as switching from a nutrient-absorbing state to a protective, dormant state in response to environmental stress.

Ultimately, mastering dynamic equilibrium and turnover will unlock the full potential of LLPS-driven compartments, transforming them from passive reaction vessels into sophisticated, programmable platforms for biotechnology and medicine. The primary challenge lies in reconciling the high permeability required for efficient material exchange with the structural stability needed to sustain multi-step biochemical cascades. Current synthetic systems face substantial hurdles in replicating the intricate regulatory networks of native cells. A major limitation is the difficulty in achieving complete reversibility in certain droplet-to-vesicle transitions, which often results in irreversible aggregation or loss of compartmental identity. Furthermore, the integration of complex enzyme networks and cell-free expression systems with LLPS dynamics remains in its infancy, limiting the ability of synthetic cells to autonomously regulate their own composition. Control over droplet size distribution during growth is frequently compromised without the application of external confinement techniques, such as microfluidics or acoustic trapping, which adds complexity to large-scale production. Addressing these gaps requires a multidisciplinary approach that combines advanced materials science with computational modeling. Predictive models incorporating Flory-Huggins theory and kinetic rate equations can help optimize the conditions for stable phase separation and guide the design of stimuli-responsive components. Moreover, the development of standardized calibration protocols for correlative microscopy will be essential to validate the spatial organization and molecular availability within these dynamic compartments. By rigorously mapping fluorescence signals to true nanoscale molecular organization, researchers can ensure that the observed dynamic behaviors accurately reflect the underlying physicochemical processes. Future research must focus on developing fully dissipative systems where synthesis and degradation compete to maintain structure, alongside the exploration of collective behaviors in larger populations to mimic tissue-like organization or evolution. Improving stability and permeability control of hybrid membrane-coacervate interfaces offers a promising pathway to prevent fusion and limit diffusion while preserving internal dynamics. By bridging fundamental physical chemistry with biological functionality, the field of dynamic synthetic cells is poised to elucidate the physicochemical basis of life's emergence and engineer smart, adaptive materials capable of self-repair, environmental sensing, and autonomous operation.

The transition from characterizing native intracellular organization to actively engineering artificial systems marks a pivotal advancement in bottom-up synthetic biology. This section shifts focus to synthetic compartments designed to recapitulate, manipulate, or enhance the structural dynamics of natural biomolecular condensates. By leveraging liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), associative polymer networks, and amphiphilic self-assembly, researchers construct artificial microenvironments that serve as foundational prototypes for protocells and industrial bioreactors. The architectural evolution within this domain is driven by the imperative to resolve spatial heterogeneity, enabling the precise orchestration of biochemical pathways that would otherwise be constrained by diffusion limits or antagonistic cross-reactions in homogeneous solutions. 

Central to this engineering paradigm is the comparative evaluation of functional outcomes across distinct compartmentalization strategies. While direct empirical comparisons between endogenous membrane-bound organelles and phase-separated condensates remain challenging due to inherent biological complexity, synthetic models provide a controlled platform to dissect how physical boundaries and interfacial tensions govern molecular partitioning, catalytic efficiency, and dynamic turnover [15]. Recent developments underscore a clear trajectory away from simple vesicular encapsulation toward hierarchical, multi-compartmental architectures. These advanced designs introduce internal physicochemical gradients and programmable interfaces, facilitating metabolon-like substrate channeling and the simultaneous hosting of incompatible enzymatic cascades. However, realizing fully autonomous synthetic cells necessitates overcoming persistent challenges related to mass-transfer limitations, long-term mechanical stability, and the precise scaling of proximity effects from mesoscopic constructs to nanoscale cellular regimes. 

To systematically address these objectives, the following subsections first examine coacervate-based protocells as dynamic, stimuli-responsive templates that guide membrane assembly and morphological shaping. Subsequently, the discussion advances to multi-compartmental and core-shell architectures, detailing how concentric patterning resolves reaction incompatibilities and amplifies catalytic throughput. Finally, the section evaluates functional integration and catalysis, synthesizing performance metrics that link confinement geometry to enhanced biochemical fluxes and establishing design principles for next-generation artificial bioreactors.

## 4. Engineering Synthetic Compartments

Coacervate droplets have emerged as pivotal platforms in synthetic protobiology, offering chemically diverse, crowded environments that recapitulate key features of intracellular organization while circumventing the rigidity of lipid bilayers. Unlike traditional membrane-bound compartments, liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) driven coacervates provide dynamic, responsive interfaces that facilitate selective partitioning, catalytic reaction centers, and morphological shaping through soft matter mechanics. Recent advances underscore a dual paradigm in the engineering of synthetic cells: coacervates function either as encapsulated organelle-like modules within larger compartments or as cytosol-like templates guiding the self-assembly of membranes and cytoskeletal elements. This subsection synthesizes the design principles, functional capabilities, and structural dynamics of coacervate-based protocells, emphasizing their role as models for early life and fundamental cellular processes. By leveraging the tunability of associative and segregative phase separation, researchers can construct protocells that exhibit emergent behaviors ranging from stimulus-responsive condensation to autonomous shape changes, thereby bridging the gap between abiotic chemistry and biotic complexity.

The versatility of coacervate systems stems from their broad chemical diversity and precisely tunable physicochemical properties, which dictate their compatibility with biological components and their capacity to host biochemical reactions. Systems can be systematically classified into simple coacervation involving single polymer species, complex coacervation driven by electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged polyions, surfactant coacervation utilizing amphiphilic networks, and specific interaction-driven assemblies employing DNA nanostars or protein motifs. Biological condensates further integrate intrinsically disordered proteins and RNA, combining nonspecific multivalent interactions with specific binding motifs to form dynamic compartments. [15] provides a comprehensive taxonomy bridging classical polymer physics with modern synthetic biology, highlighting how factors such as hydration, rheology, and reduced dielectric constants within droplets modulate biochemical reactivity. For instance, the lowered relative permittivity inside oligopeptide/mononucleotide coacervates enhances non-enzymatic reactions like aldol condensations and hydrazone formations due to local ordering and reduced water activity. Simultaneously, macromolecular crowding within the coacervate phase stabilizes proteins and accelerates enzymatic rates through cofactor accumulation and excluded volume effects, although excessively high viscosity may inhibit product diffusion. These material properties—ranging from viscoelastic fluids with tunable relaxation times to gel-like states at low hydration—allow researchers to engineer compartments with tailored permeability, interfacial tension, and mechanical responsiveness, enabling the mimicry of diverse intracellular microenvironments.

Beyond static encapsulation, coacervates serve multifaceted roles in synthetic cell architectures, functioning as functional modules that orchestrate intracellular logistics and catalysis. As organelle analogs, coacervates enable switchable condensation for cargo storage and release, localized enzymatic cascades, and intracellular trafficking. Stimuli-responsive triggers such as light, pH, temperature, and proteolytic enzymes allow controlled uptake and dissolution, mimicking regulatory mechanisms in native cells. For example, azobenzene-functionalized polymers permit light-driven bidirectional transport of molecules between immiscible coacervate phases, while nuclease-triggered degradation of linking RNA strands induces fission in DNA nanostar coacervates, demonstrating primitive division dynamics. Furthermore, coacervates exhibit capillary behaviors, including wetting phenomena that drive hierarchical structuring and membrane remodeling. Interactions with lipid bilayers and cytoskeletal filaments induce deformation and engulfment, suggesting pathways for endocytosis-like processes. The low surface tension of coacervates facilitates extensive interfacial contact with soft surfaces, promoting partial wetting and fingering instabilities that can remodel enclosing membranes. These properties position coacervates as versatile scaffolds for organizing synthetic cellular machinery, where spatial proximity and dynamic exchange can be programmed to achieve specific functional outcomes.

When acting as cytosol templates, coacervates guide the spontaneous assembly of semipermeable membranes via Pickering stabilization by nanoparticles or amphiphile adsorption, creating integrated synthetic cells with minimal external intervention. This templating effect extends to capturing live bacteria and chloroplasts, creating hybrid systems where metabolic activity fuels structural evolution. An exemplary case involves PDDA/ATP coacervates capturing *Escherichia coli* to produce ATP, subsequently driving actin polymerization and amoeboid shape changes. This integration of energy transduction with morphological dynamics illustrates how coacervates can couple metabolic outputs to mechanical behaviors, a hallmark of living cells. The capture process relies on electrostatic attraction and wetting, leading to the retention of small ions and molecules while maintaining defects or pores that allow solute exchange. Such systems demonstrate the potential for coacervates to serve as foundational cytoplasmic matrices that recruit and organize biological components, effectively bootstrapping synthetic cells from simple phase-separated drops to complex, interacting entities. The ability to confine and manipulate organelles within coacervate templates opens avenues for constructing synthetic ecosystems where multiple biological modules cooperate within a unified compartmentalized environment.

A significant leap in protocell complexity arises from enzymatically reconfigurable coacervates, which introduce temporal control over structural transitions and mimic developmental cascades. [16] demonstrates a system comprising carboxymethyl amylose and a cationic surfactant that forms liquid crystalline microdroplets exhibiting onion-like multilamellar structures and birefringence indicative of mesoscopic order. Upon amylase-mediated hydrolysis, these droplets undergo a two-step transformation: intense vacuolization yielding left-handed helicoidal intermediates, followed by expansion and coalescence into hollow coacervate vesicles. This cascade mimics cellular differentiation, where simple physicochemical interactions coupled with enzymatic activity generate life-like architectures. The transition kinetics are finely tuned by substrate concentration and charge screening, revealing a mechanistic link between polysaccharide hydrolysis degree and mesoscale ordering. Such enzyme-responsive designs offer programmable morphogenesis, enabling protocells to evolve from isotropic droplets to structured vesicles in response to biochemical cues. The chirality of the helicoidal intermediates, linked to the inherent chirality of amylose, highlights how molecular asymmetry can propagate to macroscopic morphology, providing insights into the origins of biological handedness and structural patterning in early life forms.

Integrating interactive biomolecules into reconfigurable coacervates yields 'yolk-shell' architectures, featuring an outer coacervate vesicle rim and an inner core housing artificial organelles. In the carboxymethyl amylose/didodecyldimethylammonium bromide system, proteins and nucleic acids preferentially sequester into the inner core due to differential binding affinities, creating distinct microenvironments with varying diffusion coefficients and crowding levels. Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching reveals that molecular mobility in the vesicle rim is significantly higher than in the initial liquid crystalline droplets, while the inner organelle remains more crowded and restrictive. This compartmentalization supports enhanced biochemical reactions; for example, hammerhead ribozyme cleavage proceeds faster within yolk-shell vesicles compared to initial droplets, attributed to higher molecular diffusivity in the isotropic state and optimized local concentrations. The ability to sustain catalytic cycles within nested compartments underscores the potential of coacervates to replicate the spatial heterogeneity and functional specialization observed in eukaryotic cells. By separating reactive species into distinct domains while maintaining connectivity, yolk-shell protocells can regulate reaction pathways, prevent interference, and enhance efficiency, offering a robust testbed for studying the emergence of metabolic channeling and organelle cooperation in prebiotic scenarios.

Despite remarkable progress, several challenges impede the realization of fully autonomous synthetic cells based on coacervates. Precise spatial positioning of coacervates within enclosing membranes remains difficult, and achieving symmetric division of both membrane and internal condensates is unresolved. Integrating incompatible catabolic and anabolic pathways demands advanced compartmentalization strategies to prevent cross-talk and resource depletion. Moreover, powering these systems without relying on living cells necessitates robust artificial metabolic modules capable of sustaining energy fluxes over extended periods. Future research must focus on developing orthogonal interaction sets for independent control of multiple coacervate populations, enabling the construction of multi-compartmental protocells with distinct functionalities. Exploring capillary forces to engineer programmable membrane remodeling and signal transduction represents another promising direction, potentially allowing protocells to sense and respond to environmental stimuli with greater sophistication. Additionally, investigating the supramolecular forces driving chirality and long-term stability under physiological conditions will be critical for translating these models into practical applications. Scaling up production and improving programmability for therapeutic and environmental uses also require interdisciplinary efforts to bridge synthetic chemistry, soft matter physics, and synthetic biology, ensuring that coacervate-based protocells evolve from proof-of-concept demonstrations to functional tools for biotechnology and medicine.

### 4.1. Multi-Compartmental and Core-Shell Architectures

The transition from homogeneous encapsulation to hierarchical compartmentalization marks a pivotal advancement in the engineering of synthetic protocells and bioreactors. While initial efforts focused on simple vesicular or particulate carriers to protect payloads or sequester reactive species, the functional demands of complex biochemical workflows necessitate architectures capable of spatially resolving incompatible processes, facilitating metabolic channeling, and modulating local microenvironments. Multi-compartmental and core-shell designs emerge as critical solutions to these challenges, offering tunable degrees of heterogeneity that mimic the organizational sophistication of biological organelles and biomolecular condensates. By introducing internal boundaries and distinct physicochemical domains within a single particle, engineers can decouple antagonistic reactions, concentrate intermediates, and create localized reaction hubs that significantly enhance catalytic turnover and pathway efficiency. This architectural evolution reflects a broader shift in synthetic biology toward bottom-up reconstruction of cellular logic, where structural complexity is directly coupled to functional output.

A paradigmatic illustration of this approach is found in the development of core-shell hydrogel particles designed for incompatible tandem enzymatic cascades. Such systems leverage the physical segregation of enzymes within distinct concentric domains to manage reaction fluxes that would otherwise lead to catalyst inhibition or product degradation in a bulk solution. For instance, in a glucose oxidase/horseradish peroxidase (GOX/HRP) cascade, the oxidation of glucose by GOX generates hydrogen peroxide as an intermediate, which can be detrimental to numerous biological catalysts due to its oxidative toxicity. However, HRP efficiently consumes hydrogen peroxide to drive subsequent transformations. By confining GOX and HRP within separate yet proximate regions of a core-shell hydrogel architecture, the system achieves a synergistic coupling where the toxic intermediate is rapidly channeled from the source compartment to the sink compartment before it can diffuse away or damage the surrounding milieu. This proximity effect fundamentally alters the reaction kinetics by increasing the effective local concentration of intermediates and reducing the diffusion-limited losses associated with open systems. Experimental evaluations of such core-shell hydrogel particles have demonstrated remarkable performance gains, reporting catalytic activity enhancements on the order of twenty-three-fold compared to non-segregated controls. This amplification underscores the efficacy of spatial organization in overcoming thermodynamic and kinetic bottlenecks, effectively transforming a linear pathway prone to inhibition into a robust, self-sustaining microreactor. Moreover, the hydrogel matrix itself contributes to the system's stability by providing a rigid scaffold that retains enzyme integrity and minimizes leakage, thereby ensuring sustained operation over extended periods. The low release rates characteristic of these dense polymer networks further protect encapsulated components from external proteolytic or denaturing agents, highlighting a key advantage of synthetic hydrogels over more permeable biological membranes in harsh operational environments.

To appreciate the position of core-shell hydrogels within the broader landscape of synthetic compartmentalization, it is essential to compare them against diverse architectural paradigms ranging from lipid-based vesicles to polymeric capsules and hybrid assemblies. Comprehensive surveys of compartmentalized biomimetic micro- and nanocontainers reveal a rich taxonomy of structures, each balancing trade-offs between biomimicry, mechanical robustness, fabrication complexity, and functional versatility. Lipid vesicles, including small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs), large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs), and giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs), offer unparalleled fidelity to natural cell membranes, facilitating the integration of membrane proteins, ion channels, and signaling machinery. However, their thin bilayer structures are inherently fragile, susceptible to osmotic shock, and often exhibit limited stability in non-physiological conditions. In contrast, polymeric containers, such as polymersomes and hydrogel beads, provide superior mechanical strength and chemical resistance, albeit at the cost of reduced biomimetic functionality and potentially restricted permeability for larger substrates. Core-shell hydrogels occupy a unique niche within this spectrum, combining the structural resilience of polymers with the capacity for precise internal patterning. Unlike simple solid hydrogel beads, core-shell geometries introduce an additional degree of freedom by allowing differential loading and independent tuning of the core and shell properties, such as mesh size, charge density, and ligand presentation.

Beyond simple core-shell designs, the field encompasses more intricate multi-compartmental architectures, such as multicompartment vesicles (MCVs) and multisomes. MCVs consist of multiple hemifused vesicles sharing a common lipid bilayer, enabling communication between distinct aqueous compartments via transmembrane pores while maintaining sharp chemical gradients. This architecture supports even longer enzymatic cascades, potentially involving three or more sequential steps, by segregating each reaction stage into its own dedicated volume. Similarly, double emulsions stabilized by amphiphilic lipids, known as multisomes, can encapsulate multiple inner droplets within a continuous outer phase, offering templates for complex multiphase chemistry. These advanced structures highlight the scalability of compartmentalization principles, where adding layers of hierarchy enables increasingly sophisticated control over reaction networks. The fabrication of such architectures has also evolved, with a marked transition from bulk methods like electroformation and extrusion toward high-throughput microfluidic techniques. Microfluidics offers exceptional precision in controlling particle size, content asymmetry, and encapsulation efficiency, enabling the generation of femtoliter-scale systems with monodisperse characteristics. Nevertheless, scaling down to nanoscale dimensions while maintaining compatibility with sensitive biological components remains a technical bottleneck, particularly when dealing with lipid-coated droplets that may interact unfavorably with surface-modified channels.

Functional integration extends beyond passive structural design to encompass dynamic regulation and programmable behavior. Advances in functionalizing synthetic compartments include the incorporation of stimuli-responsive gates, light-activated pores, and cell-free expression systems for on-demand protein synthesis. For example, the integration of in vitro transcription-translation (IVTT) machinery within droplets or vesicles allows for the autonomous production of membrane proteins or enzymes upon induction, mimicking the gene expression capabilities of living cells. This capability transforms static containers into adaptive microreactors capable of responding to external cues or internal feedback loops. Additionally, the strategic placement of engineered protein pores, such as alpha-hemolysin or mechanically gated channels, facilitates controlled trans-compartment transport, addressing the challenge of communication across double bilayers in nested systems. These functionalization strategies underscore the convergence of materials science, fluid dynamics, and synthetic biology in creating artificial compartments that not only house biochemical reactions but also participate in information processing and environmental sensing.

Despite these impressive achievements, several critical limitations and open questions persist in the engineering of multi-compartmental and core-shell architectures. A primary concern is the scale discrepancy between synthetic constructs and native cellular components. Many reported core-shell hydrogel particles operate at mesoscopic scales, with diameters around three hundred micrometers, which is orders of magnitude larger than typical organelles or biomolecular condensates. At this scale, diffusion timescales are significantly prolonged, and the enhancement factors attributed to proximity effects may not translate directly to nanoscale regimes where diffusion is faster and thermal fluctuations dominate. Consequently, the mechanistic insights derived from macroscopic models require careful calibration when extrapolated to cellular contexts. Furthermore, the chemical complexity of synthetic hydrogels differs markedly from the supramolecular assemblies of lipid-protein condensates driven by liquid-liquid phase separation. While hydrogels provide stable scaffolds, they lack the dynamic reversibility and compositional plasticity characteristic of native phase-separated droplets, which can rapidly assemble, disassemble, and exchange components in response to cellular signals. Bridging this gap may require hybrid approaches that combine the robustness of synthetic polymers with the responsiveness of intrinsically disordered proteins or RNA-based condensates.

Another challenge lies in the standardization of performance metrics and comparative benchmarking. Current literature often reports relative improvements within specific systems using tailored assays, making it difficult to establish universal design rules or rank architectures based on objective criteria. A systematic comparison of core-shell hydrogels, MCVs, and DIBs performing identical reaction cascades under matched conditions would yield valuable insights into the optimal balance between compartment size, membrane permeability, and interfacial area. Such benchmarks could guide the rational design of next-generation compartments optimized for industrial biocatalysis, therapeutics, or diagnostic applications. Additionally, the long-term stability and energy sustainability of synthetic systems remain unresolved issues. Most current designs rely on finite encapsulated substrates or external fuel supplies, lacking the ability to harvest energy continuously from the environment. Developing integrated power sources, such as photoactive pigments or redox couples, could enable autonomous operation and extend the operational lifetime of artificial compartments.

Looking forward, the trajectory of research in multi-compartmental and core-shell architectures points toward greater integration, automation, and biological interfacing. Emerging nanofabrication technologies, including 3D printing and optical trapping, promise to democratize access to complex compartment designs and enable the rapid prototyping of customized reactors. The integration of DNA origami structures to mediate precise positioning of enzymes or to bridge double membranes represents a promising avenue for achieving nanoscale precision in synthetic assembly. Moreover, the interface between synthetic compartments and living cells holds immense potential for hybrid therapies and biohybrid devices. Encapsulating therapeutic enzymes or sensors within protective core-shell particles could shield them from immune clearance while allowing selective interaction with target tissues. Conversely, interfacing synthetic microreactors with neuronal networks or tissue constructs could facilitate novel modes of communication and control, paving the way for intelligent bioelectronic systems. Ultimately, the continued refinement of multi-compartmental architectures will depend on a deeper understanding of the physical principles governing confinement, proximity, and phase behavior, as well as the development of robust methodologies to characterize these systems at the relevant spatiotemporal scales. By addressing these challenges, the field can advance from proof-of-concept demonstrations to practical applications that harness the power of engineered heterogeneity to solve complex chemical and biological problems.

### 4.2. Functional Integration and Catalysis

The evaluation of synthetic compartments through the lens of functional integration represents a critical juncture in the advancement of bottom-up synthetic biology and engineered biocatalysis. Historically, compartmentalization was viewed primarily as a physical strategy for sequestering molecular cargo, isolating reactive species from hostile external environments, or facilitating the delivery of therapeutic agents. However, contemporary research has fundamentally reoriented this perspective, recognizing that engineered microenvironments serve as active catalytic hubs capable of orchestrating complex biochemical networks with precision rivaling native cellular organelles. This paradigm shift necessitates a rigorous assessment of synthetic systems not merely by their morphological fidelity or material composition, but by their biochemical performance in executing targeted catalytic tasks. The transition from passive encapsulation to dynamic, functionally integrated bioreactors relies on exploiting the unique physicochemical properties of confined spaces to modulate reaction kinetics, enhance molecular availability, and protect labile intermediates. By deliberately engineering the spatial organization of enzymes, cofactors, and substrates within synthetic boundaries, researchers can recapitulate the functional advantages of biomolecular condensates, including localized concentration gradients, suppressed off-target interactions, and accelerated metabolic flux. Consequently, understanding the interplay between compartment architecture and catalytic output becomes essential for designing next-generation artificial cells and industrial biocatalysts that operate efficiently under physiologically relevant or industrially demanding conditions.

A central mechanism driving the superior performance of synthetic compartments is the exploitation of confinement effects to amplify catalytic efficiency. In conventional dilute aqueous solutions, enzymatic reactions are inherently constrained by stochastic collision frequencies, rapid substrate diffusion away from active sites, and the thermodynamic penalties associated with unfavorable binding equilibria. When enzymes are densely packed within synthetic compartments, the effective reduction in reaction volume dramatically increases the local concentration of reactants, thereby shifting the kinetic regime toward higher turnover rates. Empirical evaluations of such confined systems have consistently demonstrated remarkable performance gains that surpass the additive capabilities of individual components. Notably, investigations utilizing core-shell hydrogel particles for tandem enzymatic cascades have reported activity enhancements exceeding twenty-threefold compared to unconfined, homogeneous counterparts ([17]). This substantial amplification is mechanistically attributed to optimized proximity effects and substrate channeling, wherein the intermediate product generated by the initial catalytic step is immediately captured by the subsequent enzyme without equilibrating with the bulk solvent. Such spatial orchestration effectively minimizes diffusion losses, prevents intermediate degradation, and mimics the metabolon-like assemblies observed in native metabolic pathways. By enforcing a controlled dimensionality on molecular encounters, synthetic compartments transform inefficient, diffusion-limited reactions into highly coordinated, sequential processes, validating the hypothesis that engineered spatial organization can transcend the kinetic bottlenecks inherent in traditional batch reactors.

Despite the clear advantages of spatial confinement, the practical deployment of synthetic compartments is governed by a fundamental dichotomy between permeability and stability. This trade-off lies at the heart of compartment design, dictating both short-term catalytic throughput and long-term operational viability. High permeability is theoretically desirable for enabling rapid substrate influx and efficient product efflux, which directly correlates with maximal reaction velocities. However, overly porous barriers frequently result in the progressive leaching of encapsulated catalysts, leading to a decay in active enzyme concentration and eventual collapse of the compartment's structural integrity. Conversely, architectures that prioritize mechanical and chemical stability through dense cross-linking, tight lipid packing, or rigid polymeric networks often impose severe mass transport limitations. These restrictive barriers can starve internal enzymes of necessary precursors, cause the accumulation of inhibitory products, and ultimately suppress catalytic activity despite high initial loading densities. Recent analyses of low-release-rate systems have underscored the necessity of tuning barrier properties to establish a sustainable mass-transfer equilibrium. By engineering matrices that selectively restrict macromolecular egress while maintaining sufficient diffusivity for small-molecule substrates and cofactors, synthetic compartments can preserve high catalytic densities over extended operational windows. This balance is particularly critical for continuous-flow bioprocessing and long-duration biosensing applications, where the premature loss of biological machinery would otherwise terminate functional output. Achieving this equilibrium requires a nuanced understanding of pore-size distribution, mesh elasticity, and solute-matrix interactions, highlighting the multidisciplinary nature of modern compartment engineering.

Beyond single-enzyme encapsulation, advanced synthetic compartments are increasingly architected to host multi-step pathways and even chemically incompatible reactions simultaneously. The spatial segregation of antagonistic processes within distinct hierarchical domains prevents cross-reactivity, maximizes overall pathway yield, and enables the implementation of complex metabolic networks that mirror cellular organization. Core-shell, Janus, and multilayered droplet configurations exemplify this strategy, allowing for the independent optimization of internal and external microenvironments to suit specific enzymatic requirements. ## 5. Advanced Imaging and Structural Characterization

The transition of cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) from a qualitative imaging modality to a cornerstone of in-situ structural cell biology represents a fundamental paradigm shift in resolving biomolecular organization within native cellular environments. Central to this transformation is the integration of cryogenic focused ion beam (FIB) milling, which overcomes the inherent thickness limitations of vitrified specimens that previously restricted high-resolution tomographic reconstruction to peripheral cellular regions or thin prokaryotic cells. By fabricating thin lamellae ranging from 50 to 300 nm directly from intact, frozen-hydrated mammalian cells and tissues, cryo-FIB milling preserves macromolecular complexes in their unperturbed physiological state, thereby bridging the resolution gap between single-particle cryo-EM and low-resolution light microscopy. Recent iterations of this workflow, as highlighted in comprehensive reviews of the field, have incorporated automated fabrication protocols such as AutoTEM, Autolamella, and SmartFIB to increase throughput, alongside micro-expansion joints designed to mitigate grid-induced cracking during milling. Furthermore, the adoption of argon plasma FIB milling has emerged as a critical advancement, significantly reducing the ion-damage layer depth compared to traditional gallium-based systems and allowing deeper penetration into thicker lamellae while maintaining structural integrity. These hardware enhancements establish a robust foundation for capturing the spatial heterogeneity of crowded cellular landscapes, enabling the visualization of diverse biological structures such as sarcomere filaments, viral double-membrane vesicle pore complexes, and proteasome assemblies with unprecedented contextual fidelity. Importantly, in-situ observations frequently reveal structural states that diverge from those obtained via isolation; for instance, the architecture of nebulin within sarcomeres differs markedly from isolated preparations, underscoring the necessity of native-state imaging to accurately characterize functional conformations. The utility of these lamellae is further amplified by correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) strategies, which employ super-resolution cryo-localization techniques to precisely target regions of interest, effectively solving the "needle in a haystack" problem inherent to screening vitrified samples.

A persistent challenge in in-situ cryo-ET is the trade-off between field of view (FOV) and resolution, particularly when aiming to capture large-scale architectural features of whole cells or extensive tissue regions. Correlative cryogenic montage electron tomography emerges as a sophisticated solution to this limitation, extending the effective FOV without compromising the nanoscale resolution required for molecular identification. This approach adapts montage principles to frozen-hydrated specimens by employing image-shift acquisition combined with global translational shifts along spiral trajectories, which optimally distributes the electron dose across overlapping tile regions to mitigate radiation damage. Advanced simulation tools, such as TomoGrapher, facilitate the optimization of tiling patterns and dose accumulation prior to data collection, ensuring efficient coverage while minimizing Fresnel fringe artifacts that typically affect the outer edges of the illumination field. Experimental validations demonstrate that strategic overlap parameters—typically 15–20% along the fringe-affected axis and 10% perpendicular to it—enable seamless, automated stitching of tilt series up to high tilt angles, yielding large-scale 3D reconstructions of complex cellular architectures. Notably, the flexibility of this workflow supports both fully stitched montages for contextual overview and individual tile sub-tomogram averaging, achieving resolutions comparable to conventional methods while preserving critical spatial relationships among organelles and macromolecular machines. For example, this methodology has successfully captured the ultrastructure of budding respiratory syncytial virus virions spanning several micrometers and mapped the arrangement of mitochondria and Golgi apparatus near the nucleus in fibroblast lamellae. Comparative analyses of sub-tomogram averaging performed on fully stitched fields of view versus stitch-free zones reveal equivalent resolution outcomes, confirming that montage acquisition does not inherently degrade local structural quality, although rotational shift strategies have been observed to cause stitching failures at higher tilts, necessitating strict translational-only protocols.

Beyond expanding spatial coverage, the throughput bottleneck associated with serial tilt-series acquisition poses a significant constraint on the statistical power of in-situ structural biology, particularly given the limited number of lamellae that can be prepared from precious biological samples. Parallel cryo electron tomography (PACE-tomo) addresses this efficiency gap by leveraging beam image shifts to acquire multiple tilt series concurrently on a single FIB-milled lamella. By implementing a precise geometric model of the lamella's pretilt and position relative to the tilt axis, PACE-tomo predicts and compensates for specimen movement during off-axis imaging, eliminating the need for large tracking areas that would otherwise consume valuable sample volume. Iterative error correction algorithms further refine eucentricity and defocus control, allowing rapid data collection with minimal drift settling and autofocus overhead. Benchmarks indicate that this method can reduce acquisition times to under five minutes per tilt series on state-of-the-art microscopes, while maintaining sub-nanometer resolution, as evidenced by high-fidelity reconstructions of ribosomal complexes both in vitro and within the crowded cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. The ability to multiplex targets across a lamella maximizes data yield per preparation step, making high-resolution in-situ structural determination more feasible for lower-throughput workflows and broadening the applicability of cryo-ET to diverse biological questions requiring robust sampling. However, the efficacy of parallel acquisition is contingent upon accurate geometric modeling; inaccuracies in pretilt estimation can lead to defocus errors and loss of field of view, and side-entry holders have exhibited aberrant behaviors such as backlash, requiring separate parameter sets for positive and negative tilt branches. Additionally, radiation damage remains a concern when neighboring beam spots overlap at high tilt angles, potentially limiting the maximum number of parallel targets that can be imaged without degrading adjacent regions.

The evolution toward quantitative cryo-ET necessitates a holistic integration of hardware innovations with advanced computational pipelines capable of extracting meaningful biophysical parameters from noisy, incomplete datasets. Modern quantitative frameworks rely on three core pillars: denoising algorithms to enhance signal-to-noise ratios, segmentation tools to delineate cellular geometries, and template matching strategies to localize and count macromolecules. Machine learning approaches, including Noise2Noise-based denoisers like Topaz-Denoise and inpainting networks such as IsoNet that address the missing wedge, have shown promise in recovering structural details obscured by low electron doses. However, the application of these algorithms requires careful calibration to avoid introducing artificial biases, particularly when performing structure determination versus exploratory segmentation, as over-aggressive denoising may manipulate underlying signals. Concurrently, segmentation tools ranging from semi-automatic thresholding to convolutional neural network-based pipelines enable the spatial localization of organelles and membranes, facilitating the measurement of structural features such as membrane curvature and filament populations. Template matching algorithms, often implemented in packages like Dynamo and supported by deep-learning pattern mining tools, allow for the identification of known complexes within tomograms, although they remain susceptible to template bias and computational expense. Case studies illustrate the power of quantitative analysis, such as characterizing mitochondrial division stages through membrane constriction measurements or quantifying endoplasmic reticulum scarcity correlating with ribosome distribution during axon growth. Despite these advances, the "thickness problem" persists as a limiting factor, where electron scattering and dose constraints restrict the maximum observable volume, often mandating thinned lamellae that may exclude relevant cellular contexts. Researchers must navigate a decision matrix weighing sample thickness, resolution requirements, and dataset size to determine the appropriateness of cryo-ET for specific inquiries, acknowledging that while high-voltage electron microscopy offers alternatives for thicker samples, its extreme cost and rarity limit widespread adoption.

Synthesizing these advancements reveals a convergent trajectory in cryo-ET methodology aimed at reconciling the competing demands of resolution, coverage, and throughput. While correlative montage tomography excels in mapping expansive cellular architectures, its elongated acquisition times contrast with the rapid multiplexing capabilities of parallel acquisition schemes, suggesting that hybrid strategies combining stage shifts and beam shifts may offer optimal performance for ultra-large fields of view. Similarly, the reliance on FIB milling introduces procedural complexity and potential artifacts, underscoring the value of complementary techniques like cryo-lift-out for specific multicellular organisms, despite their current low success rates and high time costs. The computational landscape mirrors this hardware diversification, with specialized tools emerging for each stage of the pipeline, yet a unified, fully automated workflow integrating correlative light and electron microscopy targeting, milling, acquisition, and analysis remains an aspirational goal. Current implementations often operate as modular components rather than cohesive ecosystems, limiting end-to-end efficiency and increasing the risk of user-induced variability. Moreover, while template matching facilitates the localization of abundant complexes, the identification of rare species or conformational heterogeneity continues to challenge automated detection, highlighting the need for improved pattern mining and deep-learning classifiers trained on diverse, annotated datasets. Future developments point toward the incorporation of AI-driven structure prediction models, such as AlphaFold, into integrative workflows, potentially revolutionizing the identification of novel complexes and dynamic states within the native cellular milieu. The realization of "visual proteomics"—mapping hundreds of macromolecules across the proteome in situ—depends on sustained efforts to standardize data deposition, refine algorithmic robustness, and develop multimodal calibration protocols that validate fluorescent proxies against EM ground truth. As these technologies mature, cryo-ET is poised to deliver a holistic understanding of cellular function, moving beyond descriptive snapshots to quantitative, mechanistic insights into the structural dynamics of biomolecular condensates and synthetic compartments in their native crowding environments.

### 5.1. Correlative Microscopy and Calibration Gaps

The paradigm shift toward understanding intracellular organization has necessitated a decisive move beyond single-modality bioimaging, driving the rapid development of Correlated Multimodal Imaging (CMI) frameworks that seamlessly bridge disparate spatial and temporal scales. By integrating complementary techniques, researchers can now dynamically link functional processes captured via light microscopy (LM) with the exquisite ultrastructural context provided by electron microscopy (EM). This synergistic approach fundamentally expands the biomedical horizon, allowing investigators to correlate specific molecular events with their precise nanoscale architectural environment in ways that isolated modalities cannot achieve. However, the transition from rudimentary dual-modality setups to fully integrated, high-throughput pipelines remains heavily constrained by inherent methodological bottlenecks. While the conceptual framework for CMI is well-established and widely recognized, the practical execution demands rigorous attention to sample handling, region-of-interest (ROI) relocation, and sophisticated data correlation. The integration of LM directly within EM platforms represents a significant technological leap, yet it simultaneously exposes critical gaps in calibration standards and evaluation criteria that must be systematically addressed to ensure the long-term validity and reproducibility of multimodal proxies.

A primary advancement in addressing these pervasive workflow complexities is the development of integrated hardware solutions that drastically minimize intermediate sample transfers. Traditional correlative workflows frequently require multiple cryo-transfers between separate microscopes, introducing substantial risks of sample thawing, contamination, and ice deposition, all of which can severely compromise structural integrity and biological relevance. This architectural integration elegantly eliminates the physical separation between optical and electron imaging modalities, enabling rapid and highly accurate targeting of specific cellular regions identified via fluorescence prior to cryo-FIB milling. By facilitating real-time verification of lamella preparation success, this integrated design significantly enhances the safety, precision, and overall efficiency of cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) studies. The system's demonstrated capability to perform seamless navigation and correlation without relying on complex post-acquisition alignment underscores the immense potential of hybrid hardware to streamline previously arduous biological investigations, particularly for targeting rare cellular events or specific protein complexes within highly heterogeneous tissues.

Despite these impressive hardware advancements, the heavy reliance on fluorescence signals as proxies for true nanoscale molecular organization introduces profound and often underappreciated calibration challenges. A central concern in modern correlative microscopy is the fundamental validity of these proxies; while LM provides unparalleled dynamic and molecular specificity, its resolution is inherently limited by the diffraction barrier, rendering it entirely insufficient for resolving fine structural details at the nanoscale. Consequently, accurately mapping fluorescent signals to the dense macromolecular architecture visualized via EM requires exceptionally robust calibration strategies. Fiducial markers, such as quantum dots, gold nanoparticles, or carefully selected endogenous landmarks, serve as crucial geometric anchors for aligning multimodal datasets, yet their introduction into biological systems can sometimes alter native functions or suffer from severe signal quenching. However, even these minute drifts and residual errors can critically compromise the fidelity of the correlation, highlighting the urgent need for continuous monitoring and adaptive correction mechanisms during acquisition.

For example, custom interfaces like PIE-scope-commander utilize Python scripts to calculate transformations, achieving approximately 500 nm residual error in mechanical correlation, which remains compatible with external 3DCT packages for comprehensive volumetric analysis. However, as the complexity of biological samples increases—ranging from simple model organisms like yeast to intricate neural networks in Drosophila larvae—the demand for highly sophisticated non-rigid registration algorithms grows exponentially. Current approaches frequently rely on feature-based registration and specialized visualization tools to fuse images, but the widespread lack of standardized formats and universal ontologies severely hinders the reproducibility and scalability of these workflows. The absence of unified imaging metadata standards means that correlating multiscale, volumetric data across different platforms remains a highly labor-intensive process, inherently susceptible to human error and inconsistent parameter tuning. This systemic fragmentation underscores the urgent need for community-driven initiatives aimed at establishing common data structures and interoperable software ecosystems that can efficiently handle the diverse and massive datasets generated by modern CMI pipelines. Emerging technologies, such as TimePix3 photon-counting detectors offering high speed and energy resolution, or short-wave infrared (SWIR) imaging providing deeper penetration with less autofluorescence, present exciting avenues for reducing radiation doses and improving signal-to-noise ratios, yet their integration into standardized correlative workflows remains largely unexplored.

Beyond technical implementation, a significant and often overlooked gap exists in the establishment of formal evaluation criteria for correlative microscopy workflows. As multimodal imaging becomes increasingly prevalent across diverse biological disciplines, there is a pressing need to define quantitative benchmarks that rigorously assess the accuracy, precision, and reliability of cross-modal registration. Without standardized metrics, it is exceedingly difficult to objectively compare the performance of different hardware configurations or software algorithms, thereby impeding the systematic optimization of CMI protocols. Moreover, the validation of cryo-fluorescence and cryo-ET proxies requires rigorous experimental designs that can effectively disentangle artifacts introduced by sample preparation from genuine biological phenomena. Addressing this gap necessitates the development of design-only research strategies that can simulate expected outcomes under strictly controlled conditions, thereby providing a reliable baseline against which experimental data can be evaluated. Specifically, the field must confront the reality that many current pipelines operate as black boxes, where the intermediate steps of image fusion and landmark matching are rarely subjected to rigorous statistical validation. Establishing transparent evaluation criteria would not only enhance trust in correlative findings but also facilitate the comparison of results across different laboratories and instrument types.

Deep learning models hold the immense promise of overcoming traditional limitations by predicting missing structural information or enhancing resolution across modalities, effectively bridging the persistent gap between functional dynamics and ultrastructure. ### 5.2. Limitations in Resolution and Coverage

The contemporary landscape of biomolecular condensate research has been fundamentally reshaped by the advent of high-resolution cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) and correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) techniques. These methodologies have transitioned the field from indirect biochemical inference to direct, nanoscale visualization of spatial organization within near-native cellular environments. A significant portion of this empirical progress has been dedicated to elucidating the localization, clustering, and dynamic turnover of proteins within phase-separated compartments. Advanced tomographic reconstruction pipelines, combined with focused ion beam (FIB) lamellae preparation, have successfully resolved mesoscale protein architectures, revealing how intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) and folded domains coalesce to form functional hubs. For instance, detailed investigations into bacterial division machinery have demonstrated how proteins such as FtsZ undergo condensation to establish polar growth zones and septal rings, providing a clear blueprint for protein-centric spatial organization [3]. Similarly, the regulatory interplay between stabilizing and destabilizing factors, such as the ZapA-SlmA network, has been mapped to show how proteins switch between polymeric filaments and condensed liquid-like droplets in response to cellular cues [4]. These protein-focused observations have established a robust baseline for understanding how macromolecular crowding drives demixing, how diffusion constraints alter local reaction kinetics, and how structural heterogeneity emerges within the crowded cytoplasm.

Despite these remarkable advances in protein localization mapping, a critical asymmetry persists in the empirical coverage of other essential macromolecular classes. While the spatial distribution of proteins across various resolution regimes is increasingly well-documented, the corresponding organizational states of lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids remain profoundly underrepresented in direct observational datasets. This coverage gap is not merely a matter of historical research bias; it stems from fundamental biophysical and methodological constraints inherent to current structural imaging modalities. Proteins generally possess higher electron scattering cross-sections due to their dense amino acid packing and predictable folding geometries, making them more readily identifiable in tomographic slices and fluorescence correlation maps. In contrast, lipids exhibit lower mass density and greater conformational flexibility, often blending into the background noise of cryo-ET reconstructions unless specifically enriched or stained. Carbohydrates, frequently presented as glycosylation modifications or extracellular matrix components, suffer from even weaker contrast and rapid radiation damage during electron beam exposure, severely limiting their detectability in high-resolution in situ contexts. Nucleic acids, while structurally rigid, present unique challenges in crowded condensates where they compete with proteins for binding sites and may adopt transient, heterogeneous conformations that evade standard classification algorithms. Consequently, the current empirical inventory of cellular organization is heavily skewed toward protein scaffolds, leaving the precise spatial arrangement and phase behavior of non-protein constituents largely inferred rather than directly visualized.

The technical bottlenecks exacerbating this macromolecular coverage gap are multifaceted and span sample preparation, imaging physics, and data processing pipelines. Cryo-fixation protocols, while essential for preserving native hydration states, can induce preferential vitrification artifacts that disproportionately affect lipid bilayers and carbohydrate-rich matrices, leading to structural collapse or redistribution prior to imaging. Furthermore, the reliance on fluorescent protein fusions for correlative light microscopy introduces potential perturbations to native phase separation thermodynamics. Large fluorophore tags can alter the valency, hydrophobicity, and solubility parameters of target molecules, thereby shifting the saturation concentration thresholds required for condensation. When researchers attempt to track non-protein components, they often resort to inert tracers or small-molecule dyes that report on bulk solvent properties rather than specific molecular localization. Such approaches, while useful for monitoring general viscosity changes or diffusion barriers, fail to resolve the precise stoichiometric relationships and multivalent interaction networks that define true compartmentalization [2]. The absence of site-specific, minimally invasive labeling strategies for lipids and glycans means that their contribution to the cohesive forces driving LLPS remains obscured. Without direct observational evidence mapping these molecules to specific sub-compartmental niches, our understanding of condensate composition risks being reduced to a protein-centric abstraction that neglects the synergistic roles of membrane-derived lipids and sugar-modified polymers.

In the absence of comprehensive empirical data, the field has increasingly turned to synthetic and proxy models to extrapolate the behavior of understudied macromolecules. Coacervate-based protocells and simplified peptide mixtures have served as valuable testbeds for exploring the physicochemical principles of demixing, offering insights into how charge patterning, solvent quality, and ionic strength govern phase transitions. Studies utilizing synthetic peptide coacervates have successfully demonstrated how mixing distinct polypeptide chains can stabilize liquid droplets against rigid fibril formation, highlighting the delicate balance between entropy and enthalpy in condensation processes [10]. However, while these bottom-up reconstituted systems provide mechanistic clarity, they inherently lack the architectural complexity, metabolic channeling, and stochastic fluctuations present in living cells. Proxy models often isolate single variables—such as electrostatic screening or crowding agent concentration—at the expense of the multivariate reality of the cytoplasm. Consequently, findings derived from synthetic analogs cannot be directly scaled to predict the spatial organization of lipids and nucleic acids within native condensates. The gap between simplified in vitro reconstitutions and in vivo complexity underscores a critical limitation: we currently lack a unified observational framework that captures how diverse macromolecular classes co-partition, compete, or cooperatively assemble within the same nanoscale volume. This disconnect threatens to perpetuate fragmented models of cellular organization, where protein condensates are studied in isolation from the lipid rafts, glycocalyx networks, and nucleic acid scaffolds that likely modulate their material properties and biological functions.

To address these persistent empirical voids, computational modeling must evolve from a supplementary analytical tool to a central pillar of structural characterization. The inherent sparsity of direct observational data for lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids creates an ill-posed inverse problem that cannot be resolved by imaging alone. Computational approaches offer a pathway to integrate disparate, low-resolution signals into coherent, multiscale representations of condensate architecture. Coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CGMD) simulations, parameterized with experimentally derived interaction potentials, can predict the spatial distribution and partitioning coefficients of non-protein macromolecules within protein-rich droplets. By simulating thousands of possible configurations under varying crowding conditions, these models can identify energetically favorable localization patterns that guide targeted experimental validation. Furthermore, machine learning-driven density completion algorithms can leverage the high-fidelity protein maps obtained via cryo-ET to infer the probable locations of lower-contrast components, effectively hallucinating structurally consistent lipid and nucleic acid arrangements based on learned biophysical constraints. Thermodynamic integration frameworks can also quantify the free energy contributions of carbohydrate modifications or lipid tail unsaturation to overall condensate stability, translating abstract chemical properties into tangible spatial outcomes.

Additionally, active learning pipelines can be designed to iteratively refine computational models by identifying which specific macromolecular parameters, when measured experimentally, would most significantly reduce uncertainty in predicted condensate architecture. This feedback loop ensures that future imaging efforts are strategically directed toward the most informative observables, maximizing the return on investment for resource-intensive cryo-ET and super-resolution microscopy campaigns. Looking forward, bridging the resolution and coverage divide will demand methodological convergence across structural biology, biophysics, and computational science. ## 6. Future Directions and Conclusion

The paradigm shift from viewing the cellular interior as a homogeneous dilute solution to recognizing it as a highly crowded, structured environment fundamentally alters our understanding of biomolecular organization. Central to this reconceptualization is the distinction between classical membrane-bound organelles and emerging biomolecular condensates formed via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). While membrane-bound compartments rely on lipid bilayers to establish impermeable barriers that strictly regulate molecular flux, phase-separated condensates utilize multivalent, often transient interactions among intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) and RNA to create distinct physicochemical microenvironments without discrete boundaries. This dichotomy is increasingly recognized as a spectrum rather than a binary classification, as membrane surfaces can act as nucleation sites for condensates, and synthetic systems can bridge these modalities by coating droplets with amphiphilic layers. Comparative evaluations of these organizational mechanisms reveal divergent strategies for controlling molecular localization, condensation, and availability. Membrane-bound organelles typically sequester components to isolate reactions or maintain concentration gradients, whereas phase-separated condensates concentrate reactants to enhance reaction rates while permitting rapid exchange with the surrounding milieu. However, assessing the functional equivalence of these systems presents significant challenges. The lack of standardized conditions and the artificial nature of most engineered constructs limit the generalizability of synthetic outcomes to physiological contexts. For instance, while synthetic coacervates demonstrate remarkable utility in encapsulating enzymes and enhancing reaction rates, their scalability and stability profiles do not always align with the regulatory precision and dynamic adaptability observed in native cells. Qualitative synthesis indicates that although both mechanisms achieve spatial segregation, the extent to which they support complex functional coordination differs significantly due to permeability constraints versus the kinetic trapping often encountered in simplified synthetic models [19].

At the core of both native crowding and synthetic engineering lie convergent physical principles centered on demixing, proximity, and tunable stability. The thermodynamics of demixing are governed by the delicate balance between the entropic penalty of confinement and the enthalpic gains derived from multivalent interactions. In native systems, this balance is exquisitely sensitive to environmental perturbations such as pH, temperature, ionic strength, and ATP levels, enabling cells to rapidly assemble or dissolve condensates in response to metabolic demands or stress signals. Synthetic bioengineering has harnessed these principles to design responsive materials capable of mimicking biological complexity. Systems utilizing electrostatic interactions between polyelectrolytes, such as oleate and PDDA, can be modulated to alter droplet morphology and stability, demonstrating that external stimuli can drive reversible transitions between dispersed and condensed states [11]. Furthermore, the integration of lipid components onto coacervate surfaces can induce multi-layered architectures, effectively creating protocells with properties intermediate between pure liquid droplets and membrane vesicles. A critical aspect of compartment stability is the transition from metastable liquid phases to solid-like gels or amyloid fibrils, which can compromise functionality. Mechanistic analyses suggest that preventing ordered nucleation through competitive binding or the mixing of peptides can stabilize liquid states, offering a strategy to engineer robust synthetic compartments that resist pathological aggregation. For example, binary peptide coacervates have been shown to stabilize liquid droplets against rigid fibril formation by hindering the nucleation of ordered structures, thereby preserving the dynamic nature essential for function [10]. These findings underscore that stability is not merely a static attribute but a tunable parameter dictated by the valency, interaction strength, and sequence composition of the constituent molecules, a principle that unifies the design of both natural and artificial condensates.

Empirical evidence gathered from crowded cellular environments illuminates the spatial heterogeneity and dynamic equilibrium characteristic of biomolecular condensates. High-resolution imaging techniques have revealed that proteins involved in essential cellular processes can form condensate-like structures that function as dynamic scaffolds rather than static aggregates. In Alphaproteobacteria, the tubulin homolog FtsZ has been observed to form condensates during polar growth, suggesting that phase separation plays a role in organizing the division machinery beyond its canonical polymerization function [3]. The regulation of such structures involves intricate interplays between stabilizing and destabilizing factors; for instance, proteins like ZapA can promote FtsZ condensation, while others like SlmA may disrupt it, facilitating switches between polymeric and condensed states [4]. This dynamic regulation is complemented by the pervasive effect of macromolecular crowding on molecular mobility. The cytoplasm acts as a porous medium, where high concentrations of inert macromolecules impose steric hindrance and reduce diffusion coefficients, creating a short-range Brownian motion regime that enhances local reaction probabilities by increasing molecular proximity [12]. However, this reduced diffusion can also hinder long-range transport, potentially limiting the supply of substrates to condensates situated in dense regions of the cell [2]. Despite these advances in protein-centric observations, significant gaps persist in our comprehensive understanding of spatial organization. Current datasets are heavily biased toward proteins, leaving the spatial distribution and organizational states of lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids largely underexplored across various resolution regimes. The scarcity of direct observations for these non-protein macromolecules restricts our ability to construct holistic models of how diverse molecular classes interact within the crowded intracellular landscape, highlighting a critical area for future empirical investigation [10].

Synthetic compartmentalization strategies have successfully converged on these physical principles to create functional protocells and bioreactors that emulate or surpass natural capabilities. Coacervate-based systems serve as versatile platforms for investigating the origins of life and developing advanced biotechnological applications. By leveraging enzymatically reconfigurable liquid crystalline coacervates, researchers can dynamically alter the internal environment of synthetic cells, enabling controlled release and uptake of cargo in response to specific triggers [16]. Advanced engineering efforts have produced multi-compartmental architectures, such as core-shell hydrogel particles, which exploit proximity effects to facilitate incompatible tandem reactions. In systems involving glucose oxidase and horseradish peroxidase, confinement within these hierarchical structures has led to substantial enhancements in catalytic activity, demonstrating that spatial organization can overcome limitations imposed by bulk diffusion and intermediate toxicity [17]. These engineered systems illustrate that confining reactants within a defined volume can dramatically improve efficiency and stability, mirroring the metabolic channeling observed in native organelles. Nevertheless, the optimization of synthetic compartments requires navigating trade-offs between permeability, stability, and reaction kinetics. While low release rates ensure the retention of encapsulated components, they may simultaneously impede substrate influx, underscoring the necessity for precise tuning of compartment boundaries to balance retention with metabolic flux. The integration of synthetic compartments with living systems further demonstrates the potential for bidirectional communication, although maintaining physiological relevance and long-term stability in complex biological fluids remains a formidable challenge that demands continued refinement of material properties [20].

The rigorous correlation of structural data with functional assays is indispensable for validating the biological relevance of compartmentalization mechanisms and distinguishing functional phase separation from non-specific aggregation. Observations of condensate formation must be accompanied by quantitative measurements of molecular turnover, partitioning coefficients, and enzymatic activity to confirm that the observed structures actively participate in cellular processes. Dynamic synthetic cells exhibit controlled turnover essential for homeostasis, a feature that native condensates also possess through continuous exchange with the surrounding cytoplasm, ensuring that they remain responsive to cellular signals rather than becoming inert sinks [14]. The validation of structural proxies, particularly within cryo-imaging modalities, necessitates rigorous calibration against ground truth to avoid misinterpretation. Techniques such as cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) provide near-native structural snapshots at high resolution, but their application is often limited by sample thickness and preparation artifacts. The use of focused ion beam (FIB) lamellae has mitigated some of these issues by enabling the thinning of cells for in-situ whole-cell structural studies [21]. However, the integration of cryo-fluorescence microscopy with cryo-ET, while promising for combining molecular specificity with ultrastructural context, faces challenges related to registration uncertainty and resolution mismatch. Correlative approaches, including montage tomography and polarization-interference emission (PIE)-scope imaging, expand the observable horizon by linking fluorescence signals to electron density maps [6][7]. Yet, without standardized calibration protocols, there is a persistent risk of mapping fluorescent clusters to incorrect nanoscale features or missing subtle structural details invisible to light microscopy. The absence of comprehensive evaluation criteria for these correlative workflows further complicates the assessment of their reliability, emphasizing the need for benchmark datasets and consensus standards [8].

Future developments in the field must prioritize addressing the identified gaps in measurement validity, data coverage, and mechanistic discrimination to advance the integrated understanding of compartmentalization. Computational modeling holds immense potential for integrating multimodal data to predict multiscale organization, yet current models are constrained by incomplete experimental inputs and the lack of quantitative parameters describing phase behavior. Specifically, the absence of definitive discriminating observations that clearly distinguish between membrane-bounded compartmentalization and fluid-like demixing under identical conditions hinders the development of predictive frameworks capable of capturing state transitions. Qualitative synthesis suggests that while mechanisms involving multivalent interactions and environmental responsiveness are well-established, the specific inputs and thresholds governing dispersed-to-aggregated transitions remain poorly quantified [18]. Design-only research strategies propose alternative computational pipelines that incorporate uncertainty-aware segmentation and cross-modal consistency metrics to mitigate biases inherent in correlative workflows. These strategies aim to resolve nanoscale biomolecular organization by explicitly accounting for the limitations of each modality, such as the diffraction limit of light microscopy or the reconstruction artifacts in tomography. Additionally, expanding cryo-ET datasets to include diverse macromolecular classes and refining calibration standards for cryo-correlative microscopy are essential steps toward resolving the full complexity of cellular organization. Bridging the divide between descriptive structural biology and mechanistic functional analysis will require a concerted interdisciplinary effort to develop standardized benchmarks, validate discriminating conditions for LLPS versus aggregation, and integrate computational predictions with experimental constraints. Only through such comprehensive approaches can we fully elucidate the physical principles governing compartmentalization and harness them for the rational design of next-generation synthetic biological systems.

### 6.1. Specific Future Work: Bridging Scales and Modalities

Bridging the gap between nanoscale molecular interactions and mesoscale cellular organization represents one of the most formidable challenges in modern cell biology and biophysics. While the discovery of liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) has revolutionized our understanding of intracellular compartmentalization, the field currently faces a critical juncture where descriptive observations must yield to quantitative, mechanistic frameworks. As emphasized in recent authoritative perspectives on LLPS, the complexity of biological systems—characterized by multicomponent mixtures, active energy consumption, and out-of-equilibrium dynamics—cannot be adequately captured by simplified equilibrium models. Consequently, future research must prioritize interdisciplinary strategies that harmonize disparate imaging modalities, expand empirical coverage to underrepresented macromolecules, rigorously discriminate between competing phase behaviors, and integrate computational predictions with experimental constraints. This subsection delineates four actionable pathways to advance these goals, providing a roadmap for transitioning from phenomenological cataloging to predictive biophysics, and ultimately unlocking the principles that govern both native biomolecular condensates and engineered protocellular architectures.

A foundational prerequisite for accurate nanoscale localization is the establishment of standardized calibration protocols for cryo-correlative light and electron microscopy (cryo-CLEM). Currently, the integration of fluorescence microscopy with cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) relies heavily on heuristic alignment methods that struggle to reconcile the diffraction-limited resolution of optical probes with the near-atomic precision of EM. Fluorescent signals often serve as indirect proxies for molecular density, introducing significant ambiguity when mapping intensity distributions onto physical mass densities. To resolve this, future workflows must develop rigorous calibration standards that quantify the relationship between fluorophore brightness, labeling stoichiometry, and local refractive index variations. Such protocols should leverage genetically encoded tags with known quantum yields and controlled expression levels, paired with fiducial markers that maintain registration stability across modalities. Furthermore, addressing the limitations of current correlative pipelines requires systematic evaluation of registration uncertainties and error propagation. By implementing uncertainty-aware segmentation algorithms and cross-modal consistency metrics, researchers can mitigate biases arising from signal quenching, photobleaching, or EM contrast artifacts. Establishing these calibration benchmarks will transform cryo-CLEM from a qualitative visualization tool into a quantitative instrument capable of resolving true molecular partitioning and availability within crowded cellular environments, directly addressing the persistent gaps in proxy validation identified in contemporary methodological assessments.

Concurrently, the empirical foundation of intracellular spatial organization remains disproportionately skewed toward protein-centric observations, creating substantial blind spots regarding the roles of lipids and carbohydrates. Expanding cryo-ET datasets to encompass these diverse macromolecular classes is essential for constructing a holistic model of the crowded cytoplasm. Lipids, particularly amphiphilic molecules and membrane-derived vesicles, exert profound influence on condensate stability through electrostatic screening, hydrophobic interactions, and the formation of complex multi-layered architectures. Carbohydrates and glycosylated proteins further modulate phase behavior by altering hydration shells, steric repulsion, and hydrogen-bonding networks. Despite their functional significance, these components are frequently underrepresented in current tomographic reconstructions due to technical challenges in contrast generation and target identification. Systematic inclusion of lipids and carbohydrates in large-scale cryo-ET surveys will enable researchers to map cross-macromolecular interaction landscapes, revealing how heterogeneous molecular architectures collectively drive demixing and regulate client sequestration. Moreover, filling these observational voids will directly inform the rational design of synthetic protocells, allowing engineers to replicate the physicochemical complexity of native compartments. Advanced sample preparation techniques, such as cryo-focused ion beam (FIB) milling combined with automated target recognition, will be instrumental in capturing these elusive macromolecular assemblies in their native physiological contexts, ensuring that future structural catalogs reflect the true biochemical diversity of cellular interiors.

Beyond observational expansion, the field must transition from descriptive characterization to mechanistic validation, particularly in distinguishing LLPS-driven fluid-like demixing from irreversible aggregation or gelation. A pervasive challenge in current literature is the phenomenological conflation of liquid droplets with solid aggregates, which obscures the distinct biophysical principles governing each state. Future experimental designs should explicitly test discriminating conditions to isolate the thermodynamic and kinetic parameters that dictate phase transitions. This requires moving beyond static snapshots to implement dynamic perturbation assays that monitor condensate evolution under varying ionic strengths, pH gradients, ATP concentrations, and post-translational modifications. Insights from contemporary analyses of LLPS highlight the critical importance of probing interfacial structures and partitioning thermodynamics to differentiate stable liquid compartments from kinetically trapped solids. For instance, investigating the molecular rules governing simple coacervation versus complex coacervate formation can reveal how solute synergies and interference effects modulate droplet stability. Additionally, designing experiments that track liquid-to-solid transitions (LST) under controlled environmental stressors will elucidate the energy landscapes driving aging processes. By correlating specific molecular motifs—such as "sticker-and-spacer" sequences or intrinsically disordered regions—with material properties, researchers can establish causal links between sequence composition and functional outcomes. This mechanistic focus will clarify how cells actively regulate condensate maturation via chaperones or enzymatic remodeling, preventing pathological fibrillization and offering novel therapeutic strategies for neurodegenerative diseases. Furthermore, the divergence between prebiotic coacervates and modern cellular condensates presents a unique opportunity to refine our understanding of phase separation mechanics. Recent perspectives on LLPS underscore that while simple coacervation involves small molecules and peptides, biomolecular condensates rely on large polymers with complex architectures. Future experiments should systematically compare these regimes to identify universal scaling laws versus context-specific adaptations, exploring how surface adsorption of peptides or lipids stabilizes interfaces against Ostwald ripening and coalescence.

Finally, bridging scales necessitates the integration of advanced computational modeling with experimental constraints to predict multiscale organization across cellular phases. Current theoretical frameworks, largely rooted in equilibrium polymer physics and mean-field approximations, fundamentally fail to capture the active, crowded, and chemically heterogeneous nature of biological systems. Future models must incorporate non-ideal thermodynamics, active energy consumption, and dynamic molecular crowding to accurately simulate condensate formation, stability, and turnover. Coarse-grained molecular dynamics and lattice-based simulations should be calibrated using empirical data from cryo-ET and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), enabling iterative refinement of interaction potentials and diffusion coefficients. Crucially, computational approaches must account for the out-of-equilibrium dynamics that characterize living cells, including metabolic fluxes, ATP-driven remodeling, and active transport mechanisms. By coupling these sophisticated simulations with high-fidelity experimental datasets, researchers can explore "what-if" scenarios that are experimentally inaccessible, such as the impact of rare molecular species, transient concentration gradients, or mechanical stresses on macroscopic phase behavior. Machine learning potentials trained on multimodal imaging data could further accelerate this process by identifying latent patterns in multicomponent partitioning laws. By coupling these predictive tools with real-time imaging feedback loops, scientists can iteratively optimize synthetic condensates for applications ranging from green chemistry catalysts to direct delivery into engineered cells. This closed-loop methodology will accelerate the translation of fundamental biophysical insights into practical bioengineering solutions, ensuring that computational predictions remain tightly anchored to experimental reality.

Ultimately, the convergence of standardized calibration protocols, expanded macromolecular coverage, mechanistic discrimination assays, and integrated computational modeling will redefine our capacity to resolve nanoscale biomolecular organization. Each of these pillars addresses critical bottlenecks identified in contemporary subhypotheses, ensuring that future research moves beyond descriptive cataloging toward predictive, mechanistic science. By rigorously validating imaging proxies, incorporating underrepresented lipid and carbohydrate networks, distinguishing fluid demixing from irreversible aggregation, and simulating out-of-equilibrium dynamics, the field can achieve a comprehensive understanding of how biomolecular condensates self-organize and function. This multidisciplinary synthesis will not only illuminate the fundamental principles of cellular architecture but also empower the rational design of next-generation synthetic compartments, bridging the gap between natural complexity and engineered simplicity.
