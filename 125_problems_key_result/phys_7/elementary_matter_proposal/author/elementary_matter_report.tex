\title{What Are the Smallest Building Blocks of Matter?\\
\large A Resolution-Ladder Program for Testing Pointlike Fermions}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Research Proposal}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The Standard Model describes quarks and leptons as elementary fermion fields and gauge bosons plus the Higgs boson as elementary bosonic fields, whereas protons and neutrons are composite hadrons. This classification is highly successful, but elementary has an experimentally limited meaning: scattering can test pointlike behavior over a finite range of momentum transfer and under stated model assumptions; it cannot prove absolute indivisibility at every scale. This evidence-bounded proposal develops Resolution-Ladder Model Comparison, a cross-channel program for distinguishing a pointlike Standard-Model explanation from finite-form-factor, effective contact-interaction, and excited-fermion alternatives. The framework versions the kinematic definition, selection, response model, and nuisance ledger for each channel; enforces an effective-field-theory validity gate; and evaluates models on held-out bins after adversarial tests of parton distributions, detector response, and theoretical truncation. The central hypothesis is that a shared resolution ledger yields more reliable discrimination of a coherent short-distance departure from artifacts of single-channel systematics than independent limits. The program is strictly a research design. It does not operate a collider, access restricted event records, generate a new exclusion, or claim a discovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Everyday matter is assembled from atoms, but atoms are not the endpoint of physical description. Their electrons are described as leptons; their nuclei are built from protons and neutrons; and the latter are composite hadrons containing quark and gluon degrees of freedom. Particle physics goes further by describing quarks and leptons as matter fields and by describing gauge and Higgs fields as elementary bosonic sectors. This framework is the Standard Model (SM), a quantum field theory with gauge structure
\begin{equation}
SU(3)_{C}\times SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}.
\label{eq:gaugegroup}
\end{equation}
Particle-physics reviews compile the inventory, interaction conventions, and experimental status \cite{pdg2008}; standard introductions explain how the SM unifies quark, lepton, electroweak, and strong-interaction phenomena \cite{cottingham2007}.

The success of the SM should not be confused with a completed proof that every field in the model is indivisible. In experimental science, the defensible statement is that a field behaves pointlike within a tested kinematic regime and within a declared class of alternatives. A constituent model, a finite form factor, a four-fermion contact term, or an excited-fermion state can each alter a scattering distribution. Conversely, an inaccurate parton distribution function (PDF), an incomplete detector response, a background mismatch, or an invalid effective-field-theory (EFT) expansion can create a similar apparent departure. The question of the smallest building blocks is therefore a problem in discriminating hypotheses under finite resolution, not a request for a final ontological certificate.

This report constructs a research proposal rather than an experimental result. The Survey Agent freezes evidence and boundary conditions. The Idea Agent selects a cross-channel model-comparison direction. The ExperimentDesign Agent specifies a computational, \texttt{DESIGN\_ONLY} protocol. The Author Agent turns those inputs into an IEEE-style research plan. The proposed Resolution-Ladder Model Comparison (RLMC) determines when a short-distance deviation has enough kinematic, theoretical, and systematic support to be called evidence against a pointlike-SM explanation, and when the appropriate conclusion is only a range-specific constraint or an unresolved ambiguity.

\section{Scope and Definitions}

\subsection{What elementary means in this proposal}

Table~\ref{tab:inventory} fixes the vocabulary. A field classified as elementary in the SM is not assigned a resolved spatial substructure in the model. A composite hadron is a bound state with quark and gluon degrees of freedom. A pointlike experimental result means that the selected observables do not require a finite-size, contact, or excited-state deformation within the stated sensitivity, nuisance assumptions, and energy range. It does not prove that no deeper dynamics exists at arbitrarily high momentum transfer.

The Pauli exclusion principle applies to identical fermions in the same quantum state; bosons may occupy a common state. This difference in quantum statistics does not decide whether a particle is composite. Similarly, gauge bosons mediate gauge interactions, but the scalar Higgs boson is not best described as a force carrier. These distinctions matter because the proposal links a precise particle-physics claim to an equally precise experimental test rather than reproducing a simplified chart of particles.

\subsection{Known scope and open scope}

The SM has passed stringent tests in many sectors. Higgs production and decay analyses, for example, have tested selected coupling patterns against SM expectations in specified collision samples \cite{cms_higgs2015}. Yet the framework does not include a quantum theory of gravity, and the minimal massless-neutrino formulation is inadequate for neutrino-mass phenomena. It also does not uniquely identify the origin of all flavor patterns or the microscopic nature of dark matter. Those facts motivate searches for departures, but they do not predict that a particular quark or lepton must be composite.

RLMC is deliberately narrower than a generic search for all beyond-SM physics. It focuses on finite-resolution signatures most directly connected to fermion pointlikeness: high-mass and high-transverse-momentum scattering tails, angular distributions, possible form-factor suppressions or enhancements, and benchmark excited-fermion spectra. It treats weakly coupled hidden-sector searches and low-energy precision probes as complementary context rather than direct measurements of quark radius \cite{alekhin2016,jaeckel2010}.

\section{Evidence Survey: From Particle Inventory to Resolution Tests}

\subsection{Hadronic structure and finite resolution}

The mass and charge distribution of a proton are not evidence that its quarks can be isolated as free particles. In QCD, quark and gluon degrees of freedom are confined into color-neutral hadrons. Collider predictions therefore require a factorized but imperfect connection between partonic scattering and hadronic observables. PDFs summarize the momentum distributions of partons in a hadron for a stated scheme and scale. This makes them indispensable to high-energy tests of quark and lepton pointlikeness: a deformation in a high-mass spectrum can resemble a PDF shift unless the uncertainty model is explicit.

Lepton-hadron scattering is complementary because it can probe partonic structure with different systematics and kinematic lever arms. Proposed electron-ion measurements aim to improve understanding of hadronic and mesonic structure through broad kinematic coverage, but their performance estimates are not observed data \cite{arrington2021}. RLMC uses such channels only as future or optional interfaces and labels them as projected until a released measurement and response model are available.

\subsection{Contact interactions, form factors, and excited states}

When unknown heavy physics cannot be produced directly in a given energy range, an EFT can summarize its leading effects using higher-dimensional operators:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SM}}+
\sum_{i}\frac{C_i}{\Lambda^2}\mathcal{O}_i+
\sum_{j}\frac{D_j}{\Lambda^4}\mathcal{O}_j+\cdots .
\label{eq:eft}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $C_i$, scale $\Lambda$, and operators $\mathcal{O}_i$ encode a low-energy deformation; they do not specify a unique ultraviolet constituent model. Dimension-eight coefficients $D_j$ further show why a dimension-six-only conclusion can depend on an assumed truncation. A systematic SMEFT parameterization requires explicit choices of basis, flavor structure, and electroweak input scheme \cite{brivio2017}.

High-energy Drell--Yan tails can constrain semileptonic interactions, but the inferred reach depends on flavor assignments, higher-order operators, and the validity of the EFT at the momentum transfer actually used \cite{allwicher2023}. A finite-size interpretation can alternatively be expressed through a form-factor expansion:
\begin{equation}
F_f(Q^2)=1-\frac{r_f^2 Q^2}{6}+\mathcal{O}(Q^4),
\label{eq:formfactor}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is a declared momentum-transfer variable and $r_f^2$ is a model-dependent radius parameter. This expansion must not be extrapolated past its stated regime. Excited-fermion searches supply another alternative with a different predicted shape. A recent excited-tau search reported no excess in its selected final state and set benchmark-dependent limits \cite{cms_tau2025}; it did not establish that all charged leptons lack constituents at every scale.

\subsection{Complementary resolution programs}

Future colliders may offer both higher energy reach and precision control. The Future Circular Collider program describes complementarity among electron, proton, and ion collision modes, but projected sensitivities depend on machine and detector assumptions \cite{abada2019}. A resolution ladder should not append projected curves to existing data as if all were comparable measurements. It must retain an input-status field: observed public measurement, validated public-likelihood re-interpretation, synthetic closure test, or future projection. This status is part of the evidence rather than a formatting detail.

\section{Research Gaps and Idea Selection}

The Survey Agent identifies six linked gaps. Resolution is not consistently defined across channels. Contact interactions are too readily described as literal compositeness without an EFT boundary. PDF and response-model systematics can confound a short-distance signal. Form-factor, contact, and excited-state alternatives are often analyzed in isolation. Collider, lepton-hadron, and precision programs need an explicit transfer contract rather than loose complementarity claims. Finally, many interpretation claims lack a versioned record of binning, response, theory, and decision conditions.

The Idea Agent compared a single-channel Drell--Yan fit, an excited-lepton scan, a lepton-hadron transfer study, and an integrated framework. The selected idea is RLMC because it does not merely add sources. It imposes a common inference contract: each observable receives a momentum-transfer definition, a status label, a response model, a nuisance ledger, and an EFT or non-EFT validity condition before model comparison begins.

The central hypothesis is that a shared resolution ledger and cross-channel likelihood will separate coherent substructure-like patterns from EFT-truncation and channel-specific systematic artifacts more reliably than independent single-channel limits. The primary falsifiers are equally clear. The program fails to support an alternative if model families are non-identifiable, if cutoff or operator-order changes reverse the preference, if nuisance perturbations reproduce the apparent departure, or if a proposed common coefficient pattern is incompatible across channels without an explicit ultraviolet model.
