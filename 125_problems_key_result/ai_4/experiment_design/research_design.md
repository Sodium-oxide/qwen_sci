# ExperimentDesign: Uncertainty-Calibrated Human-AI Collaboration

**Status:** `DESIGN_ONLY`. This protocol has not been executed and contains no observed outcome.

## 1. Scientific question and estimands

The study asks when an AI system should automate a task, when it should augment a human, and when a human-AI team should be treated as complementary. The design separates three task regimes: stationary routine tasks, distribution-shifted tasks, and equivocal tasks in which the objective or interpretation is under-specified.

The primary estimand is risk-constrained expected utility per unit time:

\[
U_{c,r}=\mathbb{E}[R\mid c,r]/\mathbb{E}[T\mid c,r],
\qquad \Pr(L>\tau\mid c,r)\leq \alpha,
\]

where (c) is the agent condition, (r) is the task regime, (R) is task reward, (T) is completion time, and (L) is a cost-weighted loss. The constraint prevents a fast but unsafe policy from winning by ignoring rare failures.

The complementarity estimand is

\[
C_{r}=U_{H+A,r}-\max(U_{H,r},U_{A,r}),
\]

with bootstrap intervals over participants and task families. (C_r>0) is only evidence of performance complementarity under the specified regime; it is not a claim that a team is universally better.

## 2. Factorial design

The proposed human study uses a repeated-measures factorial design. An illustrative planning target is 240 participants: 80 novice, 80 intermediate, and 80 expert participants for the selected low-stakes task family. The actual sample size must be locked after simulation-based power analysis for the smallest preregistered interaction and an attrition allowance. If human recruitment is authorized, institutional review, informed consent, privacy protection, and debriefing are required.

The factors are:

| Factor | Levels | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Agent | human-only, AI-only, human+AI | Establish baselines and team effects |
| Regime | routine, shifted, equivocal | Separate automation boundaries |
| Interface | answer-only, confidence, confidence+abstain, assumption-elicitation | Test uncertainty communication |
| Calibration | calibrated, overconfident, underconfident | Separate truthful uncertainty from display effects |
| Expertise | novice, intermediate, expert | Test heterogeneous augmentation |
| Exposure | one session, repeated sessions | Test learning, deskilling, and reliance |
| Cost | symmetric, asymmetric | Test risk-sensitive delegation |

The AI-only condition is evaluated without a human correcting it. The human-only condition receives the same task information but no AI suggestion. The hybrid condition permits the human to accept, reject, edit, request evidence, or delegate. An oracle-calibrated reference supplies a confidence signal tied to the true held-out error probability; it is a measurement control rather than a deployable assumption.

## 3. Task benchmark

The core tasks are low-stakes, synthetic, and reproducible. They should require judgment but not medical, legal, employment, or safety-critical decisions. Task families include:

1. **Routine classification and scheduling:** complete information, stable label semantics, and a known cost matrix. The task tests throughput and basic accuracy.
2. **Distribution-shifted diagnosis of system states:** synthetic equipment or logistics records are generated from a training distribution and a held-out distribution with changes in feature frequency, missingness, and causal relationships. The task tests calibration and transfer, not clinical diagnosis.
3. **Ambiguous incident interpretation:** a short record admits two or more plausible explanations, and the objective is incomplete until the participant identifies an assumption or asks for a missing fact. The scoring rubric rewards appropriate clarification and penalizes unjustified certainty.
4. **Resource allocation with asymmetric costs:** choices have different false-positive and false-negative consequences. The task tests whether the agent uses a cost-sensitive threshold rather than a generic accuracy objective.
5. **Counterfactual planning:** the participant or AI predicts how an unchosen intervention would change a simulated system. The task tests causal reasoning and whether an explanation changes decisions for the right reasons.

Each episode provides a hidden ground truth generated by a known simulator. For equivocal episodes, multiple interpretations are represented in the data-generating process and the participant receives credit for identifying the ambiguity. A single forced label is not treated as the only correct expression of intelligence.

## 4. Agent and interface conditions

The AI model is frozen before the study. It produces an answer, a confidence estimate, an optional abstention decision, and a structured list of assumptions. The answer-only interface hides all uncertainty. The confidence interface displays a probability and a short machine-readable evidence summary. The confidence+abstain interface allows the AI to defer. The assumption-elicitation interface asks the human to select which missing fact or objective would change the decision before revealing an AI recommendation.

Calibration is manipulated by a preregistered monotone transformation of the AI's confidence, with the answer policy held fixed. This distinguishes the value of correct uncertainty from the value of a persuasive numerical display. A separate no-rationale condition prevents explanations from being credited merely because they increase acceptance.

The human controls final submission in all hybrid conditions. The design records whether a recommendation was accepted, modified, rejected, or requested again; time spent inspecting evidence; confidence before and after AI exposure; and whether a participant can explain a disagreement. The system never presents the AI as an authority and always displays the model version and known evaluation domain.

## 5. Procedure

### S0: preregistration and pilot

Freeze task generators, task-regime labels, cost matrices, model checkpoint, interface text, calibration transformation, randomization schedule, exclusion rules, primary contrasts, and interpretation thresholds. Pilot only for comprehension, timing, and technical failures; pilot observations are not included in the confirmatory analysis.

### S1: baseline

Participants complete a human-only block with balanced routine, shifted, and equivocal items. They report confidence and may abstain. The baseline estimates expertise differences, natural calibration, and task difficulty. AI-only episodes are executed on the same item pool without exposing participants to the answer.

### S2: collaboration

Participants are randomized to interface order using a counterbalanced Latin-square schedule. They complete hybrid blocks with calibrated, miscalibrated, and abstention-enabled interfaces. Item identities and correct answers remain hidden. In equivocal blocks, the participant may ask one of a fixed set of clarification questions, creating a measurable information-acquisition cost.

### S3: repeated exposure and transfer

The collaboration blocks are repeated on new task instances. The final block removes AI assistance and changes the task distribution. Learning is the change in unaided performance and calibration relative to the participant's own baseline. Deskilling is a decline in transfer or in the ability to detect a wrong AI recommendation after repeated exposure. Both outcomes are preregistered and neither is assumed in advance.

### S4: workflow simulation

After the individual blocks, the same item-level results feed a discrete-event workflow simulation. The simulation varies staffing, review capacity, new exception-handling tasks, and AI latency. This separates task-level performance from a claim about job replacement. It estimates which human tasks remain: verification, escalation, objective selection, missing-information collection, accountability, and maintenance.

## 6. Measurements

### Performance and risk

Record accuracy, cost-weighted loss, time, throughput, abstention, clarification requests, and tail risk. Use the same hidden item distribution for all agent conditions and report performance separately for routine, shifted, and equivocal regimes.

### Calibration and selective prediction

Use Brier score, expected calibration error, reliability diagrams, and risk-coverage curves. For a system that abstains, selective risk at coverage (q) is

\[
SR(q)=\mathbb{E}[L\mid \text{accepted},\ \mathrm{coverage}=q].
\]

The primary comparison is whether calibrated confidence plus abstention lowers selective risk at equal coverage and time. Under equivocality, the target is not only confidence but also assumption accuracy and value of information from a clarification question.

### Complementary errors

For item (i), define binary error indicators (E_{H,i}) and (E_{A,i}). Estimate conditional error dependence within regime and expertise strata, including tetrachoric or phi correlation where appropriate. A hybrid that succeeds after one member fails supplies evidence of error complementarity; a hybrid that accepts correlated errors supplies evidence of automation bias or false redundancy.

### Reliance and human learning

Measure false acceptance of a wrong AI recommendation, false rejection of a correct recommendation, time to override, trust calibration after feedback, and confidence revision. The no-AI transfer block measures unaided accuracy, calibration, explanation of disagreements, and retention after a delay. Workload uses a short validated subjective scale plus objective time and interaction counts.

### Equity and subgroup robustness

Task instances vary in difficulty, missingness, and cost asymmetry without encoding protected-person attributes. If future work uses human demographic data, it requires explicit consent and a separate fairness analysis. Report subgroup effects by expertise and relevant task strata, not by speculative biological or social stereotypes. A group-average improvement is insufficient if tail loss rises for a vulnerable task slice.

## 7. Statistical analysis

The confirmatory model is a hierarchical mixed-effects model with fixed effects for agent, regime, interface, calibration, expertise, exposure, and preregistered interactions. Random intercepts and slopes cover participant, task family, item generator, and session. A generic outcome model is

\[
Y_{pijk}=\beta_0+\beta_C C_i+\beta_R R_j+\beta_I I_k
+\beta_{CR}C_iR_j+\beta_{CI}C_iI_k+u_p+u_f+u_g+\epsilon_{pijk}.
\]

Primary contrasts are AI versus human on routine tasks, calibrated hybrid versus AI on shifted tasks, assumption-elicitation hybrid versus both baselines on equivocal tasks, and repeated-exposure transfer with versus without AI. Correct for the preregistered family of primary outcomes. Bootstrap at the participant and task-family level; do not treat repeated decisions from one participant as independent observations.

The practical decision rule maps results to three states. **Automation** is supported for a narrow task slice only when AI meets the risk constraint and does not produce unacceptable shift loss. **Augmentation** is supported when hybrid utility or human learning improves without a harmful increase in reliance. **Complementarity** is supported when (C_r) is positive, error dependence is sufficiently low, and the result survives interface and expertise sensitivity checks. None of these states implies occupation-wide replacement.

## 8. Threats to validity and controls

The first threat is benchmark overfitting. Hidden generators, new distributions, and an independent replication reduce it. The second is interface confounding: a longer rationale may consume time or persuade independently of truth. Time-matched text controls and answer-only conditions estimate that effect. The third is automation bias: a confidence number can be treated as authority. Misinformation and abstention controls measure, rather than assume away, this failure.

The fourth threat is expertise selection. Participants who volunteer for AI studies may be unusually favorable or skeptical. Stratification, pre-registered recruitment targets, and a follow-up sample from a different population provide robustness. The fifth is learning contamination: participants may remember item answers. New generators and changed surface forms prevent memorization. The sixth is ecological validity. Synthetic tasks permit causal measurement but cannot capture every organizational or social factor. The workflow simulation is therefore a bridge, not proof of labor-market impact.

## 9. Ethics and governance

The initial work is simulation-only. Human participation, if later authorized, uses low-stakes tasks, informed consent, anonymized logs, compensation independent of AI acceptance, and the right to withdraw. No participant is evaluated for employment, credit, health, immigration, or legal status. A study involving professional experts must not expose confidential records.

The model card and audit log record model version, data provenance, confidence calibration, abstentions, overrides, errors, and human final decisions. A human remains accountable for every hybrid output. NIST AI RMF principles motivate documenting validity, reliability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness. If the AI's performance changes materially under shift, the interface must default to abstention or human review rather than silently expanding its deployment domain.

## 10. Interpretation matrix

| Pattern | Interpretation | Deployment implication |
|---|---|---|
| AI wins only on routine items | Narrow automation capability | Limit scope to validated task slice |
| AI is fast but poorly calibrated under shift | Speed does not justify replacement | Require abstention and human review |
| Hybrid outperforms both with low error correlation | Conditional complementarity | Invest in delegation and auditability |
| Novice gains but transfer declines | Augmentation with possible deskilling | Add training and no-AI practice |
| Equivocal performance improves after clarification | Human value lies in objective and assumption handling | Preserve human authority over goals |
| Hybrid accepts correlated wrong answers | Automation misuse or interface failure | Redesign confidence and escalation |

## 11. Limitations

The protocol studies selected task families and cannot forecast all occupations or all future AI systems. Human intuition is operationalized as measurable uncertainty handling and goal clarification; this may omit valuable tacit knowledge. Calibration estimates depend on the test distribution. A good hybrid interface may not scale when accountability, latency, privacy, or organizational incentives change. These are reasons to interpret the result as a boundary map and not as a universal prophecy.
