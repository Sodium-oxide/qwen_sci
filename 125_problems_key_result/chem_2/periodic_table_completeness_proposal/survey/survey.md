# Survey Agent Report

## 1. Research question and concise answer

**Question.** Will the periodic table ever be complete?

**Answer.** It is not currently known that the table is complete at element 118, and IUPAC's discovery process anticipates claims beyond the seventh row. However, the question has no responsible yes/no answer unless ``complete'' is defined. The current periodic table can be complete in an institutional sense up to the highest formally recognized element while remaining incomplete as a map of physically possible nuclei. A future limit could arise from nuclear instability, vanishingly small production probability, unattainable targets or beams, inability to identify a nucleus with sufficient confidence, or a combination of these. Human creativity can improve methods and evidence, but it cannot by itself remove nuclear fission, finite lifetimes, or the need for reproducible proof.

## 2. Verified institutional context

IUPAC announced on 30 November 2016 that elements 113, 115, 117, and 118 were formally named nihonium (Nh), moscovium (Mc), tennessine (Ts), and oganesson (Og) [S1]. The announcement also notes that exploration beyond the seventh row continued and that IUPAC and IUPAP were establishing a joint working group to examine discovery-verification criteria.

The IUPAC page for the 2018 IUPAC/IUPAP Provisional Report explains why an update was required [S2]. Criteria established by the earlier Transfermium Working Group were associated with discovery methods that were being replaced by newer methods. The joint group was formed before further claims and prepared guidance for determining discovery priority. This is an institutional finding, not a prediction of an element maximum. It establishes that a future discovery must be evaluated by a proof framework that can evolve with technology.

## 3. Physics context: why 118 is neither proven endpoint nor open-ended guarantee

The heaviest nuclei exist in competition between attractive nuclear forces, repulsive proton--proton Coulomb effects, shell structure, and decay/fission channels. The shell model motivates the ``island of stability'' concept: shell closures can increase binding and lifetimes relative to a smooth liquid-drop expectation. Hofmann's review reports historical predictions of enhanced stability around neutron number \(N=184\), with candidate proton shell closures that have shifted among models, and also notes predicted stability for deformed nuclei near \(Z=108\), \(N=162\) [S3]. These are model-dependent structural expectations, not proof that a particular future element can be produced or will persist long enough for formal recognition.

The same review identifies intensive heavy-ion beams, target technology, electromagnetic recoil separation, and sensitive detector arrays as prerequisites for discoveries over the preceding decades [S3]. Thus, discovery is governed by a chain of conditions:

\[
\text{physically bound nucleus} \rightarrow \text{production pathway} \rightarrow \text{survival/transport} \rightarrow \text{identification signal} \rightarrow \text{independent assessment} \rightarrow \text{formal recognition}.
\]

Failure at any one link does not necessarily prove that an element cannot exist. It can instead identify a technology or evidence limit. Conversely, a theoretical stability estimate alone does not establish an element. This separation is central to the proposed research design.

## 4. Four meanings of completeness

| Completeness type | Question | Evidence needed | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Institutional | What is the highest element formally recognized and named? | IUPAC/IUPAP assessment and naming record | A physical maximum atomic number |
| Observational | What nuclei have been produced and identified with accepted evidence? | Decay/identification evidence, provenance, independent assessment | Non-existence of unobserved nuclei |
| Technological | What can present facilities and methods plausibly access? | Facility/model performance under stated conditions | An immutable physical boundary |
| Physical | What nuclei could exist as distinguishable bound systems? | Nuclear theory plus validated observations and uncertainty | A guarantee that they can be produced or verified |

This taxonomy corrects two symmetrical overclaims. ``No element beyond 118 has been recognized'' does not imply ``no element beyond 118 can exist.'' ``New methods may be invented'' does not imply ``there is no physics-defined limit.''

## 5. Literature synthesis by sub-hypothesis

### H1 — The IUPAC-recognized table is not evidence of a known physical endpoint

**Status: supported.** IUPAC formally recognizes named discoveries through a review process; its 2016 announcement explicitly describes continued exploration and a new criterion-review group [S1]. The evidence supports an institutional boundary at 118 at that date, not an absolute nuclear maximum.

### H2 — A physical reachability frontier should depend on stability and technology jointly

**Status: strongly supported in principle.** Superheavy-nucleus research relies on production, separation, detection, and interpretation, while shell effects modify stability expectations [S3], [S4]. A credible forecast must retain both nuclear-model uncertainty and facility/evidence constraints.

### H3 — Discovery evidence must be technology-aware but not technology-permissive

**Status: supported.** IUPAC's 2018 report was prompted by a change in methods and the need to revise earlier discovery criteria [S2]. The lesson is not that any new signal is sufficient; it is that proof obligations should be expressed in a form that can be applied to new instruments without lowering identity and priority standards.

### H4 — A predictive system can be evaluated without conducting a nuclear experiment

**Status: testable.** Historical discovery and non-confirmation records, together with model predictions available before a documented outcome, can support retrospective and prospective calibration studies. Such a study evaluates the quality of evidence-aware prioritization; it does not produce a new element.

## 6. Evidence constraints and admissible claims

| Evidence state | Admissible wording | Prohibited wording |
|---|---|---|
| Theory/model | ``A model projects a conditional stability or reachability distribution.'' | ``Element \(Z\) exists.'' |
| Facility assessment | ``A facility-method scenario is predicted to have stated reachability.'' | ``The facility will discover element \(Z\).'' |
| Signal observation | ``A signal is under evaluation using documented evidence criteria.'' | ``A new element has been discovered.'' |
| IUPAC/IUPAP recognition | ``A discovery claim has been formally assessed and recognized.'' | ``The periodic table is physically complete.'' |

## 7. Research gaps passed to the Idea Agent

1. **GAP-01 — Completion-definition gap.** Physical, technological, observational, and institutional endpoints are routinely conflated.
2. **GAP-02 — Coupled-reachability gap.** Stability predictions and expected production/detection feasibility are often discussed separately, making uncertainty and bottlenecks difficult to compare.
3. **GAP-03 — Proof-chain gap.** New instruments need a transparent mapping from raw evidence to an IUPAC/IUPAP-relevant identity claim without hard-coding obsolete methods.
4. **GAP-04 — Forecast-validation gap.** There is no proposal-level benchmark here that tests whether a reachability model was calibrated on historical outcomes and near-miss/non-confirmation cases.

## 8. Survey conclusion

The periodic table's apparent endpoint at 118 is a present recognized state, not a demonstrated final boundary. The appropriate next research question is not ``can imagination force an unlimited sequence of elements?'' It is: **can an evidence-aware model quantify where nuclear stability, production feasibility, detection capability, and discovery proof jointly make an element conditionally reachable, currently unreachable, or physically doubtful?** The model's predictions must remain conditional and falsifiable, because both the nuclear theory and the experimental technology are uncertain.
