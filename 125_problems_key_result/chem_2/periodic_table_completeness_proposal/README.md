# Four-Agent Research Proposal: Will the Periodic Table Ever Be Complete?

**Research topic.** The periodic table currently contains 118 formally named elements. This simulated four-agent run asks what ``complete'' can mean scientifically, whether new elements beyond oganesson remain plausible, and how IUPAC/IUPAP discovery standards should shape a research program.

## Scope boundary

The documents are an evidence-grounded simulation of the repository's Survey, Idea, ExperimentDesign, and Author agents. They are not a nuclear experiment plan, an accelerator operating procedure, a prediction that a particular element will be discovered, a simulated reaction result, or an IUPAC priority/naming decision.

## Primary sources checked with the in-app browser

- IUPAC's 30 November 2016 announcement formally naming elements 113, 115, 117, and 118 as Nh, Mc, Ts, and Og.
- IUPAC's 14 November 2018 page for the IUPAC/IUPAP Provisional Report, which states that discovery criteria developed by the Transfermium Working Group required review because earlier methods were being replaced by newer methods.
- S. Hofmann, ``Super-heavy nuclei,'' *Journal of Physics G*, vol. 42, Art. no. 114001, 2015, doi: `10.1088/0954-3899/42/11/114001`; the accessible abstract discusses shell-driven stability predictions, production/identification prerequisites, and the need for further facility improvement.

## Research conclusion

No accepted result sets a known final atomic number. Yet ``human inventiveness is the only obstacle'' is too strong: nuclear binding and fission, available reaction pathways, production rates, half-lives, detector sensitivity, statistical confidence, and IUPAC/IUPAP evidentiary standards all impose genuine constraints. A more defensible conclusion is conditional: the periodic table is not known to be complete at 118, but its eventual physical and practical boundary remains an open, evidence-limited nuclear-science problem.
