# Survey Agent: What Is Addiction and How Does It Work?

## Scientific reframing

The topic is reframed as:

> How do reward learning, incentive salience, stress and withdrawal, executive control, memory-driven cues, and genetic/environmental risk interact over time to produce compulsive substance or behavior seeking, and which mechanisms can be measured and safely modified without reducing addiction to a moral failure or a single neurotransmitter?

The prompt combines two topics. The passage about reverse-engineering and stimulating a mouse memory is relevant as a general example of causal memory manipulation, but it is not evidence that human memories can be uploaded, that addiction is an artificial memory, or that a memory transplant treats substance use disorder. The present Survey therefore uses memory only as one mechanism through which cues, contexts, expectations, and relapse can be learned and reactivated.

## Evidence map

### 1. Definition and clinical phenotype

The National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) describes addiction as a complex disease in which repeated drug use changes the brain and makes quitting difficult, even for people who want to stop [E1]. A clinically useful phenotype includes compulsive seeking or use, continued use despite harmful consequences, craving, impaired control, tolerance, withdrawal, and recurrent relapse. Substance use disorder is not defined by one neural image or one behavior in one laboratory task. Severity, substance, route, exposure history, comorbidity, social setting, and treatment access all matter.

The word “addiction” can also describe non-substance behaviors, but evidence and diagnostic criteria differ across substances and behavioral disorders. This study focuses on substance-use mechanisms while allowing a transfer module for non-drug reward learning only where the evidence supports it. It does not assume that all repetitive behavior shares one pathology.

### 2. Drug action and reward circuitry

NIDA’s brain-science account describes drugs as interfering with neuronal signaling through receptors, transporters, and neurotransmitter release [E2]. The basal ganglia support motivation, reward, and habit formation; the extended amygdala contributes to stress, anxiety, irritability, and withdrawal; and the prefrontal cortex supports planning, decision making, and self-control [E2, E3]. Repeated exposure produces adaptations in these circuits. Drug-related dopamine signaling is better interpreted as a teaching or reinforcement signal than as a simple pleasure meter [E2].

This circuit model is powerful but should not become a one-molecule explanation. Dopamine interacts with opioid, glutamatergic, GABAergic, stress, endocrine, and learning systems. Drug effects depend on dose, pharmacokinetics, context, prior learning, and individual biology. A change in a reward-circuit signal is therefore a candidate mechanism that needs behavioral and longitudinal validation.

### 3. Learning, memory, and cue-driven relapse

Repeated use binds drug effects to places, people, paraphernalia, internal states, and actions. These cues can later trigger craving and seeking even after long abstinence. The learned association can outlast the acute drug effect, making relapse a memory and decision problem as well as a pharmacological problem [E2, E4]. Extinction does not necessarily erase the original association; renewal, reinstatement, and spontaneous recovery can return when context or stress changes.

Addiction-related memory should be decomposed into cue identity, expected reward, action value, temporal context, affective state, and confidence in the expected outcome. An evoked craving response is not identical to conscious autobiographical recollection. Likewise, a mouse’s cue- or fear-related behavior is not direct evidence of a human subjective craving episode. The artificial-memory passage in the prompt illustrates why causal neural manipulation is scientifically interesting, but its behavioral endpoint must not be inflated into digital transplantation or a clinical cure.

### 4. Incentive sensitization and habit formation

Incentive-sensitization theory distinguishes wanting from liking: repeated drug exposure can increase the motivational pull of drug-associated cues without increasing pleasure proportionally [E5]. Habit and goal-directed systems may also shift as action values become less sensitive to outcomes and behavior becomes stimulus-driven. These theories make different predictions about devaluation, omission, cue exposure, and relapse after a change in drug value.

The relevant gap is not choosing one theory by rhetoric. A test should manipulate outcome value, cue availability, stress, and response cost while recording choice, latency, vigor, craving, and physiological state. If behavior persists despite devaluation and increased cost, that supports a compulsive or habit-like component; if it changes rapidly with outcome value, goal-directed control remains important.

### 5. Withdrawal, stress, and negative reinforcement

NIDA describes the extended amygdala as participating in aversive withdrawal states and the motivation to seek a drug for relief rather than euphoria [E2]. This negative-reinforcement process can interact with reward tolerance: reduced sensitivity to natural rewards and increased sensitivity to withdrawal discomfort narrow the choice landscape. Stress, sleep disruption, pain, trauma, and social adversity can amplify this loop.

Withdrawal is not simply a transient absence of pleasure. It can affect sleep, mood, autonomic state, cognition, and stress responsivity. A design must separate acute intoxication, post-acute withdrawal, craving, and long-term relapse vulnerability. It must also distinguish pharmacological withdrawal from the consequences of social and environmental deprivation.

### 6. Genetics, development, and environment

Addiction risk is probabilistic and multidimensional. Genetic variation can influence reward sensitivity, impulsivity, stress reactivity, metabolism, and response to treatment, while environment influences availability, stress, trauma, peer exposure, housing, poverty, policy, and access to care [E6, E7]. Adolescent neurodevelopment can increase vulnerability because control systems mature over time [E3]. No genetic variant should be treated as destiny, and no social factor should be treated as a fixed label.

The proper unit is a gene-by-development-by-environment interaction over repeated observations. Polygenic scores, when used, require calibration across ancestry and careful privacy protection. Environmental measures should not be reduced to a single “risk score” that obscures structural causes or produces discriminatory treatment.

### 7. Treatment and recovery

Addiction is treatable, and recovery can involve medication, behavioral therapy, contingency management, social support, harm reduction, and recovery services [E1, E8]. Treatment mechanisms may include reducing withdrawal, lowering cue reactivity, restoring goal-directed control, improving sleep and mood, or changing the environment. A treatment that reduces drug use but worsens cognition, agency, or quality of life needs a broader evaluation.

Relapse is not proof of moral failure or treatment impossibility. It is an outcome that can reveal which learned associations, stress states, and support conditions remain active. A mechanistic atlas should include recovery trajectories and treatment response, not only the onset of compulsive use.

## Evidence boundaries

The evidence supports the following bounded conclusions:

1. Addiction is a complex brain disease and mental-health condition involving persistent changes in motivation, reward learning, stress, self-control, decision making, and memory [E1--E3].
2. Repeated drug use can overactivate reward-related signaling and produce adaptations that increase cue-driven seeking, tolerance, and reduced response to natural rewards [E2, E5].
3. Drug-associated memories and contexts can contribute to craving and relapse, but behavioral expression is not automatically subjective recollection [E2, E4].
4. Genetic, developmental, environmental, and social factors jointly influence risk and recovery [E6, E7].
5. Addiction is treatable, but no single intervention or biomarker is universally effective [E1, E8].

The evidence does not justify claiming that addiction is only a dopamine disorder, that a single engram is the complete disease, that a mouse artificial-memory paradigm is a human treatment, or that genetic risk determines an individual outcome.

## Accepted research gaps

- **G1:** No unified longitudinal model separates reward learning, incentive salience, habit strength, withdrawal relief, executive control, and cue-memory reactivation.
- **G2:** It remains difficult to distinguish cue-driven wanting from pleasure, conscious craving, generic arousal, and motor vigor.
- **G3:** The causal relationship between drug-associated memory representations and relapse is incompletely specified across contexts and time.
- **G4:** Withdrawal, stress, sleep, pain, and negative reinforcement interact with reward adaptation in person-specific ways.
- **G5:** Genetic and environmental risk factors are often modeled separately even though their effects are developmental and interactive.
- **G6:** Treatment response and relapse prediction require calibrated multimodal models that avoid leakage, stigma, and discriminatory use.
- **G7:** Safe, selective manipulation of cue memories or control circuits must be distinguished from nonspecific suppression of motivation or affect.

## Candidate gaps requiring validation

- **G8:** A state-space “addiction control balance” could predict relapse risk better than a static severity score.
- **G9:** Individualized intervention targets may be selected from the dominant mechanism—cue wanting, withdrawal relief, habit persistence, or control failure—rather than the substance label alone.

## Handoff to Idea Agent

The Idea Agent receives the evidence map, accepted gaps G1--G7, candidate gaps G8--G9, and these invariants:

- Separate reward prediction, wanting, liking, habit, withdrawal, craving, memory reactivation, and subjective report.
- Treat addiction as a longitudinal brain-behavior-environment process, not a moral category.
- Do not equate mouse artificial-memory behavior with human addiction or digital memory transplantation.
- Control for intoxication, withdrawal, stress, sleep, medication, pain, social context, and treatment exposure.
- Preserve human autonomy, privacy, non-stigmatizing language, and clinical review.
- No substance exposure, memory manipulation, or treatment intervention is executed in this proposal.
