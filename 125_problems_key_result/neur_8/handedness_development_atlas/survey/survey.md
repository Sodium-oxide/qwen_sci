# Survey: Why Are Most People Right-Handed?

## Scientific reframing

The popular question "Why are most people right-handed?" is too broad if handedness is treated as a single binary trait. The survey therefore reframes it as a developmental systems question:

> How do polygenic neurodevelopmental lateralization, early developmental perturbations, and culturally shaped task practice jointly generate the population-level excess of right-hand preference?

The target is not a single "right-handedness gene." It is the causal architecture that links genotype, prenatal and perinatal development, hemispheric organization, motor performance, and learned task preference. This distinction matters because preference, performance, writing hand, and a latent motor asymmetry are related but non-identical phenotypes.

## Scope and evidence policy

The Survey covers human handedness, developmental neuroscience, brain asymmetry, quantitative genetics, population variation, and the measurement of motor laterality. It treats associations as associations unless a study supplies a credible temporal, family-based, instrumental, or perturbational basis for a stronger claim. The proposed downstream study is design-only: no new human data, intervention, or clinical inference is reported here.

Europe PMC visible article pages were used to verify the bibliographic metadata and abstracts on 2026-09-01. The evidence registry records the source identity and the claims that can safely be inherited. PubMed was not used after a CAPTCHA appeared; no CAPTCHA was solved or bypassed.

## Evidence map

### 1. The population pattern is real, but the number depends on phenotype definition

The largest broad meta-analysis located in this survey reviewed five meta-analyses and estimated left-handedness at 10.6% overall, with estimates ranging from 9.3% under a stringent criterion to 18.1% when non-right-handedness was used as the criterion [E1]. The review found moderation by study year, the way handedness was measured and classified, sex, and ancestry. It also argued that cultural pressure against left-hand use can change the observed magnitude of left-handedness without providing a complete explanation for the approximately 1:10 population ratio [E1].

This is direct evidence for a robust population-level asymmetry, not evidence that all right-handers share a single mechanism. A study that classifies participants only by writing hand can confound biological preference with instruction, habit, and task convenience. A study that measures multiple tasks and motor performance can distinguish stable lateralization from task-specific practice.

### 2. Familial resemblance exists, but it is not a simple Mendelian trait

The Finnish nationwide twin study of 30,161 adults reported that genetic theories had received substantial attention, while maximum-likelihood models attributed most observed variance to unique environmental effects in that sample: 92-100% in childhood and 74-86% in adulthood, depending on model specification [E6]. The authors also showed that forced right-handedness changed estimates of familial effects. These results do not erase genetic influence; they show that estimates depend strongly on phenotype definition, model assumptions, and whether cultural correction of hand use is measured.

The twin meta-analysis of 59 studies found higher prevalence of left- and non-right-handedness among twins than singletons in raw analyses, but no meaningful monozygotic-versus-dizygotic prevalence difference. Handedness concordance was only modestly higher in monozygotic pairs, and the authors concluded that secular change and covariates make it unsafe to infer a genuine twinning effect [E5]. Thus, family designs support a distributed genetic contribution while also warning against treating heritability as individual destiny or as a direct estimate of biological mechanism.

The recent genetics review summarizes a more stable quantitative conclusion: handedness heritability is consistently estimated at approximately 25% [E8]. The appropriate synthesis is therefore polygenic and probabilistic: genes contribute to developmental liability, while non-genetic development and task environments contribute substantially to which hand is preferred or trained for a given task.

### 3. Large-scale genetics points to many loci and neurodevelopmental pathways

The 2020 genome-wide association meta-analysis combined UK Biobank, 23andMe, and International Handedness Consortium data for 1,766,671 participants. It identified 41 loci associated with left-handedness and 7 associated with ambidexterity at genome-wide significance [E2]. Tissue enrichment implicated the central nervous system, and highlighted pathways involving microtubule regulation and brain morphology. The genetic correlation between left-handedness and ambidexterity was low, suggesting partly different genetic mechanisms rather than one continuous axis.

The 2019 UK Biobank study related handedness to brain imaging phenotypes in approximately 9,000 people and performed GWAS in approximately 400,000 people. Four significant loci included or regulated genes implicated in brain development and patterning, including MAP2, MAPT, WNT3, and MICB [E3]. The same study reported altered connectivity between left and right language networks in left-handers and linked one locus to handedness, psychiatric phenotypes, Parkinson disease, and white-matter integrity. These findings are biologically informative but do not imply that handedness is caused by language or that a handedness-associated variant determines a person's manual preference.

The 2024 exome-wide analysis tested rare coding variants in 38,043 left-handed and 313,271 right-handed UK Biobank participants. Rare coding variants in TUBB4B were 2.7 times more frequent in left-handers, with additional evidence involving DSCAM and FOXP1; the estimated exome-wide heritability from rare coding variants was 0.91% [E4]. This strengthens the case for rare variant contributions and microtubule-related biology, while also quantifying their limited contribution. It does not justify a single-gene explanation for the common population majority.

### 4. Brain asymmetry is a plausible intermediate phenotype, not a complete answer

The imaging and genetics results converge on neurodevelopmental lateralization. The 2019 study implicated microtubule and patterning genes and reported a relation between handedness and language-network connectivity [E3]. A 2021 study of 31,864 individuals linked handedness and its genetic influences to structural asymmetries of the cerebral cortex [E9]. A 2021 polygenic-score study also examined the relation between handedness-associated genetic liability and structural brain asymmetry [E10].

The important inference boundary is that brain asymmetry should be treated as a candidate mediator or latent state, not as a one-to-one anatomical marker. A left-right difference in one cortical feature may be unrelated to hand preference, and a behavioral preference can be expressed without a large effect in a single region. A useful experiment must measure multiple motor and language-related asymmetries, estimate their reliability, and test whether they mediate genotype-to-behavior relations out of sample.

### 5. Development and culture can alter observed preference

Handedness is expressed through repeated interactions between a developing nervous system and task environments. The Finnish study explicitly measured forced right-handedness and found that it changed familial estimates [E6]. The prevalence meta-analysis identified cultural pressure against left-hand use as a moderator [E1]. The 2025 review emphasizes that tubulin genes participate in axon guidance, axon growth, and the inner structure of motile cilia, all processes that could influence the developmental formation of lateralized motor systems [E8].

These sources support a gene-development-environment model but do not identify a single environmental exposure with a large universal effect. The central unresolved issue is whether culture changes a latent motor bias, changes only the reported preference, or changes both in a task-specific way. A design that records both hand preference and performance across culturally sensitive and culturally neutral tasks can distinguish these possibilities.

### 6. Evolutionary stability is a population question

Right-hand preference has been reported across time and place, but the exact prevalence varies with measurement and cultural context [E1]. The persistence of a minority of left-handers suggests that selection, balancing processes, developmental noise, and frequency-dependent advantages may interact. The current evidence does not identify one evolutionary mechanism as established. It does support a testable population-level goal: explain how a distributed liability model can preserve a stable majority while allowing substantial individual variation.

## Research gap ledger

The survey accepts seven gaps for Idea-stage use:

1. **G1 - Phenotype separation:** There is no universally accepted operational definition that jointly separates preference, performance, writing hand, mixed use, and ambidexterity [E1, E5, E6].
2. **G2 - Polygenic mechanism:** Many common loci are associated with handedness, but the developmental chain from variants to lateralized motor circuitry remains unresolved [E2, E3, E8].
3. **G3 - Rare variant contribution:** Rare coding variants contribute measurably but explain only a small fraction of variance; the cell-level and developmental consequences of implicated variants remain to be established [E4].
4. **G4 - Brain mediation:** Structural and functional asymmetries correlate with handedness, but the stable, multiscale mediator linking genetic liability to behavior is not defined [E3, E9, E10].
5. **G5 - Gene-environment interaction:** Cultural pressure, training, and task affordances may alter reported preference and performance, but their interaction with developmental liability is poorly identified [E1, E6].
6. **G6 - Longitudinal directionality:** Cross-sectional adult data cannot determine when lateralization, preference, and performance diverge or converge during development [E3, E8].
7. **G7 - Majority stability:** No integrated model has been adequately tested for why right-hand preference remains the population majority while left, mixed, and non-right phenotypes persist across populations [E1, E7].

## Survey decision

The strongest downstream opportunity is not another association scan. It is a longitudinal, multimodal, family-aware model that predicts a carefully defined handedness phenotype from polygenic and rare variant liability, developmental brain asymmetry, and measured task environment. The design should explicitly test whether culture changes the latent motor phenotype or only the observable expression. It should also preserve the difference between explaining population prevalence and predicting any individual's hand preference.

## Evidence boundary

The registry supports the following direct claims: right-hand preference is the norm; left-handedness is approximately 10.6% under a broad best estimate; prevalence depends on measurement and participant characteristics; handedness is polygenic; microtubule and brain-development pathways are implicated; rare coding variants contribute a small measurable component; and brain asymmetry is associated with handedness. It does not support a universal right-hand gene, a single brain location, a deterministic genetic test, or a settled evolutionary explanation.
