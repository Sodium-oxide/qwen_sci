# Survey Agent: Fossil-Fuel-Free Energy Systems

## Scientific reframing

The question “Could we live in a fossil-fuel-free world?” is scientifically useful only after separating three claims:

1. whether an energy system can operate without unabated coal, oil, and natural gas;
2. whether all end uses can be electrified or supplied by non-fossil energy carriers while preserving hourly reliability, affordability, access, and material constraints; and
3. whether non-energy uses of fossil feedstocks, including chemicals and plastics, can also be replaced or supplied from non-fossil carbon.

The operational research question is:

> Under what combinations of renewable and other low-carbon generation, electrification, storage, transmission, demand flexibility, sustainable fuels, efficiency, and non-fossil feedstocks can a region eliminate fossil-fuel extraction and combustion while satisfying hourly reliability, energy-service, affordability, access, lifecycle-emissions, land, water, mineral, and governance constraints?

This reframing distinguishes **fossil-free** from **net-zero**. A net-zero system may still use fossil fuels with carbon capture or compensate residual emissions with removal. A fossil-free system is a stronger condition: fossil extraction and combustion are absent or limited to explicitly bounded emergency or non-energy uses, with the boundary reported rather than hidden in an emissions offset.

## What the evidence supports

The International Energy Agency’s *Net Zero by 2050* roadmap describes a pathway requiring a complete transformation of the global energy system, with major roles for renewables, electrification, efficiency, storage, hydrogen-based fuels, sustainable bioenergy, carbon capture, and behavioral change. The IEA page also emphasizes that the pathway is narrow, requires large investment and infrastructure coordination, and must preserve reliable and affordable energy services. These points support a systems experiment rather than a single-technology forecast.

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report Working Group III, Chapter 6, reports with high confidence that limiting warming requires rapid and deep energy-system emissions reductions, reduced fossil consumption, increased low- and zero-carbon supply, electrification, alternative carriers for hard-to-electrify uses, efficiency, system integration, and in some pathways carbon dioxide removal for residual emissions. It also identifies the technical and operational challenge of integrating high shares of variable renewables through storage, transmission, flexible generation, advanced controls, and demand response. This is direct support for testing reliability and resource constraints together.

The evidence does not justify a universal date at which fossil fuels will disappear. The transition depends on regional resource quality, demand growth, infrastructure age, financing, industrial processes, aviation and shipping, seasonal storage, critical materials, political coordination, and access to affordable energy. A technology that works at annual energy balance can fail at hourly or seasonal reliability; a low-carbon electricity supply can still leave steel, chemicals, shipping, aviation, heating, and feedstock uses dependent on fossil carbon.

## Core concepts and boundaries

The survey uses the following distinctions:

- **Energy service:** mobility, heat, cooling, lighting, industrial work, and digital or household services, rather than raw fuel volume.
- **Hourly adequacy:** ability to meet load and reserve requirements at each modeled time step under ordinary and stress conditions.
- **Seasonal adequacy:** ability to bridge prolonged low-renewable periods and seasonal demand peaks.
- **Fossil-free boundary:** no fossil extraction and combustion in the core scenario; separately report emergency reserves, non-energy feedstocks, and carbon capture cases.
- **Lifecycle accounting:** emissions and resource burdens include construction, mining, processing, transport, operation, replacement, recycling, and end of life.
- **Just transition constraint:** access, affordability, health, employment, and regional distribution are modeled as constraints or outcomes, not as afterthoughts.

## Candidate transition levers

1. **Efficiency and demand shaping.** Building retrofits, industrial efficiency, vehicle efficiency, compact scheduling, and demand response can reduce the generation and storage requirement. Their effect must be measured as delivered service, not assumed as a fixed percentage reduction.
2. **Direct electrification.** Heat pumps, electric vehicles, resistance or induction heating, and electric industrial processes can transfer energy demand to the power system. The analysis must include peak load, distribution upgrades, and seasonal electricity demand.
3. **Variable renewable generation.** Wind and solar are scalable sources but introduce weather-correlated variability. Geographic diversity, forecasting, transmission, overbuild, curtailment, and firming are part of the resource, not optional corrections.
4. **Firm low-carbon supply.** Hydropower, geothermal, nuclear, sustainable bioenergy, and other firm resources may reduce storage requirements, but their land, water, safety, waste, biomass, and social constraints need explicit accounting.
5. **Storage and flexibility.** Batteries, pumped storage, thermal storage, hydrogen, synthetic fuels, vehicle-to-grid, industrial load shifting, and demand response operate on different timescales. A portfolio should be tested against hourly, multi-day, and seasonal events.
6. **Non-fossil molecules and feedstocks.** Hydrogen, ammonia, sustainable biofuels, recycled carbon, biomass, and direct air capture may serve hard-to-electrify transport, industry, or chemicals. Their upstream energy, water, land, leakage, sustainability, and conversion losses can dominate system performance.
7. **Networks and institutions.** Transmission, distribution, interconnection, market rules, reliability standards, data, permitting, and cross-border coordination determine whether technical potential becomes usable capacity.

## Research gaps

The main gap is not the absence of candidate technologies; it is the absence of a common, stress-tested comparison of complete portfolios. Existing studies often optimize annual energy or cost while underrepresenting extreme weather, correlated outages, transmission bottlenecks, long-duration storage, industrial feedstocks, and household energy-service access. A second gap is boundary inconsistency: some scenarios call a system “clean” while retaining fossil feedstocks or fossil generation with offsets. A third gap is endogenous adoption. Infrastructure lead times, supply chains, financing, and public acceptance can determine the speed of change as strongly as device efficiency.

The study should therefore report at least four system states: fossil-dependent baseline; fossil-free electricity with fossil-dependent non-energy feedstocks; fossil-free energy services with bounded non-fossil molecules; and a stringent fossil-free materials case. This prevents a successful electricity transition from being mistaken for complete fossil independence.

## Proposed survey conclusion

A fossil-fuel-free world is technically plausible for many energy services, but it is not a single universal engineering configuration and cannot be inferred from the presence of cheap solar panels or electric vehicles alone. The strongest scientific question is conditional: which portfolios remain reliable, affordable, resource-feasible, and low-emission during the hardest hours and years, and which non-energy feedstock pathways close the remaining fossil boundary? The next agent should develop a stress-tested, multi-sector portfolio design around this question.
