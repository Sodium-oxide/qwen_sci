# Idea Agent: Fossil-Free-System Portfolio

## Selected direction

**FOSSIL-FREE-SYSTEM-PORTFOLIO**: evaluate whether a region can remove fossil extraction and combustion through a coordinated portfolio of efficiency, direct electrification, renewable generation, firm low-carbon supply, storage, transmission, demand flexibility, sustainable molecules, and non-fossil industrial feedstocks.

The key design choice is to optimize the complete energy-service system rather than compare isolated technologies. Fossil fuels are not used only to generate electricity. They also supply high-temperature heat, mobility, dispatchable energy, hydrogen, chemical carbon, plastics, asphalt, lubricants, and other materials. The paper should therefore report progressively stricter boundaries instead of treating a clean grid as equivalent to a fossil-free civilization.

## Central contribution

The proposed contribution is a **boundary-explicit, shock-aware transition portfolio model** coupled to a staged empirical validation plan. It combines:

1. hourly and seasonal adequacy;
2. sector coupling across buildings, transport, electricity, industry, and chemicals;
3. lifecycle emissions, minerals, water, land, and replacement requirements;
4. infrastructure and adoption lead times; and
5. affordability, access, and distributional reliability.

The model compares portfolios on a Pareto frontier rather than claiming that any single technology solves the transition. Its decisive metric is delivered energy service under stress, not annual primary-energy substitution.

## Candidate routes

### 1. Electrification-first portfolio

Use efficiency, heat pumps, electric vehicles, electric boilers, induction, and electric industrial processes wherever service quality permits. Use the resulting load profile to size generation, networks, storage, and reserves. Test the risk that electrification shifts rather than reduces peak demand.

### 2. Renewable-plus-flexibility portfolio

Combine wind and solar with geographic diversity, overbuild, transmission, batteries, thermal storage, pumped storage, demand response, and vehicle-to-grid. Test low-renewable weather regimes and transmission outages rather than relying on average capacity factors.

### 3. Firm-and-seasonal portfolio

Add hydropower, geothermal, nuclear, sustainable bioenergy, hydrogen, ammonia, or synthetic fuels where they reduce multi-day or seasonal adequacy risk. Explicitly price water, biomass, land, safety, waste, conversion losses, and upstream emissions.

### 4. Industrial molecule and feedstock portfolio

Use hydrogen-derived fuels, sustainable bio-based carbon, recycled carbon, and carbon capture or direct-air-derived carbon for aviation, shipping, steel, chemicals, and materials that cannot be directly electrified. Keep fossil-free and net-zero-with-CCS scenarios separate.

### 5. Demand-reduction and sufficiency portfolio

Model building retrofits, modal shift, load scheduling, material efficiency, reuse, and product redesign as service-preserving interventions. Test rebound, comfort, equity, and adoption instead of assuming an unconstrained demand reduction.

### 6. Coordinated transition portfolio

Combine the previous routes with transmission planning, distribution reinforcement, manufacturing capacity, permitting, financing, carbon standards, procurement, and targeted risk-sharing. This is the selected route because the survey indicates that technical availability alone cannot prove system feasibility.

## Falsifiable claims

1. At matched delivered energy-service reliability, coordinated portfolios with direct electrification and flexibility require less fossil backup than portfolios that add renewable generation without demand or network changes.
2. Annual net energy balance overstates feasibility when portfolios are exposed to correlated low-renewable weather, multi-day outages, or seasonal demand peaks.
3. A portfolio can satisfy a fossil-free electricity boundary while failing a full fossil-free boundary because aviation, shipping, industrial heat, chemicals, and feedstocks remain dependent on non-electric molecules or carbon.
4. Including lifecycle materials, replacement, and network constraints changes the ranking of generation and storage choices relative to an operational-only model.
5. Energy-service affordability and reliability are heterogeneous; a globally low-cost portfolio can still fail access or distributional constraints in particular regions.
6. The transition speed is bounded by manufacturing, transmission, permitting, and adoption lead times even when the long-run portfolio is technically feasible.
7. No universal portfolio dominates across climates, demand profiles, resource endowments, and institutional conditions.

## Required comparisons

The Author Agent should compare at least four states:

- **B0:** current fossil-dependent baseline;
- **B1:** fossil-free electricity but fossil-dependent non-energy feedstocks;
- **B2:** fossil-free energy services with bounded non-fossil molecules and feedstocks;
- **B3:** stringent fossil-free materials system including plastics, chemicals, and industrial carbon.

For each state, report the boundary, assumptions, energy-service output, loss-of-load probability, unserved energy, storage duration, transmission expansion, lifecycle emissions, mineral and water demand, land footprint, cost, affordability, and transition time. A scenario may pass one boundary and fail another; this is a result about scope, not an inconsistency.

## Expected scientific value

The study can answer a stronger question than “are renewables enough?” It can identify the binding constraint for a region and the portfolio change that relaxes it. If a portfolio fails only because seasonal storage is unavailable, the research agenda differs from a failure caused by transmission, critical minerals, fuel sustainability, or household affordability. The framework is therefore useful even if no region reaches the strictest fossil-free boundary immediately.
