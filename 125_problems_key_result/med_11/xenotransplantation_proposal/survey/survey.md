# Survey Agent Report: Can Xenotransplantation Solve the Shortage of Donor Organs?

## Scope and research question

This evidence map asks a deliberately narrower question than the headline suggests: **under what conditions could genetically modified pig organs become a safe, durable, scalable, and equitable supplement to human donation?** It does not treat a short-term functioning xenograft as proof that the organ shortage is solved. The focus is solid-organ pig-to-human xenotransplantation, with kidney evidence as the nearest human feasibility anchor and cardiac/nonhuman-primate evidence as supporting, organ-specific context.

This is a targeted evidence synthesis for a research proposal, not a systematic review, clinical guideline, or recommendation for patient care. No new animal, human, pathogen, genetic-engineering, or transplant procedure was performed.

## Evidence map

### 1. Biological feasibility has crossed an important but limited threshold

The clearest human feasibility evidence is the two-case study by Montgomery *et al.* [S1]. Genetically modified pig kidneys were placed in brain-dead human recipients maintained with physiological support. Across 54 hours, the grafts remained perfused and produced urine; serial biopsies did not show hyperacute or antibody-mediated rejection. This establishes a short observation-window feasibility signal in a highly controlled brain-dead-human model. It does **not** establish long-term survival, benefit in living recipients, routine clinical effectiveness, or supply-scale deployment.

Longer duration signals come principally from nonhuman primate experiments. Mohiuddin *et al.* [S2] report progressive donor-gene modifications associated with survival approaching nine months in a life-supporting pig-to-baboon cardiac setting. Such results strengthen the claim that durable function is technically plausible, but they cannot be directly converted into a human durability estimate because organ type, recipient species, immune context, perioperative management, and endpoint differ.

### 2. Rejection, inflammation, coagulation, and physiology remain coupled barriers

Xenograft injury is not one obstacle. The literature identifies preformed antibody/complement activity, antibody-mediated and cellular rejection, innate inflammatory activation, and cross-species physiological incompatibility as coupled mechanisms [S3, S4]. Coagulation dysfunction is particularly important because endothelial injury, inflammation, immunity, and mismatched regulation of hemostasis may amplify one another rather than occur as separable failure modes [S3].

Genetic modification of donor pigs is a promising strategy for lowering antigenicity and improving complement/coagulation compatibility [S3]. The correct inference is not that genetic modification has removed rejection. Rather, it creates a testable design space in which organ-specific combinations of donor characteristics, recipient biology, and safety monitoring must still be evaluated.

### 3. Infectious-risk management is a deployment requirement, not an appendix

Fishman and Mueller [S5] emphasize that clinical xenotransplantation requires sustained attention to donor-derived and recipient infectious risks. Source-animal microbiological control and long-term surveillance reduce risk but do not make uncertainty disappear. WHO consultation guidance likewise frames donor-source quality, infection control, recipient follow-up, and oversight as conditions for clinical translation [S6]. A program that achieves good graft function but cannot sustain credible infection governance should not be counted as deployment-ready.

### 4. Organ quantity is not the same as deployable organ supply

The shortage question is a systems question. A theoretical number of source pigs only becomes clinically relevant when each graft is durable enough, is accompanied by quality assurance and traceability, meets regulatory and ethical requirements, is supported by trained centers and follow-up infrastructure, and is accessible beyond a small number of exceptional sites. Contemporary reviews consistently describe immune injury, infection, physiological incompatibility, donor production, governance, and patient selection as interdependent translational conditions [S3, S6, S7].

Consequently, xenotransplantation may plausibly **relieve** shortage in defined indications before it can be said to **solve** shortage generally. A credible answer requires a mechanism-to-access bridge: biological readiness must be joined to manufacturing, surveillance, regulatory, and equity readiness.

## Evidence classification and claim limits

| Claim | Evidence | Permitted interpretation | Prohibited interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-term pig-kidney function in brain-dead humans | [S1], direct human but 54 h | Short-term human feasibility | Long-term living-recipient efficacy or routine availability |
| Extended pig-heart survival in baboons | [S2], preclinical and organ-specific | Durability is technically plausible | Human long-term outcome |
| Genetic strategies target immune/coagulation barriers | [S3], mechanistic review | Rationale for multi-domain assessment | Barriers are eliminated |
| Infection control needs durable oversight | [S5], [S6] | Governance belongs in readiness assessment | Infection risk is zero or solved |
| Pig organs may become an additional source | [S3], [S7] | Conditional potential to supplement donation | Guaranteed replacement of human donation |

## Sub-hypothesis coverage

**H1 — Functional feasibility:** genetically modified pig grafts can show measurable short-term function in human-relevant settings. **Coverage:** direct but short-term human evidence [S1]; supportive preclinical evidence [S2].

**H2 — Durable biological control:** clinically useful duration requires an integrated solution to immune injury, coagulation, inflammation, physiology, and infection. **Coverage:** strong mechanistic and review support [S3--S5], but human long-duration evidence remains sparse.

**H3 — Deployable supply:** a positive biological result becomes shortage relief only if manufacturing, quality, surveillance, regulation, and health-system capacity are simultaneously viable. **Coverage:** conceptually supported by translational and regulatory sources [S5--S7]; no accepted cross-domain quantitative readiness method was identified.

**H4 — Just deployment:** benefit cannot be evaluated solely by graft count; access, long-term follow-up burden, and governance must be represented explicitly. **Coverage:** recognized by regulatory/clinical translation literature [S5--S7]; operational metrics are underdeveloped.

## Accepted research gaps

1. **G1: Cross-domain readiness gap.** No common, auditable framework was identified that combines graft durability, immune/coagulation injury, microbiological safety, manufacturing quality, regulatory feasibility, and equity/access without collapsing their distinct uncertainties.
2. **G2: Translation-to-supply gap.** The literature frequently establishes mechanistic progress or a single graft outcome, but lacks a transparent bridge from such outcomes to the number of safely deployable grafts that can actually reduce waiting-list pressure.
3. **G3: Governance-as-threshold gap.** Infection surveillance, source-animal quality, regulatory oversight, and long-term follow-up are often discussed separately from biological performance, even though each can prevent routine deployment.
4. **G4: Model-transfer gap.** Evidence from cells, nonhuman primates, brain-dead humans, and living recipients has different external validity. The field lacks a uniform way to expose rather than conceal these transfer boundaries.

## Survey conclusion and handoff

The evidence supports an ambitious but conditional conclusion: xenotransplantation has moved beyond a purely speculative alternative and may become a meaningful, indication-limited supplement to human donation. It has not yet demonstrated that it can independently solve donor-organ shortage. The next research step should therefore not be another unconstrained claim of feasibility. It should create a transparent framework that determines when a proposed organ-and-indication pathway is biologically promising, conditionally trial-ready, or still systemically non-deployable.

The Idea Agent receives the accepted gaps, evidence-level caveats, and the instruction that any selected direction must distinguish *preclinical promise*, *short-term human feasibility*, and *safe scalable deployment*.
