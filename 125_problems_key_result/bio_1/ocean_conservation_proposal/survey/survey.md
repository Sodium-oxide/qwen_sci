# Survey Agent Report: What Could Help Conservation of the Oceans?

## Scientific reframing and scope

The original question is valuable but too broad to test. This Survey Agent reframes it as:

> **How can an equity-constrained, climate-robust, land-sea intervention portfolio reduce nutrient and plastic pressure while protecting coastal ecosystem function and livelihood safeguards in a specified coastal seascape?**

The target is not one universal intervention and not a claim of observed conservation success. It is a decision problem: select and sequence a portfolio of land-based nutrient actions, plastic-leakage reduction, coastal-habitat/blue-carbon protection or restoration, spatial fisheries protection, monitoring, and governance measures under climate uncertainty. The initial proposal is computational and evidence-integrative; it does not prescribe field actions at a particular site.

## Evidence map

### 1. Coastal conservation is a coupled land-sea and climate problem

Coastal eutrophication illustrates why disconnected actions fail. Paerl [S1] argues that nitrogen and phosphorus reductions should be considered jointly across the freshwater-marine continuum rather than assuming a single upstream nutrient control will solve downstream coastal impacts. Malone and Newton [S2] synthesize how nutrient enrichment interacts with overfishing, development, and climate-driven pressures and emphasize the challenge of managing point and diffuse sources. Reusch *et al.* [S3] use the Baltic Sea as a multistressor example in which watershed-sea integration and cross-border governance are needed, while warming can offset management gains. These sources support a portfolio logic; they do not identify one fixed intervention mix for every coastal system.

### 2. Pollution control and ecosystem protection require distinct but connected levers

Plastic leakage is a material ecosystem-services and policy problem, with circular-economy, waste-management, behavior, and governance levers rather than a purely marine clean-up problem [S4]. Nutrient loading, plastic leakage, habitat degradation, and fishing pressure have different drivers, timescales, measurement systems, and distributional consequences. A useful decision model must retain this difference while representing their interaction at the coastal ecosystem level.

Blue-carbon ecosystems can contribute climate-mitigation and adaptation co-benefits, but their valuation and governance conditions matter [S5]. The user-requested browser check reached the full Frontiers record for [S5], verifying its title, venue, DOI, abstract framing, and its emphasis on conservation/restoration, socio-economic considerations, valuation, governance, and global coordination. This supports treating habitat protection/restoration as a conditional portfolio component, not as a substitute for emissions reductions or pollution control.

### 3. Area-based protection works through governance and capacity, not designation alone

Marine protected areas (MPAs) are important ecosystem-based tools, yet their role depends on integration with fisheries management, spatial planning, institutions, and stakeholder participation [S6]. Community perceptions of access, livelihood effects, and governance can shape support for MPAs [S7]. Gill *et al.* [S8] show that capacity shortfalls hinder global MPA performance. Therefore an MPA polygon or restoration map is not itself a conservation outcome. A portfolio model should include implementation capacity, legitimacy, monitoring, and distributional safeguards as explicit variables.

### 4. Equity, observation, and knowledge governance are core conditions

Marine conservation is a social-ecological process. Conservation social science improves the legitimacy, salience, robustness, and effectiveness of interventions by addressing human dimensions [S9]. Blasiak *et al.* [S10] identify inequitable distribution of capacity in an ocean-knowledge context, while Levin *et al.* [S11] highlight needs for integrated observing, data standards, accessibility, and cross-sector collaboration. These records support an explicit equity and observability layer: a portfolio cannot be called robust if its effectiveness depends on data, finance, enforcement, or participation capacities that are unavailable to affected communities or institutions.

## Evidence boundaries

| Claim | Evidence role | Permitted interpretation | Prohibited interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dual nutrient reduction matters across freshwater-marine continuum | [S1], [S2] | Nutrient actions should be represented as coupled land-sea levers | A fixed N/P recipe works in every basin |
| Plastic leakage needs cross-sector action | [S4] | Waste, circularity, policy, and community factors belong in portfolio design | A marine clean-up alone solves plastic pollution |
| Blue-carbon measures may deliver co-benefits | [S5] | Habitat protection/restoration is a conditional climate-biodiversity component | Blue carbon substitutes for emissions mitigation or local consent |
| MPAs are governance-dependent tools | [S6]--[S8] | Area protection must be modeled with capacity, participation, and fisheries context | Drawing an MPA boundary guarantees ecological or social benefits |
| Equity and observing capacity affect outcomes | [S9]--[S11] | Data access, participation, and distributional effects are decision variables | A global score can erase local rights or uncertainties |

## Sub-hypothesis coverage

**H1 -- Multi-pressure coupling:** A portfolio that independently tracks nutrients, plastic leakage, habitat condition, fishing pressure, and climate exposure is more informative than a single-pressure conservation score. Supported conceptually by [S1]--[S5].

**H2 -- Non-substitutability of governance:** A biologically attractive intervention cannot be considered implementation-ready if monitoring, enforcement, legitimacy, or capacity are unsupported. Supported by [S6]--[S11].

**H3 -- Equity-constrained performance:** A portfolio can look efficient on an aggregate ecological objective while producing unacceptable livelihood or participation burdens. Supported by [S7], [S9], [S10].

**H4 -- Climate robustness:** Portfolio ranking should be assessed over climate and uncertainty scenarios, not only under historical mean conditions. Supported by [S2], [S3], [S5].

## Accepted research gaps

1. **G1 -- Cross-pressure portfolio gap:** Conservation evidence is often organized by intervention type; a practical framework is needed to evaluate nutrient, plastic, habitat, fisheries, and observing actions as connected but non-identical levers.
2. **G2 -- Land-to-sea decision gap:** Watershed pressures and coastal outcomes are connected, but their evidence, institutions, and planning boundaries are often disconnected.
3. **G3 -- Capacity and legitimacy gap:** Management capacity, monitoring, participation, and implementation feasibility are frequently treated as secondary to ecological optimization.
4. **G4 -- Equity and distribution gap:** Aggregate gains can conceal who bears restrictions, monitoring burdens, or opportunity costs.
5. **G5 -- Climate-robustness gap:** Static intervention rankings are vulnerable to changing temperature, rainfall/runoff, habitat exposure, and socioeconomic conditions.

## Survey conclusion and handoff

The evidence supports a strong but bounded conclusion: ocean conservation is helped by integrated, place-sensitive portfolios rather than a single universal solution. The next research contribution should not announce the best global policy. It should build an auditable framework that identifies interventions that remain attractive across climate scenarios, refuses portfolios that fail equity or capacity gates, and records the evidence uncertainty behind every recommendation.

The Idea Agent receives gaps `G1--G5`, the above claim limits, and a rule that no downstream output may state that any proposed intervention has already improved an ecosystem or livelihood.
