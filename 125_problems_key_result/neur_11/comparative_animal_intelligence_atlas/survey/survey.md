# Survey: How Smart Are Nonhuman Animals?

## Scientific reframing

The prompt lists tool use, communication, camouflage, memory, and problem solving in crows, dolphins, octopuses, and chimpanzees. These observations are scientifically valuable, but they do not justify a single ranking of animal intelligence. A better question is:

> **Which cognitive capacities are shared or independently evolved across nonhuman animals, how do ecological and social demands shape those capacities, and how much of apparent intelligence transfers across tasks when testing is species-appropriate?**

The survey separates cognition from performance. A failure can reflect motivation, sensory access, morphology, training history, neophobia, or task mismatch rather than absence of a cognitive process. Conversely, a successful trained behavior does not automatically demonstrate a human-like concept. The relevant unit is a capacity under a defined ecological and experimental context.

## Evidence map

### 1. Tool manufacture and physical problem solving in corvids

Hunt and Gray documented the crafting of hooked-twig tools by wild New Caledonian crows [1]. The observed sequence included selecting raw material, trimming it, and sculpting a hook. This is stronger evidence than simple object use because the animal transforms material through multiple ordered manipulations. It supports flexible sensorimotor problem solving under ecological selection.

The result does not by itself show human-like causal theory or unlimited innovation. Corvid performance can reflect a species-specialized physical ecology, developmental experience, social transmission, or a combination. Rook studies of cooperative and physical problem solving also show why social and physical cognition should be measured separately [2], rather than compressed into a single score.

### 2. Chimpanzee memory and symbolic behavior

Matsuzawa reviewed comparisons in which three young chimpanzees outperformed adult humans on briefly presented Arabic numerals in a working-memory task, while performing less well on several other tasks, including imitation, cross-modal matching, and one-to-one correspondence [3]. This is a particularly useful result because it simultaneously demonstrates a striking domain-specific advantage and a limit to broad human-like abstraction.

The correct conclusion is not that chimpanzees are generally smarter than humans or that humans are generally smarter than chimpanzees. The result shows that task demands, training, perceptual strategy, and species history can reverse the performance ordering. A comparative design must preserve those reversals as data rather than averaging them away.

Claims about chimpanzee sign language and symbol combinations also require a distinction between trained symbol use, spontaneous communication, referential signaling, compositional structure, and generative language. Each is a different target variable.

### 3. Cetacean cognition and communication

Marino and colleagues reviewed behavioral, anatomical, and evolutionary evidence for complex cognition in cetaceans [4]. The review links large cetacean brains and sophisticated social lives to cognitive capacities, while also emphasizing that brain size is not a direct readout of intelligence. Dolphins show impressive acoustic perception, imitation, memory, and social learning in appropriate tasks, but the existence of complex communication is not equivalent to a human language system.

Cetacean cognition also demonstrates the danger of removing behavior from ecology. Echolocation, underwater social organization, long-distance acoustic signaling, and marine sensory constraints create a cognitive niche unlike that of terrestrial primates or birds. A fair comparison must ask whether an animal can flexibly solve problems in its own sensory and motor world, not whether it can reproduce a human laboratory routine.

### 4. Octopus tool use and distributed control

Finn, Tregenza, and Norman reported defensive tool use in a coconut-carrying octopus [5]. The animal transported coconut shell pieces and used them as shelter or armor. This is evidence of object transport and context-dependent use in a cephalopod with a highly divergent nervous system. It makes octopuses especially informative for testing convergent solutions to behavioral flexibility.

The observation is not evidence that octopuses have primate-like planning, language, or self-concepts. It shows that complex problem solving can evolve in a nervous system with a different body plan and different neural organization. Octopus research should therefore include body-environment interaction, tactile exploration, camouflage, escape, and flexible object manipulation rather than relying exclusively on visual primate tasks.

### 5. Social cognition and cooperation

Emery and colleagues argued that social bonding in birds can select for cognitive adaptations [6]. Seed, Clayton, and Emery tested cooperative problem solving in rooks and explicitly compared pressures often associated with primate social intelligence [2]. These studies support a broad evolutionary principle: social complexity can favor memory for individuals, prediction of partners, inhibition, coordination, and learning, but the exact profile depends on ecology.

This principle is not a universal social-intelligence score. A species may excel at individual recognition and still show limited cooperation, or cooperate effectively without representing a partner in a human-like way. The survey therefore treats social learning, coordination, and social prediction as related but separable domains.

## Cross-species interpretation

The evidence supports a **cognitive mosaic** view. Nonhuman animals show strong, sometimes extraordinary abilities in particular domains: corvid material transformation, chimpanzee rapid numerical working memory, cetacean acoustic-social cognition, and octopus flexible object use. These abilities may arise through convergent evolution, lineage-specific specialization, developmental learning, and body-environment coupling. There is no evidence that one scalar ranking captures all of them without losing the scientific structure.

Three comparisons are useful:

1. **Domain-general flexibility:** reversal learning, transfer, innovation, inhibition, and adaptation to changed rules.
2. **Domain-specific expertise:** tool manufacture, echolocation, camouflage, spatial memory, numerical memory, or tactile manipulation.
3. **Social learning and coordination:** copying, teaching-like interactions, cooperation, communication reference, and partner prediction.

A species-appropriate battery should contain both common computational cores and ecological modules. The common cores enable comparison of latent processes such as flexibility and memory. The ecological modules prevent a human-centric test from defining intelligence as “being good at our task.”

## Research gaps

The central gap is the absence of a validated cross-species framework that separates general cognitive flexibility from specialized expertise while controlling for sensory and motor affordances. Existing demonstrations are often compelling but heterogeneous: wild observations provide ecological validity but limited experimental control; laboratory tasks provide control but may reward training history or human-designed strategies.

Additional gaps include: whether high performance transfers from one task family to another; how social learning changes tool or problem-solving performance; how much observed intelligence reflects long childhood or juvenile learning; whether neural architecture predicts cognitive flexibility across radically different body plans; and how to quantify welfare-preserving, species-appropriate testing.

## Survey conclusion and handoff

The Survey recommends the **Ecological Cognitive Architecture Atlas (ECAA)**. ECAA predicts that a shared but incomplete latent flexibility factor will appear across species, while domain-specific factors will explain large and meaningful residual differences. It further predicts that ecological fit, social learning, and sensorimotor affordances will moderate performance. The design must therefore compare latent structure, transfer, learning curves, and species-specific expertise rather than produce a leaderboard.
