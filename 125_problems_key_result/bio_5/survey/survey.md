# Survey - Will It Be Possible to Cure All Cancers?

## Scientific restatement

Cancer is not one disease and ``cure all cancers'' is not a single endpoint. A testable question is whether a defined therapeutic strategy can provide durable, patient-relevant control or eradication for a declared set of cancers and patient states while meeting toxicity, resistance, and long-term follow-up requirements. Any cross-cancer claim must separately address tumor genomic diversity, antigen heterogeneity, metastasis, immune evasion, microenvironmental suppression, normal-tissue expression, and clonal evolution.

## Evidence map

Cancer cells can grow uncontrollably, invade and metastasize, evade or co-opt immunity, accumulate genomic changes, and use altered nutrient programs. Surgery, radiotherapy, cytotoxic therapy, molecularly targeted therapy, endocrine treatment, and immunotherapies can be highly consequential for particular diseases and stages, but their benefit and risk boundaries differ. Immunotherapy demonstrates that immune mechanisms can create durable responses in some settings; it does not establish a universal cure.

Immune recognition is limited by target availability, antigen presentation, T-cell state, suppressive stromal and myeloid programs, cytokine context, trafficking, tumor burden, and adaptive escape. A broad target hypothesis requires evidence in diverse tumor and normal tissues, across molecular subtypes, and under selective pressure. A laboratory screen that identifies tumor killing in cell culture is mechanism evidence, not patient efficacy, normal-tissue safety, persistence, or prevention of relapse.

The MR1-restricted T-cell-receptor work described in the prompt is interpreted here as a promising discovery-stage hypothesis: altered tumor metabolism may create an immune-recognition opportunity. It does not prove that one receptor can eliminate every cancer, that MR1 recognition is tumor-specific in humans, or that an engineered cell product is safe. Such a claim needs explicit testing for tumor coverage, normal-tissue effects, on-target/off-tumor risk, antigen loss, persistence, cytokine toxicity, and clinical durability.

## Source boundary

The report uses NCI cancer biology information, cancer-hallmark literature, immune-checkpoint and engineered-cell therapy reviews, and primary MR1-related evidence as sources. It makes no patient-level treatment claim, trial result, tumor-response estimate, safety result, or cure claim.
