# Survey Agent Output: Is De-Extinction Possible?

## Research framing

The literal question, *Is de-extinction possible?*, is too coarse for scientific decision-making. It conflates at least four different activities: preserving living cells and cloning an extant taxon; selective back-breeding; editing an extant relative to express selected extinct-lineage traits; and releasing a population intended to restore an ecological function. These activities have different evidentiary, biological, ethical, and legal requirements. The survey therefore uses the following operational question:

> Under what genomic, developmental, welfare, ecological, and governance conditions could an extinct-species proxy be scientifically feasible and conservation-defensible; and under what conditions should a proposal be rejected or held in abeyance?

An **extinct-species proxy** is defined here as a living population derived from an extant lineage and intentionally selected or modified to express a limited, evidence-supported set of traits associated with an extinct lineage. It is not a recovered historical individual and should not automatically be treated as the same biological, legal, or cultural entity as the extinct species. This distinction follows the IUCN framing of ``proxies of extinct species for conservation benefit'' rather than a claim of literal resurrection [S6].

## Evidence corpus and reliability boundary

The evidence corpus contains peer-reviewed work on ancient-DNA preservation, mammoth genomics, conservation genetics, rewilding, and de-extinction governance, plus the IUCN guiding principles. Candidate papers were discovered through OpenAlex and AnySearch; DOI, title, venue, and bibliographic metadata were cross-matched when both services returned a record. Key DOI landing pages and the IUCN web site were additionally opened in the requested in-app browser. Some publisher pages did not expose machine-readable citation tags to the browser session, so the report records that limitation rather than treating page scraping as additional scientific evidence.

The corpus supports four firm boundaries.

1. **Ancient DNA is damaged, fragmented, and preservation-dependent.** Allentoft *et al.* measured exponential decline of DNA fragments in dated bone, with substantial sample-to-sample variation associated with taphonomy and diagenesis [S1]. Kistler *et al.* similarly emphasize environmental and tissue effects in paleogenomic datasets [S2]. Thus, age alone is not a sufficient predictor, but time and preservation jointly restrict what can be inferred.
2. **Paleogenomes can illuminate extinct lineages without recreating their complete developmental state.** Million-year-scale mammoth DNA has resolved parts of evolutionary history [S3], while population-genomic work reveals bottlenecks and genetic erosion before extinction [S4], [S5]. Sequence recovery does not provide an embryo, an epigenetic state, maternal environment, learned behavior, microbiome, or a validated genotype-to-phenotype map.
3. **Functional proxy claims require population and ecological reasoning.** Steeves *et al.* argue that a proxy population intended for conservation benefit needs evolutionary potential beyond the first generations [S9]. Rewilding literature likewise treats ecological outcomes as contingent on food webs, landscapes, climate, and human activity rather than as a property of a genome alone [S10].
4. **Governance is a scientific constraint, not a communications appendix.** IUCN recommends a conservation-benefit framing for creating extinct-species proxies [S6]. Legal and ethical scholarship highlights uncertainty, moral hazard, stewardship duties, and the mismatch between new organisms and existing regulatory categories [S8], [S11]. Genomic work on culturally significant taxa demonstrates that data stewardship and engagement with Indigenous or local rights-holders require substantive participation [S12].

The evidence does **not** establish that an extinct animal has been recreated, that genomic similarity is a sufficient proxy-fidelity measure, or that any specific proxy would provide a net ecological benefit. No such claim is made in this survey.

## Scientific synthesis

### A. The molecular limit is not a single DNA ``expiry date''

Ancient DNA survives as a damaged molecular record. Depurination, fragmentation, and cytosine deamination alter the available sequence molecules [S1], [S2]. Good preservation can extend the informative window and exceptional contexts can retain very old environmental DNA [S3], but this is not equivalent to an intact nuclear genome in a viable cell. Environmental DNA may identify taxa or genomic fragments in an ancient ecosystem while remaining unsuitable for reconstructing a complete individual genome. This matters directly for public claims about dinosaurs: non-avian dinosaurs are separated from the present by tens of millions of years, far beyond a plausible route to a recoverable cellular genome. Fossils can inform morphology and evolutionary history; they do not provide a realistic DNA route to a dinosaur clone or genomic re-creation.

The relevant technical question is therefore not, ``Can DNA be found?'' It is, ``Which sequence states can be reconstructed with what uncertainty, and which candidate traits remain supportable after that uncertainty is propagated?'' A variant call supported by multiple authentic molecules and comparative alignment is different from an imputed ancestral state; both differ from a speculative trait assignment. Any readiness assessment must keep these categories separate.

### B. Comparative genomes are necessary but insufficient

Mammoth and elephant comparative genomics demonstrates that extinct-lineage sequence can be informative. Yet the biological object resulting from changes to an extant relative would begin with the extant relative's cellular, developmental, mitochondrial, maternal, and social context. It would not retrospectively restore a mammoth individual. This remains true even if a long list of extinct-associated alleles were accurately inferred.

The gap between genotype and organism is especially important for polygenic, regulatory, developmental, behavioral, and ecological traits. A sequence difference can be confidently observed while its phenotype, pleiotropic effects, developmental timing, or environmental interaction remains unknown. Population-genetic evidence also cautions against treating a handful of reference genomes as a species-level template. Woolly mammoth studies document changing demographic history and genetic load across time [S4], [S5]. A single target genome cannot represent the diversity, population structure, or adaptive potential of a lost lineage.

### C. Conservation benefit is a counterfactual claim

The strongest conservation rationale is not spectacle or taxonomic naming; it is a claim that a candidate population can supply a lost ecological function with net benefit relative to feasible alternatives. This is counterfactual. A proposed proxy must be compared not only with doing nothing, but also with habitat protection, protection of extant relatives, translocation, invasive-species control, assisted gene flow, and other uses of the same resources. Functional resemblance is not automatically ecological benefit: a species can alter vegetation, disease dynamics, trophic interactions, and human-wildlife conflict in site-specific ways. The rewilding evidence base calls for hypothesis testing and monitoring because outcomes depend on the ecological context [S10].

For a defensible candidate, ecological evidence must state: (i) the lost function; (ii) the causal pathway by which the proxy may restore it; (iii) local environmental preconditions; (iv) competing explanations; (v) possible harms; (vi) a comparator conservation portfolio; and (vii) reversal or abstention criteria. A historical range map or a general claim about megafauna does not satisfy this requirement.

### D. Welfare and governance are non-compensatory constraints

Technical possibility cannot ethically compensate for avoidable welfare risk in donor lineages, developmental systems, surrogates, or offspring. Nor can a predicted ecological benefit compensate for the absence of lawful, legitimate, and rights-respecting governance. These constraints should be explicit gates rather than low-weight terms in an attractiveness score. A proposal with uncertain genomic evidence may remain at the research stage; a proposal with unacceptable welfare, governance, or opportunity-cost conditions should be rejected rather than ``averaged'' into conditional approval.

## Accepted research gaps

The survey accepts seven actionable gaps. Each is phrased so that a downstream project can test a claim rather than simply restating uncertainty.

| Gap ID | Accepted gap | Consequence for research design |
|---|---|---|
| G1 | ``De-extinction'' is routinely used for biologically distinct interventions. | Require a taxonomy of claim types and prohibit unsupported identity language. |
| G2 | Ancient-DNA uncertainty is rarely propagated from molecular evidence to proposed traits. | Build evidence cards separating observed, imputed, and unknown variants and traits. |
| G3 | Genomic similarity is not a validated measure of developmental, behavioral, or ecological fidelity. | Test proxy-fidelity claims against independent phenotype and ecology evidence rather than sequence similarity alone. |
| G4 | Feasibility arguments often omit welfare, developmental, and multigenerational risk. | Make welfare/development a non-compensatory gate with human review. |
| G5 | Ecological-benefit claims are often not counterfactual or site-specific. | Use scenario models, comparator portfolios, and adverse-outcome analysis. |
| G6 | Governance, rights-holder participation, and opportunity costs are too often narrative afterthoughts. | Treat legitimacy, lawful authority, data stewardship, and conservation displacement as gates. |
| G7 | No falsifiable, auditable readiness benchmark consistently separates impossible, premature, and conditionally reviewable proposals. | Develop and stress-test a decision framework with negative controls and abstention categories. |

## Survey conclusion and handoff

The present state of evidence supports a clear conclusion: **literal recovery of an extinct historical organism is not a general capability, and it is not a scientifically defensible description of edited extant-relative populations.** Some recent extinct taxa may support increasingly informative genomic reconstructions, but this only establishes part of the evidence needed for a proxy proposal. A scientifically serious program must be able to reject candidates with good genomic data but poor developmental, welfare, ecological, or governance readiness.

The recommended downstream direction is an evidence-gated, design-only decision framework called **ProxyGate**. Its purpose is not to authorize animal creation or environmental release. Its purpose is to make the research question testable: can a candidate be shown to survive all required evidence gates under uncertainty, or should the correct result be **REJECT**, **ABSTAIN**, or **REQUIRES EVIDENCE**?
