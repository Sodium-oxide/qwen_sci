# Survey: How Migratory Animals Determine Where to Go

## Scientific reframing

The operational question is not whether a migrating animal possesses one universal navigation sense. It is: **which sensory cues, internal computations, and learned or inherited information allow a defined species and life stage to estimate direction, geographic position, route progress, and seasonal timing under stated environmental conditions?** This reframing separates a directional compass from a position-sensitive map and from the separate control problem of deciding when to depart, stop, molt, feed, or reproduce.

Migration occurs across multiple spatial scales. An animal leaving a familiar refuge may only need local landmark guidance. A nocturnal songbird crossing unfamiliar regions must maintain a seasonally appropriate bearing, detect displacement from a target region, compensate for wind, and select safe stopover habitat. Marine animals additionally face moving water masses, attenuated visual signals, and depth-dependent sensory conditions. Consequently, a strikingly accurate track cannot by itself identify the cue that produced it.

## Evidence landscape

Mouritsen's 2018 Nature review argues that long-distance navigation is intrinsically multiscale and multisensory: no single cue gives pinpoint geographic accuracy across thousands of kilometres. This provides the central boundary for the study. Visual landmarks, coastlines, odours, wind and wave conditions, solar and stellar patterns, polarized light, and the geomagnetic field may all be informative, but their availability and reliability vary by species, location, time of day, weather, and developmental history.

Celestial navigation offers a clear example of a computation rather than a static sensor. The solar azimuth changes during the day, so a sun compass must be time-compensated by a circadian system. Many insects and birds can use polarized skylight near sunrise or sunset to calibrate this compass. Nocturnal migrants can also learn stellar rotation patterns. These results support a directional-cue role; they do not by themselves prove that a given animal carries a geographic map.

Magnetoreception is especially important because the geomagnetic field provides both directional and spatial gradients. In birds, behavioural work has long supported a light-dependent inclination compass. Xu *et al.* (Nature, 2021) showed that cryptochrome 4 from the migratory European robin has magnetic-field-sensitive photochemistry in vitro, more strongly than CRY4 from two non-migratory species. This is meaningful molecular evidence for a radical-pair candidate, but it remains distinct from evidence that the protein mediates a complete animal-level navigation calculation in the wild.

Three broad mechanistic families should not be conflated. First, a radical-pair mechanism can convert magnetic-field-dependent spin dynamics in photoexcited molecules, plausibly cryptochromes, into a light-dependent retinal signal. Second, biogenic magnetic particles have been proposed as a magnetite-based receptor mechanism, but localization, specificity, and causal behavioural evidence must each be established. Third, electromagnetic induction can generate electric fields as conductive animals move through water in a magnetic field; it is particularly relevant to aquatic taxa with electroreceptive systems. These mechanisms may differ across lineages and may support different functions, such as compass orientation versus map-like position estimation.

## Gaps that prevent strong claims

Four recurring gaps motivate a stricter research design. (1) A cue-conflict result can show that an animal changes bearing but not whether it has lost its map, timing program, or motivation. (2) A molecular magnetic effect can be real without being the receptor that drives a behavioural response. (3) A laboratory arena may remove wind, social context, visual panorama, predation risk, and route experience, making transfer to free migration uncertain. (4) Averaging bearings can hide subgroups that use different cues, switch cues with latitude, or become disoriented only under particular light spectra or magnetic conditions.

The literature therefore supports conditional claims: animals can use multiple cues; magnetic information is demonstrably relevant in several taxa; and radical-pair photochemistry is a credible mechanistic candidate in birds. It does not support a universal receptor, a single navigation algorithm, or the conclusion that a protein measurement explains migration across taxa.

## Design implications

A useful experiment must predefine the species, developmental stage, migratory phase, route segment, sensory environment, and outcome. It should compare natural and controlled magnetic conditions, rotate or virtually displace only the cue under test, retain cross-cue conditions, and include a restoration control. Field tracking is necessary to establish ecological consequence; controlled experiments are necessary to establish cue dependence. Both are required before a claim can move from orientation observation to an indication-specific navigation mechanism.

The proposed framework, MAGNAV, treats migration as a layered inference problem: cue availability, cue weighting, directional decision, map correction, route behaviour, and reproductive or survival consequence. Its purpose is to make mechanistic claims falsifiable and comparable across species without assuming that the same sensory pathway operates everywhere.
